Your Medicare Supplement premium went up again. Maybe a little, maybe a lot. Either way, nobody warned you it would keep happening, and most of what you read online treats rate increases as a minor footnote instead of one of the biggest financial risks in Medicare planning. Let's fix that.

## Why Medigap Rates Rise (And Why the Explanation You've Heard Is Incomplete)

The standard answer is: "Insurers raise rates due to medical inflation." True, but incomplete to the point of being misleading.

The real driver is something called **block aging**, and it's worth understanding in plain terms. When an insurer sells a Medigap plan, that pool of policyholders tends to age together. As the group gets older and sicker over time, claims go up. The insurer responds by raising rates. New, healthier people don't join that pool because new applicants go into newer policy blocks. So the original pool gets progressively sicker and more expensive, and the rate spiral accelerates. This is why a Plan G you bought at 65 can have dramatically higher premiums at 75 than a Plan G someone buys fresh at 75 today.

Medical trend is real too, around 5-8% annually in recent years, but that's table stakes. Block aging is what separates a manageable 5% increase from a jaw-dropping 15-20% one.

There's also the insurer's loss ratio to consider. States require that insurance companies spend a certain percentage of premiums on actual claims (typically 65% for individual Medigap policies). If an insurer has been underpricing to gain market share, regulators may actually allow a steeper correction later. Some of the most aggressively priced plans in 2015-2018 came back to bite policyholders hard in subsequent years.

I've seen this pattern repeat with enough regularity that I'd call it predictable. The plan that looks like the best deal at enrollment often isn't the best deal at year seven.

Three ways insurers price Medigap plans exist, and this is where most coverage completely glosses over what matters.

**Community-rated** plans charge everyone the same premium regardless of age. Your neighbor who enrolled at 65 pays the same as you at 78. Increases still happen (medical trend applies to everyone), but there's no built-in acceleration just because you're getting older. Wisconsin, Massachusetts, and Minnesota standardize Medigap differently and largely use community rating.

**Issue-age-rated** plans lock in a rate based on how old you were when you first enrolled. No additional age factor piles on top each year. You still see increases, but they're applied to a stable base.

**Attained-age-rated** plans are the most common, and the riskiest for long-term budgeting. Your premium climbs with every birthday on top of whatever medical trend increase the insurer applies. When you're 70 it's manageable. By 78 or 80, you can be paying 40-50% more than you would on a community-rated plan that started at the same price.

Here's my contrarian take on this: most people pick plans primarily based on the current monthly premium, and that instinct is backwards. The rating method is a better predictor of your five-year cost than today's rate. A community-rated plan priced $30 higher per month at enrollment can easily save you thousands over a decade. Run the math before you assume the cheaper option is cheaper.

## What "Filed and Approved" Really Means for Your Wallet

Medigap rates aren't arbitrary. Every state requires insurers to file rate changes with the state insurance commissioner, and most require approval before the increase takes effect. This sounds reassuring. In practice, it's not as protective as it sounds.

State oversight varies wildly. Some states scrutinize rate filings aggressively. Others approve them almost automatically. Even in strict states, regulators are balancing the needs of the whole market: if they deny too many rate increases, insurers exit and leave policyholders with fewer choices. So there's real pressure to allow increases that are actuarially justified.

You'll typically get 30-60 days written notice before a rate increase takes effect. That notice comes by mail, often buried in language that doesn't scream "your premium is going up 12%." Read it. If you toss anything from your Medigap insurer unopened, stop doing that.

The [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) publishes data on Medicare Supplement plan enrollment and financials, though tracking specific historical rate increases by insurer requires digging into state insurance department filings. Your state's insurance commissioner website is where the actual filed rates live. It's not the most intuitive research, but it's public information.

## The Shopping Window Problem

Here's the thing most people don't know until it's too late: you don't have an annual Open Enrollment for Medigap the way you do for Medicare Advantage or Part D drug plans. Miss your Medigap Open Enrollment Period (the six months starting when you're 65 and enrolled in Part B), and switching plans later requires medical underwriting in most states.

That means if your current insurer raises rates aggressively but you've developed diabetes, high blood pressure, or had a cardiac event since you enrolled, you may not be able to get a better rate elsewhere without being denied or charged more. You're stuck.

This asymmetry matters enormously. Younger, healthier enrollees have more leverage. An aggressive rate increase at 68 is a different situation than the same increase at 76 with a few diagnoses on your chart.

A few states do offer guaranteed issue rights for Medigap outside the normal window (Missouri, New York, Connecticut, Massachusetts, and Maine have various protections worth checking). And certain life events trigger guaranteed issue rights federally, like losing employer coverage or a plan leaving your service area. But for most people in most states, the window is narrow and the stakes of missing it are high.

The [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free, unbiased counseling in every state on exactly these questions. If you're weighing a switch and aren't sure whether you'd qualify for coverage elsewhere, talk to a SHIP counselor before you do anything else. They'll know your state's specific rules.

## What To Do When the Rate Increase Letter Arrives

Don't panic and don't ignore it. Do this instead:

Start by comparing current rates for your plan letter (G, N, F, etc.) from other insurers in your area. The same Plan G has identical benefits regardless of which company sells it. You're comparing only price, financial stability, and rate increase history. Use Medicare.gov's plan comparison tool or call a licensed independent broker who represents multiple carriers.

Ask the broker (or research yourself) about the insurer's rate increase history in your state. Some insurers are known for stable, predictable increases. Others have a pattern of aggressive underpricing followed by steep corrections. This history is publicly available through state insurance filings, though it takes some digging. An experienced broker can shortcut this for you.

Consider whether switching makes sense given your health status. If you're healthy and under 72, switching is often worth exploring. If you have significant health issues and live in a state without guaranteed issue protections, be careful. Losing your current plan to get a lower rate is a bad trade if you can't get back in later.

One more thing: don't switch based solely on a $15/month savings. Transaction costs matter. You'll need to qualify, set up new billing, and there's always some administrative friction. The calculus changes at $50-100/month savings, especially if the new insurer has a better rate-stability track record.

## Plan N and the Tradeoff Worth Knowing

If Plan G rates are starting to feel painful, Plan N is worth a serious look. It's not a downgrade from Plan G. It's a different tradeoff.

Plan N covers the same hospital costs as Plan G but leaves you with a $20 copay for most doctor visits and a $50 copay for ER visits that don't result in admission. You also remain responsible for any Part B excess charges (the difference between what Medicare approves and what a non-participating provider charges). For people who see their primary doctor two or three times a year and use participating providers, the out-of-pocket exposure is often modest while the premium savings can be $30-60 per month or more.

The catch: if your plan G rate is rising because your insurer is having block-aging problems, Plan N from the same insurer has the same problem. Switching plan letters within the same company addresses the cost, not the underlying actuarial pressure. The insurer matters as much as the plan letter.
