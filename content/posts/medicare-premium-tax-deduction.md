Medicare premiums can absolutely be deducted from your federal taxes, but whether that deduction actually saves you money depends on a few specific conditions most people overlook. The short version: you can deduct Medicare premiums as a medical expense, but only if you itemize and only the amount exceeding 7.5% of your adjusted gross income (AGI) counts. If you're self-employed, the rules are even friendlier, and you may be able to skip the threshold entirely.

That 7.5% floor is where most people get tripped up. It's not a dollar-for-dollar deduction. It's a threshold you have to clear first. If your AGI is $60,000, only medical expenses above $4,500 (7.5% of $60,000) are actually deductible. For someone with modest medical costs and a standard deduction that already beats their itemized total, the Medicare deduction may be worth exactly zero. That's not pessimism; it's math worth running before you assume you're saving money.

## Who Actually Benefits

Let's be honest about who this deduction is worth pursuing. For most retirees with standard income and typical medical bills, the standard deduction is simply higher than anything they'd get from itemizing. In 2026, the standard deduction for a single filer 65 or older is roughly $16,550, and for a married couple both over 65 it's higher still. (Always confirm the current figure at IRS.gov before filing.)

If you have significant medical expenses beyond premiums, things like dental work, [hearing aids](/does-medicare-cover-hearing-aids/), long-term care insurance premiums, or prescription costs, the picture changes. Those stack on top of your Medicare premiums and may push you past both the 7.5% floor and the standard deduction. That's the scenario where itemizing pays off.

Self-employed people are in a genuinely different position. If you're running a business and paying Medicare premiums, you can deduct them as an adjustment to income on Schedule 1 of Form 1040, Line 17. No itemizing. No 7.5% hurdle. The only catch: you can't deduct more than your net self-employment income, and you can't take this deduction for any month you were eligible to enroll in an employer-sponsored health plan (through your spouse's job, for example).

## What Premiums Qualify

More premiums qualify than people realize. Here's what the IRS includes:

- Medicare Part A premiums (most people get Part A free, but those who buy in pay $311 or $565 per month in 2026 and can deduct that)
- Medicare Part B premiums ($202.90/month at the standard rate in 2026)
- Medicare Part D (prescription drug plan) premiums
- Medicare Advantage (Part C) plan premiums
- Medigap (Medicare Supplement) premiums
- IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount) surcharges, which are higher premiums charged to people above certain income thresholds

That IRMAA point catches people off guard. If your 2024 income was above $109,000 (single) or $218,000 (joint), you're paying more than $202.90 for Part B in 2026, and all of it counts. Use the [IRMAA Medicare Calc](/irmaa-medicare-calculator/) if you want to see exactly which tier applies to your situation.

What doesn't qualify: late enrollment penalties added to your Part B or [Part D premiums](/medicare-part-d-premium-2026/). Those aren't considered premiums by the IRS; they're penalties.

## The Math in Practice

**Example:** Consider a hypothetical married couple, both retired, with combined AGI of $80,000 in 2026. Each pays the standard Part B premium of $202.90/month, and they each have a Medigap Plan G policy costing $180/month. They also paid $2,400 in dental expenses during the year.

Their total qualified medical expenses:

| Expense | Annual Amount |
|---|---|
| Part B premiums (2 people x $202.90 x 12) | $4,869.60 |
| Medigap premiums (2 people x $180 x 12) | $4,320.00 |
| Dental expenses | $2,400.00 |
| **Total** | **$11,589.60** |

Their 7.5% AGI floor: $80,000 x 7.5% = $6,000.

Deductible amount: $11,589.60 minus $6,000 = **$5,589.60**

Whether itemizing beats the standard deduction depends on their other itemized deductions (mortgage interest, state taxes, charitable contributions). If those plus $5,589.60 exceed the standard deduction, itemizing makes sense. If not, it doesn't.

This isn't a small calculation to eyeball. A tax professional or a good tax software package can run this comparison quickly, and it's worth doing every year because income, premiums, and medical costs shift.

## Self-Employed: A Cleaner Path

If you're self-employed and paying Medicare premiums, the deduction sits on the front end of your return as an adjustment to income. That's meaningful because it reduces your AGI, which in turn can affect other income-based calculations: your IRMAA tier, your [Medicare Savings Program](/medicare-savings-programs/) eligibility, even the taxation of your Social Security benefits.

The form to use is Schedule 1, Form 1040. The IRS Publication 535 (Business Expenses) covers the self-employed health insurance deduction in detail. The logic: Medicare premiums are treated as health insurance premiums for the self-employed.

One scenario worth flagging: if you became eligible for Medicare mid-year while still running a business, you can only deduct premiums for the months you were actually enrolled. It's prorated. Don't claim January through December if you enrolled in July.
