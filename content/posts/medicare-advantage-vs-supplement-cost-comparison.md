Imagine you're sitting at your kitchen table with two folders in front of you. One is from a Medicare Advantage plan with a $0 premium and a colorful brochure about dental and vision benefits. The other is a Medigap (Medicare Supplement) application with a monthly premium that made you catch your breath. Your neighbor swears by her Advantage plan. Your brother-in-law says supplements are the only way to go. And you have no idea who's right. I've sat at that table with hundreds of clients over the years, and here's what I can tell you: both of them are probably right for their own situations. The real question is which one is right for *yours*.

## What You're Actually Comparing

Before you can compare costs, you need to understand what you're actually buying with each option.

Original Medicare (Parts A and B) covers a lot, but it leaves real gaps. There's no cap on what you can owe out of pocket in a given year. Hospital stays, specialist visits, and procedures can stack up fast. That's the problem both of these products solve, just in very different ways.

A **Medicare Advantage plan** (Part C) replaces your Original Medicare coverage entirely. You still use Medicare's rules, but a private insurance company manages your benefits. Most plans bundle in Part D prescription drug coverage, and many add extras like dental, vision, and hearing. In exchange, you typically work within a network of doctors, get referrals to see specialists, and share costs through copays and coinsurance each time you use care.

A **Medigap plan** (also called Medicare Supplement insurance) works differently. You keep Original Medicare as your primary coverage, and the supplement policy pays some or all of the costs Medicare leaves behind, like your Part B coinsurance (usually 20% of approved costs) or the Part A hospital deductible. You pay a monthly premium for this coverage, and in return, your exposure to surprise bills shrinks dramatically. Most Medigap plans let you see any doctor or specialist in the country who accepts Medicare. No network restrictions. No referrals.

Neither one is the "cheap" option or the "good" option. They're genuinely different structures, and the right one depends on your health, your finances, and how you want to use your coverage.

Here's what most people don't realize when they see a $0-premium Medicare Advantage plan: the premium is just one piece of the cost equation.

With Medicare Advantage, your upfront monthly cost is often low, sometimes nothing at all beyond your Part B premium (which you pay regardless). But every time you use the plan, cost-sharing kicks in. A specialist visit might run $40-$75. An outpatient surgery could mean 20% coinsurance. An inpatient hospital stay often involves a copay per day for the first several days. These costs feel manageable when you're healthy. When you're managing a serious illness, a hospitalization, or multiple specialists, they pile up.

That's why the number that really matters is the plan's **annual out-of-pocket maximum**, which is the ceiling on what you can spend on covered services in a year. Federal rules cap this figure, but plans can set it anywhere below that cap. I've seen plans with maximums in the range of $4,000-$9,000 or more. If you hit that ceiling during a bad health year, you'll feel the difference.

Medigap works the opposite way. You pay more every month, guaranteed, whether you use healthcare or not. The trade-off is that once you've paid your premium, your exposure to per-use costs is largely eliminated, depending on which plan letter you choose. Medigap Plan G, for example, covers almost everything Medicare doesn't, after you pay the Part B deductible once per year. Plan N covers quite a bit but leaves you with modest copays for office visits and emergency room trips.

If you're in good health and rarely use medical care, a Medigap plan can feel like paying for insurance you're not using. But that's exactly how insurance is supposed to work. The question is whether the peace of mind and financial protection are worth the premium to you.

## A Side-by-Side Look: Common Cost Scenarios

Let's be concrete about this. The table below isn't meant to show you exact numbers, because those vary wildly by location, age, plan, and health status. What it shows is the *structure* of costs in different situations.

| Situation | Medicare Advantage | Medigap + Part D |
|---|---|---|
| Healthy year, minimal care | Low total cost (low or $0 premium, few copays) | Higher total cost (monthly premium with little use) |
| Moderate use (a few specialist visits, one procedure) | Moderate cost (copays add up but stay manageable) | Low-to-moderate cost (premium paid, minimal extra costs) |
| Major illness, surgery, or hospitalization | Potentially high cost, up to the out-of-pocket max | Generally low extra cost beyond the monthly premium |
| Ongoing chronic conditions, frequent specialist care | Costs vary, network restrictions may matter a lot | Predictable monthly cost, broad provider access |
| Travel or use of out-of-network providers | May be limited or not covered outside network | Covered anywhere Medicare is accepted nationwide |

The pattern is clear. Medicare Advantage tends to look better when you're healthy. Medigap tends to look better when you're sick or when you need frequent care.

## The Hidden Factors That Change the Math

Cost comparison isn't just arithmetic. A few factors can tilt the scales that most online calculators won't tell you about.

**Where you live matters enormously.** Medigap premiums are set by age and location. In some parts of the country, premiums are relatively affordable. In others, they're steep enough that the monthly cost becomes a real hardship, especially on a fixed income. Meanwhile, Medicare Advantage plan availability and quality vary dramatically by county. Urban areas often have more competitive plans with richer benefits. Rural areas may have very limited Advantage options, or none at all.

**Your ability to get Medigap coverage can change over time.** This is the part that catches people off guard. When you first enroll in Medicare Part B, you have a six-month Open Enrollment window during which insurance companies cannot deny you a Medigap policy or charge you more due to health conditions. After that window closes, most states allow companies to use **medical underwriting**, meaning they can reject you or charge more based on your health history. I've seen clients try to switch from Medicare Advantage to a Medigap plan after a cancer diagnosis, only to be turned down by every carrier. That window at the start of Medicare is genuinely precious. Don't assume you can always switch later.

**Prescription drug coverage is a separate moving part.** Most Medicare Advantage plans include drug coverage. With Medigap, you need to buy a standalone Part D drug plan. Depending on your medications, this can affect your total cost significantly in either direction.

**Extra benefits can be genuinely valuable or mostly irrelevant.** Many Medicare Advantage plans offer dental, vision, hearing, and even gym memberships. If you need dental work, that can represent real savings. But read carefully. These benefits often come with annual maximums, network requirements, and coverage limits that make them less generous than they first appear.

## How to Actually Compare Your Options

The comparison doesn't have to be overwhelming. Here's a practical way to approach it.

**Step 1: Pull your last year of medical bills.** Look at what you actually spent on doctors, specialists, labs, hospital care, and prescriptions. This is your baseline for how much healthcare you use.

**Step 2: Get Medigap premium quotes for your age and zip code.** Compare Plan G and Plan N since those are the most popular among new Medicare enrollees today. You can shop these through insurance brokers, or get a sense of the landscape at Medicare.gov's plan comparison tool.

**Step 3: Look up Medicare Advantage plans in your area.** Use Medicare.gov's plan finder to see what's available, compare star ratings (Medicare rates plans from 1-5 stars for quality), check the out-of-pocket maximums, and verify that your doctors are in-network.

**Step 4: Add up your realistic annual costs for each scenario.** For Advantage, that means premium times 12, plus estimated copays based on your typical use, plus a stress-test of what happens if you hit the out-of-pocket max. For Medigap, that means premium times 12, plus the Part D premium and estimated drug costs, plus any costs your specific plan doesn't cover.

**Step 5: Talk to someone who doesn't sell insurance.** The [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) provides free, unbiased Medicare counseling in every state. These counselors don't earn commissions, so they have no incentive to steer you toward one option or another. [AARP's Medicare resource center](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/) also offers solid educational tools if you want to do more research before that conversation.

## What About Switching Later?

People often ask whether they can change their minds. The answer is: sometimes, with important caveats.

Medicare Advantage enrollees can switch plans or go back to Original Medicare during the **Annual Enrollment Period** (October 15 through December 7 each year), effective January 1. There's also a Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period from January 1 through March 31, during which you can make one change.

Switching from Medicare Advantage back to Medigap is where things get complicated, as I mentioned above. In most states, you'll face medical underwriting, and if you've developed health conditions, you may not qualify for coverage you would have had during your initial enrollment window. A handful of states, including New York, Massachusetts, and Connecticut, have more consumer-friendly rules that allow year-round Medigap enrollment without underwriting. If you live in one of those states, you have more flexibility. Most people don't.

The practical upshot: if you're leaning toward Medigap, strongly consider enrolling during your initial Medicare enrollment period rather than trying Advantage first and switching later.
