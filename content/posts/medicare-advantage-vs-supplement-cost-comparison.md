You're sitting at your kitchen table with two folders. One has a $0-premium Medicare Advantage plan and a brochure promising dental and vision. The other is a Medigap application with a premium that made you wince. Your neighbor loves her Advantage plan. Your brother-in-law swears by supplements. You have no idea who's right.

I've worked through this decision with hundreds of people, and here's what actually matters: both of them work brilliantly for the right person. The question isn't which one is objectively better. It's which one fits your situation.

## What You're Actually Comparing

Before comparing costs, you need to know what you're buying.

Original Medicare (Parts A and B) covers plenty, but leaves real gaps. There's no annual cap on what you can owe out of pocket. Hospital stays. Specialist visits. Procedures. They stack up fast. Both Medicare Advantage and Medigap solve that problem, just completely differently.

**Medicare Advantage** (Part C) replaces your Original Medicare coverage entirely. A private insurance company manages your benefits under Medicare's rules. Most plans throw in Part D drug coverage and extras like dental, vision, and hearing. The catch? You work within a network, need referrals for specialists, and pay copays or coinsurance every time you use care.

**Medigap** (Medicare Supplement insurance) keeps Original Medicare as your main coverage. The supplement policy then covers costs Medicare leaves behind, like that 20% coinsurance on most services or the Part A hospital deductible. You pay a monthly premium. In return, surprise bills basically disappear. You can see any doctor or specialist nationwide who accepts Medicare, with no referrals or network hassles.

Neither is cheap or good. They're structurally different. Which one works depends on your health, your money, and how you want to access care.

## The Real Cost Picture: Premiums Are Only the Beginning

Here's what catches most people: that $0-premium Medicare Advantage plan isn't actually free.

The monthly premium might be low or nonexistent (beyond your Part B premium, which you're paying anyway). But every time you use the plan, you pay. A specialist visit runs $40 to $75. Outpatient surgery means 20% coinsurance. Hospital stays have daily copays for the first several days. When you're healthy, these feel manageable. During a serious illness, hospitalization, or when you're bouncing between multiple specialists, they pile up fast.

The number that actually matters is the **[annual out-of-pocket maximum](/medicare-advantage-out-of-pocket-maximum/)**, the ceiling on what you'll spend on covered services in a year. I've seen plans with maximums ranging from $4,000 to $9,000 or higher. Hit that ceiling in a bad health year, and you'll notice.

Medigap flips this around. You pay more every month, whether you use healthcare or not. The payoff? Once you've paid the premium, per-use costs mostly vanish, depending on which plan letter you choose. Medigap Plan G covers almost everything Medicare doesn't (after you hit the Part B deductible once yearly). Plan N covers quite a bit but leaves modest copays for office visits and ER trips.

Healthy and rarely seeing doctors? A Medigap plan feels expensive for insurance you're not using. But that's how insurance works. The real question is whether the predictability and protection are worth the monthly cost to you.

## The Hidden Factors That Change the Math

This isn't just arithmetic. A few factors can shift things dramatically, and most online calculators won't flag them.

**Geography matters enormously.** Medigap premiums depend on age and location. Some parts of the country have reasonable premiums. Others are steep enough to hurt on a fixed income. Medicare Advantage options vary just as wildly. Urban areas often have competitive plans with solid benefits. Rural areas might have almost nothing available.

**Your ability to buy Medigap can change after your first year.** This catches people off guard. When you first enroll in Medicare Part B, you get a six-month window where insurance companies can't deny you a Medigap policy or charge more based on your health. After that window closes, most states allow **medical underwriting**, meaning carriers can reject you or charge higher premiums based on your medical history. I've seen someone try to switch from Medicare Advantage to Medigap after a cancer diagnosis and get turned down by every insurer. That initial enrollment window is irreplaceable. Don't assume you can always switch later.

**Prescription drug coverage moves independently.** Medicare Advantage plans usually include it. With Medigap, you need a standalone Part D plan. Depending on your medications, this can shift your total cost significantly either way.

**Extra benefits can deliver real value or feel like window dressing.** Many Advantage plans offer dental, vision, hearing, and gym memberships. Dental work savings can be substantial. But read the fine print. These benefits often have annual caps, network limits, and coverage restrictions that make them less generous than advertised.

## How to Actually Compare Your Options

This doesn't need to be complicated. Here's a practical approach.

**Step 1: Pull your medical bills from the last year.** What did you actually spend on doctors, specialists, labs, hospital care, and prescriptions? This is your baseline.

**Step 2: Get Medigap premium quotes for your age and zip code.** Shop Plan G and Plan N, the most popular among new Medicare enrollees now. Try insurance brokers or Medicare.gov's comparison tool.

**Step 3: Look up available Medicare Advantage plans.** Use Medicare.gov's plan finder. Check star ratings, out-of-pocket maximums, and whether your doctors are in-network.

**Step 4: Calculate realistic annual costs.** For Advantage: premium times 12, plus estimated copays based on your actual use, plus what happens if you hit the out-of-pocket max. For Medigap: premium times 12, plus Part D costs, plus anything your plan doesn't cover.

**Step 5: Talk to someone unaffiliated with insurance sales.** The [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free, unbiased Medicare counseling in every state. These counselors earn zero commissions. [AARP's Medicare resource center](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/) has solid educational tools if you want more research before that conversation.

## What About Switching Later?

Can you change your mind? Sometimes, with serious caveats.

Medicare Advantage enrollees can switch plans or return to Original Medicare during the **Annual Enrollment Period** (October 15 through December 7 each year), starting January 1. There's also a Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period from January 1 to March 31, where you can make one change.

Switching from Medicare Advantage back to Medigap gets sticky. In most states, you'll face medical underwriting, and any health conditions you've developed might disqualify you or lead to higher premiums. A handful of states, including New York, Massachusetts, and Connecticut, have more consumer-friendly rules that allow year-round Medigap enrollment without underwriting. If you live there, you have flexibility. Most people don't.

Bottom line: if Medigap appeals to you, enroll during your initial Medicare period rather than trying Advantage first and switching later.
