Most people pick the plan with the lower monthly premium and call it a day. That's the single most expensive mistake you can make in Medicare.

I've sat across from hundreds of seniors over the years who chose a $0-premium Medicare Advantage plan, got hit with a serious illness or surgery, and ended up paying thousands more out-of-pocket than they ever expected. And I've seen the opposite too: people paying $200-plus a month for a Medigap (Medicare Supplement Insurance) plan they barely use, convinced they're being smart, when a Medicare Advantage plan would've cost them a fraction of the total. The honest truth is that neither plan type is categorically "better." The total cost story is messier than any insurance company's brochure will tell you.

So let me walk through what I actually found when I dug into the numbers and the fine print.

## The Basic Structure, Because It Matters More Than People Think

Medicare Advantage (MA, sometimes called Part C) and Medicare Supplement Insurance (also called Medigap) are fundamentally different animals, even though they both fill gaps in Original Medicare.

Original Medicare, which is Parts A and B, covers a lot but leaves you exposed to real financial risk. Part A has a hospital deductible of $1,676 per benefit period in 2025, not per year, per benefit period. Part B has a 20% coinsurance with no cap. No cap. If you have a $500,000 cancer treatment course, you're theoretically on the hook for $100,000. That's the gap both plan types are trying to address.

Medicare Advantage does it by replacing Original Medicare entirely. You're still using Medicare dollars, but a private insurer manages your care, usually through a network. These plans often have $0 premiums, and many include dental, vision, and hearing benefits that Medigap doesn't touch. The tradeoff is cost-sharing at the point of care: copays, coinsurance, deductibles, and an annual out-of-pocket maximum (MOOP). In 2025, [the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) sets the MOOP ceiling for MA plans at $9,350 for in-network care, though many plans set their own lower limits.

Medigap works differently. You keep Original Medicare, pay the Part B premium ($185/month in 2025 for most enrollees), and then buy a standardized supplemental policy from a private insurer. Plan G, currently the most popular option for new enrollees, covers nearly everything after you pay the Part B deductible ($257 in 2025). Your cost-sharing after that is close to zero.

That standardization is important. A Plan G from Mutual of Omaha and a Plan G from AARP/UnitedHealthcare cover exactly the same benefits. The only real variable is the premium.

Here's where I'll be honest: comparing total annual costs between these two approaches is genuinely hard, and anyone who gives you a clean, confident answer is probably oversimplifying.

The Medigap math is more predictable. Take Plan G: in many markets, you're looking at premiums ranging from about $100 to $250 per month depending on your age, location, and the insurer, plus the $185 Part B premium, plus the $257 annual deductible. Do the rough math: if you're paying $150/month for Plan G, your floor for the year is around $4,000 (premiums plus deductible) before you've used a single benefit. But once you hit that deductible, your additional medical costs are effectively zero for covered services. If you have a hip replacement, a cardiac event, dialysis, anything serious, your total cost doesn't move much.

Medicare Advantage math starts lower but has a wider range of outcomes. A $0-premium MA plan sounds like free insurance, but you're still paying the $185 Part B premium. Add in typical primary care copays of $0-$10, specialist visits at $30-$50 each, and a hospital stay that might cost $350-$500 per day for the first several days, and a moderately active year can easily run $1,500-$3,000 in out-of-pocket costs even without a major illness. A serious health event could push you to the MOOP of $5,000-$9,350 depending on your plan.

What surprised me when I actually modeled this out is how close the numbers get for people with moderate health needs. Someone who's generally healthy but sees a few specialists, takes a handful of prescriptions, and has one minor procedure in a year might spend roughly the same total under either approach. The divergence happens at the extremes: very healthy people lean toward MA savings, seriously ill people often lean toward Medigap protection.

## The Variables Nobody Puts in Their Calculator

A few things that most comparison articles skip over.

**Drug costs.** Medigap doesn't cover prescriptions. At all. You'll need a standalone Part D (prescription drug) plan on top of it, which adds roughly $15-$100/month depending on your drugs and coverage tier. Many Medicare Advantage plans bundle Part D coverage in, which is convenient but can create complications if your preferred pharmacy isn't in-network or your drugs land on a high tier.

**Geography.** This one is huge and underappreciated. In rural areas, MA plans often have thinner networks and fewer options. I've talked to people in rural Kentucky and western Nebraska who technically had access to MA plans but found their local doctors weren't in-network. Medigap with Original Medicare gives you access to any provider in the country who accepts Medicare, which is something like 93% of non-pediatric physicians, according to data from [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/). If you travel, have a winter home in Florida, or simply want maximum flexibility, that universality has real dollar value.

**Premium increases over time.** MA plan premiums and benefits can change every single year. I've watched plans add cost-sharing, shrink networks, or eliminate dental benefits at the annual plan renewal. Medigap premiums also increase, typically tied to your age and inflation, but the core benefit structure is stable because it's standardized by federal law.

**The underwriting problem.** In most states, if you enroll in Medigap during your Initial Enrollment Period (the 6-month window when you first get Part B), insurers can't deny you coverage or charge you more based on health. Wait too long, switch from MA to Medigap later, and in most states you can be medically underwritten and potentially denied or charged more. This creates a real lock-in effect. If you go MA and later develop cancer, diabetes, or heart disease, switching to Medigap might not be an option. That asymmetry is something I wish more people thought about before they pick based on premium alone.

## A Rough Framework for Thinking About This

I'm hesitant to give you a blanket recommendation because the research here is genuinely mixed and individual circumstances drive so much of the outcome. But I can share a rough framework I've used for years.

People who tend to do better with Medicare Advantage: those in excellent health with limited chronic conditions, people on tight fixed incomes who can't afford $150-$250/month in Medigap premiums, people who value the extra benefits (dental and vision especially), and people who live in metro areas with robust MA plan networks.

People who tend to do better with Medigap: those with existing chronic conditions or family history of serious illness, people who travel frequently or split time between states, anyone who values predictability over lower average costs, and people who can comfortably afford the higher monthly outlay.

There's a third option worth knowing: some people pair a high-deductible Plan G (premiums can be as low as $30-$50/month in some markets) with a Health Savings Account if they're still working, which dramatically lowers the Medigap premium while maintaining catastrophic protection. It's not widely advertised but worth asking about.

If you want to compare actual plan costs in your area, the Medicare Plan Finder at Medicare.gov is the place to start. It's not perfect, but it's the most comprehensive free tool available.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Medicare Advantage really free if the premium is $0?

No. You'll still pay the standard Medicare Part B premium ($185/month in 2025), plus copays and coinsurance each time you use care. A $0 premium just means the private insurer isn't charging you beyond what Medicare already does. Your total out-of-pocket costs for the year can still run several thousand dollars depending on how much care you use.

### Can I switch from Medicare Advantage to Medigap whenever I want?

In most states, no. Outside of your Initial Enrollment Period, Medigap insurers can medically underwrite you (except in a handful of states like New York, Connecticut, and Massachusetts that have guaranteed issue rules year-round). That means if you develop health problems while on an MA plan, you might not be able to get Medigap coverage, or you could be charged significantly more. This is one of the strongest arguments for choosing carefully upfront.

### Which plan type is better for someone with a serious chronic condition?

Generally speaking, Medigap tends to offer more financial predictability for people with ongoing serious health needs. High utilization of specialists, hospitalizations, and frequent procedures can push MA out-of-pocket costs toward the annual maximum, while a Plan G or Plan N holder mostly pays only their regular premium after meeting the annual deductible. That said, this depends heavily on specific plan terms, drug needs, and providers, so it's worth running the numbers for your situation.

### Does Medicare Advantage cover care when I travel out of state?

Most MA plans cover emergency and urgent care anywhere in the country, but routine care typically must stay in-network. For people who spend part of the year in another state or who travel frequently, this can be a real constraint. Medigap with Original Medicare covers you anywhere in the U.S. that accepts Medicare, with no network restrictions.

### What's the difference between Plan G and Plan N for Medigap?

Both are popular standardized Medigap plans, but Plan N has a lower premium in exchange for copays of up to $20 for office visits and up to $50 for emergency room visits (waived if you're admitted). Plan G covers those copays with no additional cost-sharing after the Part B deductible. If you rarely see doctors, Plan N's lower premium might save you money over time. If you're a frequent health system user, Plan G's predictability often wins.

The real cost of Medicare coverage isn't what you pay every month. It's what you pay in a bad year, with a bad diagnosis, when you're not in a position to fight with an insurance company about network coverage. That's the number worth obsessing over, and it looks very different depending on which path you choose.
