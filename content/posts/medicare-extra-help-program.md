## What Is Extra Help, and Why Does It Exist?

Extra Help is a federal program run by the Social Security Administration (SSA) that helps people with Medicare pay for their prescription drug costs. The official name is the Low Income Subsidy (LIS), and you'll see it called both names depending on who you're talking to. They mean the same thing.

Here's the short version of how Medicare drug coverage works: Medicare Part D is the prescription drug benefit. It comes with monthly premiums, annual deductibles, and copays each time you fill a prescription. Without Extra Help, those costs can pile up fast, especially if you take multiple medications or any specialty drugs. Extra Help was created specifically to make sure that lower-income Medicare beneficiaries aren't forced to choose between groceries and prescriptions.

What most people don't realize is how much this program actually covers. Qualifying beneficiaries can see their Part D costs reduced dramatically, including the monthly premium, the annual deductible, and the copays at the pharmacy. Depending on your income and assets, you may pay as little as a few dollars per prescription or even nothing at all for certain generic drugs. The federal government caps these costs each year, and for 2024, copays for Extra Help recipients are capped at $4.50 for generic drugs and $11.20 for brand-name drugs (these figures are updated annually, so always verify current amounts at [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/)).

This is where a lot of people assume they won't qualify and stop reading. Please don't. The income and asset limits are more generous than most people expect, and the program was expanded in 2024 to bring in even more people.

The SSA looks at two things: your income and your resources (assets).

For income, the general guideline is that you must earn below 150% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL). For a single person in 2024, that works out to roughly $21,900 per year. For a married couple, it's higher. These thresholds shift a bit each year, so treat these as ballpark figures and check ssa.gov or [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) for the current numbers.

For resources, the SSA looks at things like bank accounts, stocks, and bonds. Your home, one car, personal belongings, and life insurance are generally not counted. For 2024, the resource limit is roughly $16,600 for an individual and $33,240 for a married couple.

One important development: in 2024, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) eliminated what used to be called the "partial" Extra Help level. Now there are two tiers: full Extra Help and partial Extra Help. People who previously got partial benefits may now qualify for full benefits. If you received Extra Help in previous years and were told you got a reduced amount, it's worth checking your eligibility again.

A few groups get automatic enrollment in Extra Help. If you're enrolled in Medicaid (the state-federal program for low-income individuals), receive Supplemental Security Income (SSI), or are in a Medicare Savings Program (MSP), you're automatically eligible for full Extra Help. You don't need to apply separately.

## How to Apply for Extra Help

The application process is genuinely straightforward. I've walked clients through it in under 30 minutes. Here's exactly how it works.

**Option 1: Apply online at SSA.gov**
Go to ssa.gov/extrahelp. The online form takes 15 to 30 minutes to complete. You'll need to provide information about your income, assets, and household size. You don't need to upload documents upfront.

**Option 2: Call the Social Security Administration**
Call 1-800-772-1213. TTY users call 1-800-325-0778. Representatives are available Monday through Friday, 8 a.m. to 7 p.m. Eastern time.

**Option 3: Visit your local Social Security office**
Bring documents with you to save time. A list of local offices is at socialsecurity.gov/locator.

**Option 4: Apply through your state Medicaid office**
Many states allow you to apply for Extra Help and Medicare Savings Programs at the same time. This can actually unlock additional savings on top of Extra Help.

**What to have ready before you apply:**

- Your Medicare number (on your red, white, and blue Medicare card)
- Social Security number
- Information about your income: Social Security benefit amount, pension payments, wages if applicable
- Bank account balances (checking, savings)
- Approximate value of any stocks, bonds, or IRAs
- Information about any life insurance policies

After you apply, the SSA will send you a letter with their decision. If you're approved, they'll notify Medicare and you'll be automatically connected to the benefit. If you're denied, you have the right to appeal.

## What Extra Help Actually Covers, Broken Down

Let's get specific, because the details matter here.

| Cost Category | Without Extra Help | With Full Extra Help (2024) |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly Part D premium | You pay full premium | Covered up to benchmark amount |
| Annual deductible | Up to $545 | $0 |
| Copay for generic drugs | Varies by plan | $4.50 or less |
| Copay for brand-name drugs | Varies by plan | $11.20 or less |
| Coverage gap ("donut hole") costs | You pay a percentage | Eliminated entirely |

The coverage gap, also called the "donut hole," used to be a major financial hit for people with high drug costs. With Extra Help, it doesn't apply to you at all. That's a significant protection.

One thing to understand about the premium benefit: Extra Help will pay your Part D premium up to the "benchmark" amount, which is based on the average premium for plans in your region. If you choose a plan with a premium below the benchmark, you pay nothing. If you choose a plan above it, you pay the difference. This is why plan selection still matters even with Extra Help. [AARP's Medicare resource center](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/) has a helpful guide on choosing Part D plans that can walk you through what to look for.

## What If You're Denied, or Your Situation Has Changed?

Being denied for Extra Help doesn't necessarily mean you truly don't qualify. I've seen people denied because of a clerical error in their application, or because they listed an asset that's actually excluded from the count. Always appeal if you feel the decision is wrong.

To file an appeal, you have 60 days from the date of the denial letter. You can appeal in writing, online, or by phone. The SSA will assign someone to review your case.

Beyond appeals, there's another important point: your circumstances change, and so can your eligibility. If you've had a change in income, a spouse has passed away, or your assets have decreased, you may now qualify even if you were denied before. The SSA encourages you to reapply whenever your situation shifts.

If you're already receiving Extra Help, you'll be automatically re-evaluated each year. If your income or assets go up significantly, you may receive a letter saying your level of help is changing. Read those letters carefully and respond quickly if you think something is incorrect.

Also worth knowing: if you qualify for Extra Help but haven't enrolled in a Part D plan, the government will auto-enroll you in a benchmark plan. This ensures you don't lose benefits just because you haven't made a choice. But you're always allowed to switch to a plan that better fits your medications during your enrollment periods.

## Extra Help Alongside Other Programs

Extra Help doesn't exist in a vacuum. It works best when paired with other assistance programs, and most people who qualify for one qualify for others.

**Medicare Savings Programs (MSPs):** These four state-run programs (Qualified Medicare Beneficiary, Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary, Qualifying Individual, and Qualified Disabled Working Individual) help pay Medicare Part A and/or Part B premiums, deductibles, and copays. If you enroll in an MSP, you automatically get Extra Help too. Many people qualify for both and only know about one.

**Medicaid:** If you have both Medicare and Medicaid, you're what's called "dual eligible." You get Extra Help automatically. Your drug coverage may be coordinated between both programs, and your out-of-pocket costs should be very low.

**State Pharmaceutical Assistance Programs (SPAPs):** Many states offer their own drug cost help that layers on top of Extra Help. Not every state has one, but those that do can reduce costs even further. Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) can tell you what's available in your state.

**SHIP counselors:** These are free, unbiased local counselors funded by the federal government. They can review your full situation and help you apply for every program you qualify for. Find your local SHIP at shiphelp.org or by calling 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227).
