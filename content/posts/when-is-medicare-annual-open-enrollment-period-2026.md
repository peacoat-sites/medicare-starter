## October 15 to December 7: The Window That Actually Matters

Mark it now. October 15, 2026 through December 7, 2026.

That's 54 days. It sounds like plenty of time, and then suddenly it's Thanksgiving and you haven't looked at a single [plan comparison](/medigap-plan-g-vs-plan-n-comparison/) and December 7 is ten days away. I've seen this happen to sharp, organized people more times than I can count.

During this period, known officially as the Annual Enrollment Period (AEP), you can make any of the following changes to your Medicare coverage:

- Switch from Original Medicare (Parts A and B) to a Medicare Advantage plan (Part C)
- Switch from a Medicare Advantage plan back to Original Medicare
- Switch from one Medicare Advantage plan to a different one
- Join a Part D prescription drug plan if you don't already have one
- Switch from one Part D plan to a different Part D plan
- Drop your Part D plan entirely (though I'd strongly caution against that unless you have other creditable drug coverage)

Whatever you decide, the new coverage starts January 1, 2027. No exceptions.

One thing I always tell people in my first session with them: don't assume that because you did nothing last year, doing nothing again is the right call. Plans change their formularies (that's the list of covered drugs) every single year. A medication that cost you $45 a month under your current plan in 2026 might jump to $112 under that same plan in 2027 if they've changed its tier. I've seen it happen, and it blindsides people.

Let's clear up a confusion I hear constantly.

Open Enrollment for Medicare is NOT the same as Open Enrollment for the Health Insurance Marketplace (the ACA plans). Different dates, different rules, different population. The Marketplace runs November 1 through January 15 in most states. If you're on Medicare, that window is largely irrelevant to you.

Also worth knowing: if you have a Medicare Supplement plan (often called Medigap), the Annual Enrollment Period does NOT automatically give you the right to switch Medigap plans without medical underwriting. Medigap operates under different rules entirely. You can always apply for a new Medigap policy, but outside of specific guaranteed-issue windows, insurers can reject you or charge more based on your health history. This is a distinction that trips up a lot of people, and honestly, the way Medicare materials explain it doesn't help.

What AEP does cover, very clearly, is Part C (Medicare Advantage) and Part D (prescription drug plans). Those are the two levers you're working with here.

There's a meaningful range in what people pay, and the premium is often the least interesting part of the comparison. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) reported that as of this year, roughly 54% of Medicare Advantage plans available nationally carry a $0 monthly premium. That statistic shocks a lot of people. But a $0-premium plan can still cost you significantly more out-of-pocket than a plan with a $60/month premium, depending on your copays, deductibles, and how often you actually use care.

Here's a worked example that might make this concrete:

**Scenario:** Margaret, 71, takes three brand-name medications and sees a cardiologist every three months. Her current Part D plan charges a $480 annual deductible and places one of her medications on Tier 4, costing her $98/month for that drug alone. → **Action:** During AEP 2025, she compared plans on Medicare.gov's plan finder tool and found a plan with a $0 deductible that placed the same medication on Tier 2. → **Result:** Her out-of-pocket drug costs dropped by approximately $840 for the year, even though the new plan's monthly premium was $23 more than her old one.

The math isn't always that dramatic, but it's often surprising.

A second example, on the Medicare Advantage side:

**Scenario:** Robert, 68 and healthy, has been on a PPO (Preferred Provider Organization) Medicare Advantage plan paying $89/month because he assumed higher premium meant better coverage. → **Action:** He called his State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) counselor, who walked him through an HMO (Health Maintenance Organization) option in his zip code with the same in-network specialists, a $0 premium, and a lower maximum out-of-pocket cap. → **Result:** He saved $1,068 in premiums alone in 2026, with no meaningful reduction in access to his preferred providers.

The [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers this kind of comparison help for free, by the way. No sales pitch, no commission. I refer people there constantly.

## The Comparison You Actually Need

Here's a side-by-side of the major enrollment periods under Medicare, because I find the confusion between them is what paralyzes people most:

| Enrollment Period | When It Runs | Who It's For | What You Can Do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial Enrollment Period (IEP) | 7 months around your 65th birthday | New to Medicare | Enroll in Parts A, B, C, D |
| Annual Enrollment Period (AEP) | Oct 15 – Dec 7, every year | All Medicare beneficiaries | Switch or add Part C or Part D |
| Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment (MA OEP) | Jan 1 – Mar 31, every year | People already in MA plans | Switch MA plans or return to Original Medicare |
| Special Enrollment Period (SEP) | Varies by qualifying event | Specific life circumstances | Varies |
| General Enrollment Period (GEP) | Jan 1 – Mar 31 | Missed initial enrollment for Part B | Enroll in Part B (may have penalty) |

The Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period (MA OEP), January through March, is one people often don't know about. If you make a change during AEP and then realize in February that you picked the wrong plan, you do get one more chance to switch. But you can only switch to another Medicare Advantage plan or back to Original Medicare during that window. You can't use it to switch Part D plans.

## What "Doing Nothing" Actually Means

I want to push back gently on the common assumption that staying put is the safe, neutral choice.

According to CMS data, Medicare Advantage and Part D plans are permitted to change their benefits, premiums, copays, and formularies every single year. When you do nothing during AEP, you're not keeping what you have. You're accepting whatever your plan has decided to offer next year, which may or may not resemble what you chose.

Your plan mails you an Annual Notice of Change (ANOC) document every September. It outlines what's changing. Most people throw it away. That's understandable because it's a dense, jargon-heavy document. But buried in there might be the single most important piece of financial information you'll read all year. If your plan is hiking your specialty drug copay, or dropping a hospital from its network, that ANOC is where you'll find it.

AARP's Medicare resource center has a helpful guide on reading your ANOC without losing your mind, and it's worth bookmarking at [aarp.org](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/).

One more concrete example, because this is the kind of thing that only lands when you see it happen:

**Scenario:** David, 74, ignored his ANOC in September 2024 and did nothing during AEP. His Medicare Advantage plan quietly moved his primary care physician out of network effective January 2025. → **Action taken:** None in time. → **Result:** He spent the first quarter of 2025 either paying out-of-network rates or scrambling to find a new in-network doctor, costing him both money and continuity of care. He now reads his ANOC first thing every September.

## How to Actually Use the 54 Days

The practical steps, as of July 2026, for making the most of this window:

Start in September, before AEP officially opens. Read your ANOC when it arrives. Write down what's changing. Make a list of your current prescriptions, their dosages, and the pharmacies you use.

When October 15 arrives, go to [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov) and use the official Plan Finder tool. You can enter your drugs, your pharmacy preferences, and your zip code, and it will show you estimated annual costs across all available plans. This tool has gotten significantly better over the past few years. It's not perfect, but it's the most accurate starting point you have.

If you want a human being to help you, call your local SHIP office. It's free. The counselors aren't selling you anything. You can find your state's SHIP contact at [shiphelp.org](https://www.shiphelp.org/).

Submit your enrollment decision before December 7. Online, by phone (1-800-MEDICARE), or by mailing an enrollment form directly to the plan. If you're enrolling by mail, I'd build in a full week of buffer before the deadline. Postmarks don't save you here.
