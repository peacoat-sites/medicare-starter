Every fall, Medicare sends out a document most people toss in the recycling bin without reading. That's a mistake that can cost you real money.

The Annual Notice of Change, usually called an ANOC, is a letter your Medicare Advantage (Part C) plan or Medicare Part D [prescription drug plan](/how-to-choose-a-medicare-part-d-prescription-drug-plan/) is required by law to mail you each year. It tells you exactly what's changing in your plan starting January 1. Premiums, deductibles, copays, drug formularies, provider networks, if any of it is shifting, the ANOC is where you'll find out first.

Plans must mail it by September 30 each year. That timing isn't random. It lines up with the start of Medicare's [Annual Enrollment Period](/medicare-annual-enrollment-period-dates-2026/) (AEP), which runs October 15 through December 7, giving you a window to compare plans and switch if what you read doesn't work for you.

## What the ANOC Actually Contains

Think of it as a side-by-side comparison of your plan this year versus next year. The document isn't glamorous, but it's structured so you can flip to the sections that matter most to you.

You'll typically see:

- **Plan costs:** Monthly premium, annual deductible, copayments and coinsurance for different tiers of care
- **Drug coverage (formulary):** Which drugs are still covered, which moved to a higher cost-sharing tier, and which were removed entirely
- **Provider and pharmacy network:** Any changes to which doctors, hospitals, or pharmacies are in-network
- **Benefits:** Any additions or reductions in dental, vision, hearing, or other supplemental benefits (common in Medicare Advantage plans)
- **Service area:** Whether the plan still covers your geographic area

The ANOC is not a full plan document. It's a summary of changes. For the complete picture, you'd want the full Evidence of Coverage (EOC), which should arrive around the same time, or you can find it on your plan's website.

## The Section You Really Can't Skip

Drug formulary changes deserve more attention than most people give them. This is where plans quietly make moves that affect your out-of-pocket costs the most.

A drug you've been taking for years might still be "covered" but moved from Tier 2 to Tier 4 on the formulary. That tier jump can mean the difference between a $15 copay and a $80+ coinsurance charge, every single month. Plans also add [prior authorization](/original-medicare-now-requires-prior-authorization-in-six-states/) requirements, quantity limits, or step therapy rules (meaning you have to try a cheaper drug first) for drugs that had none of those restrictions before.

Look up each of your medications in the ANOC's formulary section. If your drug isn't listed at all, call the plan before the AEP closes. Don't assume a gap is a typo.

**Example:** Assume you take three maintenance medications, one on Tier 1, one on Tier 2, and one on Tier 3. Your ANOC shows the Tier 3 drug moving to Tier 4 specialty, with a 25% coinsurance instead of a flat $45 copay. If that drug costs $400/month at the pharmacy, your cost goes from $45 to $100 per fill. Over 12 months, that's $660 more out of pocket, all from one formulary change you might have missed.

## Reading the ANOC vs. Actually Using It

There's a difference between reading the document and doing something with it.

The most practical approach is to treat the ANOC as a trigger for comparison shopping, not a final verdict. Even if your plan didn't change much, a competing plan in your area might have improved its formulary, added benefits, or dropped its premium. You won't know unless you look.

Medicare's official plan comparison tool at [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov) lets you enter your drugs and preferred pharmacies to compare costs across all available plans in your zip code. It's genuinely useful. So is our [Cost Estimator](/medicare-cost-estimator/), which can help you model your total yearly spending across premiums, deductibles, and cost-sharing gaps before you commit to any plan.

One thing people often misunderstand: you don't need a "qualifying life event" to switch Medicare Advantage or Part D plans during the AEP. The Annual Enrollment Period exists for everyone, every year, no special reason required.

## What to Compare When You Sit Down with the ANOC

Here's a simple framework for comparing your current plan's changes against alternatives:

| What to Check | Why It Matters | Where to Find It |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly premium | Affects your fixed costs regardless of health use | ANOC cover page or Section 1 |
| Annual drug deductible | Applies before most drug coverage kicks in | ANOC drug coverage section |
| Formulary tier for each of your drugs | Determines your actual per-fill cost | ANOC formulary list or plan website |
| In-network status of your doctors | Out-of-network care can be dramatically more expensive | Plan website or call member services |
| Out-of-pocket maximum | The most you'll pay in a year for covered services | ANOC benefits section |
| Supplemental benefits | Dental, vision, OTC allowances, fitness programs | ANOC benefits summary |

Don't skip the out-of-pocket maximum column. Medicare Advantage plans are required to cap your annual costs for covered medical services; original Medicare has no such cap. If your plan's maximum is rising significantly, that's worth weighing.

## If Your Plan Is Leaving Your Area or Being Discontinued

This happens. Plans sometimes exit a service area or stop offering coverage in specific counties. If yours is doing that, you'll receive a separate notice, but it may come bundled with or close to your ANOC.

If your plan is discontinued, you'll automatically get a Special Enrollment Period (SEP) to pick a new plan. You won't be left without coverage. But the plan you're auto-enrolled into as a default may not be the best fit, so act during that SEP rather than letting it lapse.

The [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free, unbiased counseling in every state and can walk you through your options if your plan exits. SHIP counselors are not salespeople; they have no financial stake in what you choose. That matters more than it sounds.

## A Common Mistake Worth Naming

A lot of people assume that if they don't hear anything dramatic, their plan stayed the same. That assumption is wrong more often than you'd expect. Plans frequently make incremental changes, a new copay tier here, a narrowed formulary there, that don't feel dramatic in isolation but add up over a year.

The other common mistake is waiting until November or early December to look at the ANOC. By then, the enrollment period is almost over, you have less time to compare, and if you do decide to switch, you may not have time to verify that your doctors are in the new plan's network before January 1.

[AARP's Medicare resource center](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/) has plain-language guides on comparing plans during the AEP that are worth bookmarking alongside your ANOC when it arrives.
