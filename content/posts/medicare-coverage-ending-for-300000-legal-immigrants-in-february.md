Termination notices started landing in mailboxes this week, and if you or someone you love received one, the clock is already ticking. The Social Security Administration began mailing letters in late November 2026 to roughly 300,000 lawfully present immigrants, warning them that their Medicare coverage will end on February 1, 2027. This is not a billing mistake or a routine renewal notice. It is the direct result of H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which President Trump signed into law and which rewrites who is allowed to participate in Medicare at all. For the first time in Medicare's 60-year history, people who are already enrolled and actively using their benefits are being removed mid-coverage.

The scope of this is striking. The Congressional Budget Office originally estimated in 2025 that about 100,000 people would be affected. The actual notice volume, 300,000, is three times that figure. CMS actuaries now estimate roughly 32,000 people will ultimately lose coverage once eligibility is verified, but that gap between 300,000 notices and 32,000 projected terminations is itself alarming. It suggests a significant number of notices may have gone to people who are still legally eligible, based on errors or outdated records in government databases. Advocates at the Center for Medicare Advocacy have flagged this explicitly, warning that inaccurate government records could wrongly terminate people who qualify under the new rules.

Recipients have exactly 30 days from the date on their notice to appeal or submit documentation proving they meet the new eligibility standards. Miss that window, and coverage ends February 1 regardless.

## Who the New Law Covers, and Who It Cuts Off

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act narrows Medicare eligibility to a specific list of immigration statuses. If you fall outside that list, you lose coverage, regardless of how long you've been enrolled or how much you've contributed.

| Still Eligible Under the New Law | No Longer Eligible |
|---|---|
| U.S. citizens | Refugees |
| U.S. nationals | Asylum grantees |
| Lawful Permanent Residents (green card holders) | Temporary Protected Status (TPS) holders |
| Certain Cuban and Haitian entrants | Trafficking survivors with special visas |
| Compact of Free Association (COFA) migrants | ESRD patients with other humanitarian statuses |

That last row in the "No Longer Eligible" column is worth pausing on. People with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), a condition that requires dialysis three times a week to survive, have historically had a special pathway into Medicare regardless of age. Some of them enrolled through that pathway and now hold non-citizen statuses that the new law excludes. Losing Medicare doesn't mean they can skip dialysis. It means they face catastrophic out-of-pocket costs or no care at all.

The Washington Post reported that the affected immigrants contributed more than $6 billion to Medicare through payroll taxes, yet the new law includes no exceptions for prior financial contributions. There is no grandfather clause, no credit for years paid in.

## Why So Many Notices Went Out, and Why Some May Be Wrong

The gap between 300,000 notices and the 32,000 projected actual terminations deserves a plain explanation. The Social Security Administration cross-referenced its [Medicare enrollment](/medicare-enrollment-at-65/) files against immigration status records. Those records are not always current. People who have since naturalized, adjusted their status to lawful permanent resident, or otherwise updated their immigration paperwork may still appear in older government files under a now-excluded status.

That is a real and documented risk. The Center for Medicare Advocacy warned directly that inaccurate government records could lead to wrongful terminations. If you received a notice and believe it is an error, that belief alone is not enough. You need to respond, in writing, within 30 days, with supporting documentation.

For people who aren't sure where to start, Medicare.gov has a contact page and a 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) helpline. You can also contact your local State Health Insurance Assistance Program, known as SHIP, which provides free, unbiased counseling. Find your state's SHIP office at shiphelp.org.

## What Affected Individuals and Families Should Do Right Now

If a termination notice arrived in your mailbox or a family member's, here is the practical picture:

**Check the notice date.** The 30-day appeal clock starts from that date, not from when you opened the envelope.

**Gather immigration documents.** If your status has changed since you first enrolled in Medicare, now is the time to pull your naturalization certificate, your green card, your I-551, or any other proof that you currently hold an eligible status.

**Submit an appeal or documentation.** The notice itself should include instructions. Follow them precisely and keep copies of everything you send.

**Contact a legal or benefits advocate.** Nonprofit immigration legal aid organizations and Medicare advocacy groups can help you assess whether your status qualifies under the new law and can assist with the appeal. The Center for Medicare Advocacy at medicareadvocacy.org publishes updates on this specific situation.

**Look into alternative coverage now, not later.** If the termination stands, February 1 arrives fast. Depending on your situation and state of residence, Medicaid may cover some or all of the same services. Eligibility rules for Medicaid also shifted under H.R. 1, so this requires a conversation with your state Medicaid office or a qualified navigator.

## The Broader Policy Moment

This situation is genuinely unprecedented. Medicare has added eligible groups over the decades. It has changed premiums, benefits, and rules. It has never, until now, sent termination notices to people already enrolled in the program. That distinction matters because the people receiving these letters built their lives, their medical care, and in some cases their survival around the assumption that their coverage was stable.

Whether or not you agree with the policy itself, the administrative reality right now is that tens of thousands of people may lose coverage who actually remain eligible, simply because a government database has outdated information. The appeal window is short. The documentation requirement is real. And February 1 will not move.

If you received a notice, treat it as urgent. If someone in your family received one, help them respond. And if you're uncertain about anything, reach out to Medicare directly or to a qualified advocate before the 30-day window closes.
