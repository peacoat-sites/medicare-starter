Most people assume their Medicare Part B premium is just one flat number. It isn't, and that misunderstanding costs some beneficiaries real money every year.

The standard 2026 Part B premium is **$185.00 per month**. That's the number CMS (the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services) confirmed for the 2026 plan year, and it's what most people on Medicare actually pay. But depending on your income, you could pay anywhere from $185.00 to $628.90 a month for the exact same coverage. Same benefits. Wildly different price tag.

Here's what I want to do in this article: walk you through what Part B actually costs in 2026, explain the income-based surcharges clearly (they're called IRMAA, and they trip people up constantly), and tell you a few things most Medicare guides don't bother to mention.

## The Standard Premium, the Deductible, and What Part B Actually Covers

Start with the basics, because they matter.

In 2026, the standard monthly Part B premium is **$185.00**. That's up from $174.70 in 2024 and $185.00 in... wait, let me be precise: the 2025 standard premium was $185.00 as well. CMS held it flat for 2026. That's genuinely good news, because Part B premiums have climbed fairly steadily over the past decade.

The **annual deductible for Part B in 2026 is $257**. You pay that out of pocket before Medicare starts covering most outpatient services. After you hit the deductible, Medicare generally pays 80% of approved costs, and you're responsible for the remaining 20% with no out-of-pocket cap unless you have a Medigap (Medicare Supplement) policy or a Medicare Advantage plan.

Part B covers two broad buckets: medically necessary services (doctor visits, outpatient procedures, lab work, durable medical equipment) and preventive services (annual wellness visits, certain screenings, flu shots). The 20% coinsurance applies to the medically necessary side; many preventive services are covered at 100% with no cost-sharing.

One thing that catches people off guard: if you see a provider who doesn't accept Medicare assignment, they can charge up to 15% above the Medicare-approved amount. That extra charge is called an "excess charge," and it's not covered by Medicare at all. A good Medigap Plan G or Plan N will cover excess charges (Plan G does; Plan N doesn't). Worth knowing before you choose a supplement.

## IRMAA: The Income-Based Surcharge Nobody Warned You About

IRMAA stands for Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount. It's the extra premium that higher-income beneficiaries pay on top of the standard $185.00. The [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) calculates it annually based on your income from two years prior. So your 2026 premium is based on your 2024 tax return.

That two-year lookback is the part that surprises people most. You retire in 2025, your income drops dramatically, but you're still paying a 2026 surcharge calculated from your 2024 working income. There's a fix for this (called a Life-Changing Event appeal, filed on SSA Form SSA-44), but you have to know to ask.

Here are the 2026 IRMAA brackets. These apply to your 2024 **modified adjusted gross income**, or MAGI:

**For individuals (single filers):**

- Up to $106,000: $185.00/month (standard)
- $106,001 to $133,000: $259.00/month
- $133,001 to $167,000: $370.00/month
- $167,001 to $200,000: $481.00/month
- $200,001 to $500,000: $591.90/month
- Above $500,000: $628.90/month

**For married couples filing jointly, the thresholds roughly double** (e.g., up to $212,000 pays the standard rate). Married filing separately gets treated more harshly by the formula, with surcharges kicking in at much lower income levels.

I want to flag something here that most articles gloss over: these IRMAA brackets are adjusted annually for inflation, but the adjustments are modest. If your income sits just above a threshold, it's worth running the numbers. Staying $3,000 below a bracket cutoff could save you $74 a month, or $888 a year, in Part B premiums alone. That's before you factor in the parallel IRMAA surcharge on Part D (drug coverage), which layers on top.

## How You Actually Pay the Premium

If you're already receiving Social Security benefits, your Part B premium is deducted automatically from your monthly check. You don't write a check or set up a bank transfer. It just comes out.

If you're not yet drawing Social Security (maybe you're 65 but delaying benefits to grow your monthly payment), Medicare bills you directly, usually quarterly. You can pay online at pay.gov, by check, or through Medicare Easy Pay, which is an automatic bank draft. I'd go with Easy Pay if you're in this situation. Forgetting a quarterly bill and triggering a lapse in coverage is a headache nobody needs.

Railroad Retirement Board beneficiaries have their premiums deducted from their RRB payments, same basic concept as Social Security.

## When You Might Pay More (or Less) Than the Standard

A few specific situations change your premium outside of IRMAA.

**Late enrollment penalty.** If you didn't sign up for Part B when you were first eligible and didn't qualify for a Special Enrollment Period, you'll pay a 10% premium penalty for every 12-month period you went without Part B. These penalties are permanent. Someone who delayed two years pays 20% more for life. The standard $185.00 becomes $222.00, and that's before any IRMAA surcharge.

**Low-income help.** If your income and assets are limited, Medicare Savings Programs (MSPs) can pay your Part B premium entirely. There are four MSP levels, and the most common, the Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) program, covers the Part B premium, deductible, and coinsurance. In 2026, QMB income limits are roughly $1,325/month for individuals (the exact figure adjusts by state). The [AARP Medicare resource center](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/) has solid, plain-English guidance on MSP eligibility if you want to dig in. These programs are under-enrolled. If there's any chance you qualify, apply.

**Employer or union coverage.** Some retirees have Part B premiums reimbursed by a former employer or union health plan. This is especially common among federal retirees under FEHB. If you have this benefit, track it, because if your coverage type changes, so can the reimbursement.

## What the Premium Gets You, Practically Speaking

I think it helps to put $185.00 a month in context. That's $2,220 a year for coverage that includes all your outpatient care: primary care visits, specialist visits, lab work, imaging, surgical procedures done in outpatient settings, most durable medical equipment, chemotherapy (in some settings), and a long list of preventive screenings.

The 20% coinsurance with no cap is the real financial risk. A $50,000 outpatient procedure leaves you with a $10,000 bill. This is why most people pair Part B with either a Medigap policy or a Medicare Advantage plan, both of which add a layer of cost protection on top of Original Medicare.

Medigap Plan G is the most comprehensive option available to new enrollees in 2026 (Plan C and Plan F, which cover the deductible too, are only available to people who were Medicare-eligible before January 1, 2020). Plan G premiums vary enormously by age, location, and insurance company. A 65-year-old in a mid-sized Midwest city might find Plan G for $110 to $160 a month; in New York or California, the same coverage can run $250 or more. That's a real range, and it's worth comparing through Medicare.gov's plan finder or working with an independent insurance broker who can pull quotes from multiple carriers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the $185 Part B premium going up in 2027?

Nobody knows yet. CMS announces the following year's premium in the fall, typically October or November. The 2027 premium will be set by fall 2026. What we do know is that Part B premiums have generally increased over time, tied to Medicare spending growth. Holding flat two years in a row (2025 and 2026 both at $185) is actually unusual.

### Can my Part B premium change mid-year?

In most cases, no. Your premium is set at the start of the year and stays fixed through December. The exception: if your income changes significantly and you file an IRMAA appeal (SSA Form SSA-44) due to a qualifying life-changing event like retirement, divorce, or the death of a spouse, SSA can adjust your premium during the year.

### Do I have to pay Part B if I have coverage through my spouse's employer?

No, you can delay Part B without penalty if you're covered by an active employer group health plan (your own or your spouse's). "Active" is the key word. Retiree coverage does not count, COBRA does not count, and marketplace coverage does not count. When that employer coverage ends, you have an 8-month Special Enrollment Period to sign up for Part B penalty-free.

### Does Part B cover dental, vision, or hearing?

Original Medicare Part B does not cover routine dental, vision, or hearing. No cleanings, no glasses, no hearing aids. Some Medicare Advantage plans include these benefits, which is one of the main reasons people choose Advantage over Original Medicare plus Medigap. If you stick with Original Medicare, you'll need separate standalone coverage or pay out of pocket for these services.

### What happens if I don't pay my Part B premium?

Medicare can disenroll you from Part B if you fall behind on premiums, typically after a two-month grace period. If that happens, you lose your outpatient coverage and will likely face a permanent late enrollment penalty when you re-enroll. It's a situation worth going to considerable lengths to avoid. If you're struggling to afford the premium, check MSP eligibility before you skip a payment.

The $185.00 standard premium is a starting point, not a guarantee of what you'll pay. Your income, your enrollment timing, your state, and your other coverage all move that number. Look up your specific situation before assuming the standard applies to you, and if IRMAA is likely to hit you next year, plan around the brackets now rather than after the fact. A brief conversation with a Medicare counselor through your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) is free and often worth an hour of your time.
