[Open enrollment](/medicare-open-enrollment-period/) closes December 7, 2026. And this year, missing that deadline carries a specific, calculable cost that it hasn't carried in recent memory.

Here's what changed. A federal program called the Part D Premium Stabilization Demonstration has been quietly holding down your Medicare drug plan premiums for the past two years. It ends December 31, 2026, one year earlier than originally planned. CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz ended it ahead of schedule, calling it a "bailout" for insurers, according to reporting from The Business Journal. When it disappears, the average standalone Part D ([prescription drug](/medicare-prescription-drug-costs-2026/)) plan premium jumps from about $34 a month in 2026 to a projected $51 a month in 2027, based on CMS's November 17, 2026 announcement. That's a roughly 50% increase in a single year. KFF has warned that some beneficiaries could see increases of up to $20 a month depending on their current plan. CMS's own estimate, reported by Yahoo Finance on November 18, 2026, is that most people will see increases of $10 or less, but that average hides a lot of individual variation.

The window to do something about this closes December 7. After that, you're locked into whatever plan you're in, at whatever premium it charges come January.

## Who Gets Hit the Hardest

Not every Medicare enrollee feels this equally. If you're in a [Medicare Advantage plan](/medicare-advantage-plan-exits-are-accelerating-into-2027/) (also called Part C) that includes drug coverage, your situation is mostly unchanged. The subsidy reduction targets standalone Part D plans, which are bought separately by people who have Original Medicare (Part A and Part B) rather than a Medicare Advantage plan.

If you have Original Medicare and a standalone Part D plan, this is the change that directly affects your wallet. Your current plan's premium may jump significantly, and your plan may not even be the best option anymore once premiums reset.

There's a real protection in place for lower-income beneficiaries. If you're enrolled in Extra Help, sometimes called the Low Income Subsidy or LIS, you're shielded from these premium increases. Extra Help is a federal program that helps people with limited income and resources pay for Part D costs. If you think you might qualify but haven't applied, now is a very good time to check at Medicare.gov or call 1-800-MEDICARE.

## What's Actually Changing in 2027

The premium increase gets the most attention, and rightly so, but 2027 brings a few other changes worth knowing.

| What's Changing | 2026 | 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Average standalone Part D premium | ~$34/month | ~$51/month |
| Part D out-of-pocket cap | $2,100 | $2,400 |
| Medicare-negotiated drug prices (IRA) | First 10 drugs | 15 drugs, including Ozempic and Wegovy |

The out-of-pocket cap rising from $2,100 to $2,400 is a step backward in affordability for people who take high-cost drugs. That cap, introduced in 2025, was a major win for people managing expensive chronic conditions. Watching it climb stings.

On the other side, 15 drugs with newly negotiated prices take effect January 1, 2027, under the Inflation Reduction Act. Ozempic and Wegovy are on that list, which matters enormously to the growing number of Medicare enrollees using GLP-1 medications. If you take one of the newly negotiated drugs, your out-of-pocket cost per dose may drop even as your premium rises. Whether that tradeoff works in your favor depends entirely on which drugs you take and how often.

## Why Your Current Plan May No Longer Be the Best Deal

Insurance companies respond to market shifts, and the end of the subsidy is a big one. A plan that was smartly priced in 2026 may look very different when premiums reprice for 2027. Plans that received more subsidy dollars may see larger increases. Plans that relied less on the subsidy, or that adjusted their formularies (the list of covered drugs), might end up being better value.

The only way to know is to compare. Medicare's Plan Finder tool at Medicare.gov lets you plug in your specific drugs and pharmacy to see what you'd actually pay across all plans available in your area. Don't just look at the premium. Factor in your drug copays, your pharmacy's participation, and the deductible. A plan with a $20 lower premium but $50 higher copays on a drug you take monthly isn't saving you anything.

If your income is higher and you're wondering whether that affects your Part D costs, the [IRMAA Medicare Calculator](/irmaa-medicare-calculator/) can show you whether you're subject to income-related premium surcharges for Part B and Part D, which is a separate layer of cost some enrollees don't anticipate.

## The Steps That Still Make Sense Before December 7

There's not much time, so let's keep this practical.

First, find out what your current plan is charging in 2027. Your plan is required to send you an Annual Notice of Change (ANOC) by October. If you haven't read it, find it now. It will show your new premium, deductible, and any drug formulary changes taking effect January 1.

Second, go to Medicare.gov and run a comparison using the Plan Finder. Enter every drug you take, the dosage, and your preferred pharmacy. Look at the full year's estimated cost, not just the monthly premium.

Third, if you find a better plan, switch before December 7. Enrollment is straightforward through Medicare.gov, by calling 1-800-MEDICARE, or through a State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) counselor in your area. SHIP counselors are free and unbiased.

If you're unsure whether Original Medicare plus a standalone Part D is even the right structure for you, as opposed to Medicare Advantage, that's worth thinking through carefully. Plans differ significantly by region, and what makes sense depends on your health situation and your providers.

## A Few Things This Change Doesn't Affect

Medicare Part A and Part B premiums, deductibles, and coverage are unchanged by the end of this demonstration. The Part B deductible and the standard Part B premium are set separately and aren't tied to Part D at all.

Extra Help recipients, again, are specifically protected. If you're on Medicaid and Medicare (sometimes called "dual eligible"), you likely already have Extra Help. If you're unsure of your status, ask your state Medicaid office or call 1-800-MEDICARE.

This change also doesn't affect standalone Medigap (Medicare Supplement) policies, which cover gaps in Original Medicare but don't include drug coverage. Those policyholders still need a separate Part D plan, which means this premium increase applies to them too.

Waiting to see what happens in 2027 before making a change isn't really an option here. Once December 7 passes, your current plan locks in for the full year. Given that this particular subsidy expiration is a one-time structural shift rather than a minor annual adjustment, taking a few hours now to review your options is genuinely worth your time. Medicare.gov has the tools to make that comparison straightforward, and SHIP counselors can walk you through it at no cost if you'd prefer help from a real person.
