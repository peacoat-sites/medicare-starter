Most people find out about IRMAA the worst possible way: they get their Medicare bill and it's $100 or $200 higher than they expected, with no warning and no explanation they can easily understand. I've seen that moment play out in my office more times than I can count, and the look on people's faces is always the same. Confusion, then a flash of anger, then something close to defeat. You've done everything right, saved carefully, and now Medicare is charging you more because of it.

So let's talk about what IRMAA actually is, why it hits when it does, and what your real options are.

## What IRMAA Is and Why It Exists

IRMAA stands for Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount. That's Medicare's official name for the extra premium surcharge added to your Part B (medical insurance) and Part D (prescription drug coverage) costs when your income exceeds certain thresholds.

Here's the basic idea: Medicare Part B has a standard premium that most enrollees pay. In 2025, that standard amount is $185.00 per month. But if Social Security sees that your income was above a certain level two years ago, you'll pay more than that. Potentially a lot more.

The same logic applies to Part D. You don't pay a flat IRMAA surcharge on top of your plan-specific premium. You pay it regardless of which Part D plan you choose, and it changes based on the same income brackets.

The reason it exists is simple: Congress decided in 2003, and then expanded the policy in 2010 and again in 2011, that higher-income Medicare beneficiaries should shoulder more of the program's costs. Whether that's fair is a whole separate conversation. What matters right now is that it's the law, and it's enforced automatically.

This is the part that blindsides people most often.

Social Security doesn't look at what you earned this year when calculating your IRMAA. They look at your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) from two years prior. In 2025, that means they're using your 2023 tax return.

MAGI for Medicare purposes includes your Adjusted Gross Income plus any tax-exempt interest income you received. So yes, even tax-free municipal bond interest counts.

The thresholds for 2025 are divided into five tiers above the standard premium:

- Individual filers with 2023 MAGI between $106,000 and $133,000 pay an extra $74.00/month in Part B IRMAA
- $133,001 to $167,000 adds $185.00/month
- $167,001 to $200,000 adds $295.90/month
- $200,001 to $500,000 adds $406.90/month
- Above $500,000 adds $443.90/month

Married couples filing jointly have thresholds that are roughly double those figures. Married individuals who lived together but filed separately face a much harsher schedule, with the second bracket kicking in at just $106,000.

That joint-filer-living-apart rule trips up a lot of people. I once worked with a couple who had lived in different states for years, one retired in Florida and one still working in New York. They filed separately for legitimate tax reasons, and the wife ended up paying the second-highest IRMAA tier on what was actually a modest retirement income.

## The Life-Changing Events Exception: This Is Where People Leave Money on the Table

Okay, here's where I get a little fired up, because this part of IRMAA is underused to a degree that's almost criminal.

If your income has dropped significantly since that two-year-ago tax return because of a qualifying life event, you can ask Social Security to use a more recent year's income instead. The formal request is made on SSA Form SSA-44, and it's free to file.

The qualifying events are:

- Marriage, divorce, or annulment
- Death of a spouse
- You or your spouse stopped working or reduced work hours
- You or your spouse lost income-producing property due to a disaster or other event beyond your control
- You or your spouse experienced a scheduled cessation, termination, or reorganization of an employer's pension plan
- You or your spouse received a settlement from an employer or former employer because of closure, bankruptcy, or reorganization

Notice what's on that list: retirement. If you retired in 2024 and your income dropped substantially, but SSA is still basing your 2025 IRMAA on your 2023 working income, you have every right to file that SSA-44 and ask them to use your 2024 income or an estimate of your current income instead.

I have watched people walk out of Social Security offices having reduced their monthly Part B premium by more than $300 by doing exactly this. It takes time and paperwork, but it's worth every minute.

AARP's Medicare resource center has a solid breakdown of the SSA-44 process if you want a second set of eyes on how to complete it, and I always recommend cross-checking eligibility details at [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) directly.

## What You Can Actually Do About IRMAA (And What You Can't)

Let me be direct: if you're already on Medicare and your income was high in the lookback year, you may just owe it. No appeal changes the income calculation itself, only the *year* used as the basis. If 2023 was legitimately a high-income year and your life hasn't changed in a qualifying way, the surcharge is probably accurate.

But there are real strategies worth knowing, especially for people who haven't retired yet or are still making financial planning decisions.

Roth conversions are the most commonly discussed tool, and for good reason. Traditional IRA or 401(k) withdrawals count toward your MAGI; Roth withdrawals generally don't. So a retiree who spent years moving money into a Roth account before Medicare enrollment may avoid IRMAA thresholds entirely. The math isn't always obvious, and a financial planner or CPA who specializes in retirement income needs to run these numbers for your specific situation.

Tax-loss harvesting, timing large capital gains, and being thoughtful about when you take required minimum distributions (RMDs) can all move the needle. The two-year lookback means you often have a window to act.

What a lot of people don't realize is that you can request a new income determination every year. If your income dropped for a non-qualifying reason (a bad investment year, for instance), that won't help you now, but it will help you in two years when that year becomes the lookback year.

One more thing: if you receive a notice that you owe IRMAA and you believe the amount is wrong because of an IRS data error or incorrect information, you can appeal that too. You don't need a qualifying life event to challenge a factual error. Request the appeal in writing within 60 days of the determination notice.

## How IRMAA Shows Up in Practice

Social Security deducts your full Part B premium, including any IRMAA, directly from your Social Security benefit check. So you won't receive a separate invoice for most people. It just comes out automatically.

Part D IRMAA is handled differently. Your plan itself is not notified of your IRMAA amount. Instead, Social Security bills you directly for the surcharge, separate from your plan premium. You can pay by check, bank draft, or have it deducted from your Social Security payment. If you don't pay it, you can lose your Part D coverage.

That last sentence bears repeating. Non-payment of Part D IRMAA is an actual disenrollment risk. Don't ignore those Social Security billing notices.

If you're on a Medicare Advantage plan (sometimes called Part C) that includes drug coverage, you still pay Part D IRMAA. Medicare Advantage doesn't shelter you from it.
