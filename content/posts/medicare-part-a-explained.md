## What Medicare Part A Actually Covers

Medicare Part A is your hospital insurance. That's the simplest way to describe it. But "hospital insurance" covers more ground than most people expect.

Part A pays for inpatient hospital care, which means care you receive after a doctor formally admits you as a hospital inpatient. It also covers care in a skilled nursing facility (SNF) following a qualifying hospital stay, hospice care for those with a terminal illness, and some home health care services. Each of these comes with its own rules, so let's take them one at a time.

**Inpatient hospital care** includes your semi-private room, meals, general nursing, medications you receive during your stay, and most other hospital services and supplies. This is probably what most people picture when they think of Part A.

**Skilled nursing facility care** is a benefit that surprises a lot of my clients. If you've had a qualifying inpatient hospital stay of at least three consecutive days (not counting the discharge day), and your doctor certifies that you need skilled care like physical therapy, wound care, or IV medications, Part A can help cover your SNF stay. The key word is "skilled." Custodial care, meaning help with bathing, dressing, and daily activities, is not covered by Part A.

**Hospice care** is covered when a doctor certifies that a patient is terminally ill with a life expectancy of six months or less, and the patient chooses comfort-focused care over curative treatment. Part A covers hospice services almost entirely, including pain relief medications related to the terminal diagnosis.

**Home health care** under Part A covers medically necessary skilled nursing care, physical therapy, speech-language pathology, and occupational therapy when you're homebound and a doctor orders the care. There's no cost-sharing for approved home health services.

One important thing people sometimes get confused about: observation status. If you're in the hospital but haven't been formally admitted as an inpatient, Medicare considers you an outpatient, even if you stay overnight for several days. That matters because observation stays don't count toward the three-day SNF qualifying requirement, and your cost-sharing will fall under Part B instead of Part A. Always ask your care team whether you're admitted as an inpatient.

Here's the part many people are happily surprised by. Most people pay $0 in premiums for Part A. That's because you or your spouse paid Medicare taxes (through payroll deductions) during your working years, and you've essentially earned your Part A coverage.

To get premium-free Part A, you generally need 40 work credits, which translates to about 10 years of work in jobs where you paid Medicare taxes. If you have between 30 and 39 credits, you'll pay a reduced premium. If you have fewer than 30 credits, you'll pay the full premium. Medicare.gov posts the current premium amounts each year, and those figures are updated with each annual cost-of-living adjustment.

But premium-free doesn't mean completely free. Part A has cost-sharing that kicks in when you actually use the benefit.

**The Part A deductible** applies per benefit period, not per calendar year. A benefit period begins the day you're admitted as a hospital inpatient and ends when you've been out of the hospital and haven't received skilled care in an SNF for 60 consecutive days. You pay the deductible once per benefit period, and there's no cap on how many benefit periods you can have in a year.

After you've paid the deductible, Part A covers your full hospital costs for days 1 through 60. From days 61 through 90, you pay a daily coinsurance amount. Beyond day 90, you dip into what's called your "lifetime reserve days." You have 60 lifetime reserve days total (not per benefit period), and once they're gone, they're gone. After those are exhausted, you're responsible for all hospital costs.

For SNF care, Part A covers the first 20 days fully. Days 21 through 100 require a daily coinsurance payment. After 100 days, Part A pays nothing. This is why many people choose to add a Medicare Supplement plan (also called Medigap) to help cover those gaps.

## When to Enroll: Deadlines You Really Can't Afford to Miss

Timing your Medicare enrollment correctly is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make leading up to retirement. Get it wrong, and you could face late enrollment penalties or coverage gaps.

Your **Initial Enrollment Period (IEP)** for Medicare Part A spans seven months: the three months before the month you turn 65, your birthday month, and the three months after. If you enroll before or during your birthday month, coverage can start on the first day of your birthday month. If you enroll in one of the three months after, your start date shifts forward.

If you're already receiving Social Security benefits when you turn 65, you'll be automatically enrolled in both Part A and Part B. Your red, white, and blue Medicare card will arrive in the mail about three months before your 65th birthday.

If you're still working at 65 and covered by an employer group health plan through a current employer (or a spouse's current employer), you may qualify for a Special Enrollment Period (SEP) that lets you delay enrolling without penalty. The key word is "current" employer. COBRA coverage and retiree health coverage don't count.

If you don't enroll when you're first eligible and you don't qualify for an SEP, you'll have to wait for the **General Enrollment Period (GEP)**, which runs from January 1 through March 31 each year, with coverage starting July 1. And if you have to pay a premium for Part A (meaning you have fewer than 40 work credits), late enrollment can trigger a penalty of 10% added to your premium for twice the number of years you delayed. That penalty stays for as long as you have Part A.

When in doubt, talk to a SHIP counselor. The State Health Insurance Assistance Program ([SHIP](https://www.shiphelp.org/)) offers free, unbiased help from trained counselors in every state. They can look at your specific work history, current coverage, and timeline and tell you exactly what to do.

## How Part A Works With Your Other Coverage

Part A doesn't work in isolation. Understanding how it fits with the rest of your Medicare picture matters quite a bit.

**Part B** is your medical insurance, covering doctor visits, outpatient procedures, lab work, and preventive services. Parts A and B together form what people call Original Medicare. Most people enroll in both at the same time.

**Medicare Advantage (Part C)** plans are offered by private insurers approved by Medicare. If you enroll in a Medicare Advantage plan, you still technically have Parts A and B, but the private plan delivers your benefits instead of Original Medicare. Your hospital stays would be covered under the plan's rules, which may include different cost-sharing structures, prior authorization requirements, and network restrictions.

**Medicare Supplement (Medigap) plans** are sold by private insurers and designed to fill the gaps in Original Medicare's cost-sharing. Because Part A's deductible applies per benefit period and the daily coinsurance amounts for long hospital stays can add up significantly, many people find a Medigap plan worth the monthly premium. The coverage provided by each standardized plan letter is set by federal rules, so a Plan G from one insurer covers exactly the same things as a Plan G from another.

**Part D** covers prescription drugs. Original Medicare (Parts A and B) does cover drugs administered in a hospital setting, but it doesn't cover the prescriptions you pick up at a pharmacy. For that, you'll need a Part D plan or a Medicare Advantage plan that includes drug coverage.

## Step-by-Step: Getting Your Part A Coverage Set Up

Here's a practical checklist to make sure your Part A enrollment goes smoothly.

**Step 1: Check your Social Security statement.** Log into your My Social Security account at ssa.gov to see your work credits. Confirm whether you have 40 or more.

**Step 2: Find out if you'll be auto-enrolled.** If you're already collecting Social Security or Railroad Retirement Board benefits at least four months before turning 65, you'll be enrolled automatically. If not, you'll need to apply.

**Step 3: Apply if needed.** You can apply for Medicare Part A online at ssa.gov, by calling Social Security at 1-800-772-1213, or by visiting your local Social Security office. The online application takes about 10 minutes.

**Step 4: Determine if you should delay.** If you have qualifying employer coverage through a current employer, consult with your HR department and a SHIP counselor before deciding whether to delay. Get the decision in writing.

**Step 5: Note your effective date.** Once approved, confirm the date your coverage begins. Don't let it catch you off guard.

**Step 6: Decide about supplemental coverage.** Once Part A is in place, think through whether Original Medicare alone is enough or whether a Medigap plan makes sense. [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) has a plan finder tool that lets you compare options in your area.

**Step 7: Keep your Medicare card safe.** Treat it like any other important identity document. You'll present it at hospitals and care facilities.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Medicare Part A

### Does Medicare Part A cover long-term nursing home care?

No. This is one of the most important misunderstandings I run into. Part A covers skilled nursing facility care for a limited time after a qualifying hospital stay, but it does not cover long-term custodial care. If you need help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, or eating over an extended period, that cost falls to you personally, or it may be covered by Medicaid if you meet income and asset requirements. Long-term care insurance is a separate product designed to address this gap.

### What is a benefit period, and why does it matter?

A benefit period is how Medicare measures your use of Part A benefits. It starts when you're admitted as a hospital inpatient and ends when you've been out of the hospital and not in skilled care for 60 consecutive days in a row. You pay a separate Part A deductible for each benefit period. In theory, you could face multiple deductibles in a single calendar year if you have separate illnesses or injuries with enough time between them. There's no annual limit on benefit periods.

### Can I have Part A without Part B?

Yes, you can have Part A without Part B. Some people who are still working and have employer coverage choose to enroll in Part A (especially if it's premium-free) while delaying Part B. However, if you take Part A, you generally cannot continue contributing to a Health Savings Account (HSA). Talk to your benefits administrator and a SHIP counselor before making that decision.

### What happens if I need emergency care before I've enrolled in Medicare?

If you're eligible for Medicare but haven't yet enrolled, Original Medicare won't cover your care. This is why understanding your enrollment windows is so important. If you're in your Initial Enrollment Period and you haven't enrolled yet, do it now. If you're past your IEP and don't have a qualifying Special Enrollment Period, you may face a coverage gap until the next General Enrollment Period.

### Does Medicare Part A cover mental health care in a hospital?

Yes. Part A covers inpatient mental health care received in a general hospital or a psychiatric hospital. There is a lifetime limit of 190 days for inpatient psychiatric hospital care specifically (not including psychiatric units in general hospitals), though most people never approach that limit. The same deductible and coinsurance structure applies as with medical inpatient stays.
