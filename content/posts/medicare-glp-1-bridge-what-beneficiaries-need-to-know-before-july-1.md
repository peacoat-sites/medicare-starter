Most of the coverage on the Medicare GLP-1 Bridge has focused on the headline: starting July 1, 2026, Medicare will cover weight-loss drugs for the first time in its history. What's getting buried is the fine print that actually determines whether this helps you, and how much it costs. A few details in particular could surprise people who assume they're set.

Here's what you need to know before the program goes live.

## What the Bridge Actually Is (and Isn't)

The Medicare GLP-1 Bridge is an 18-month demonstration program run by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, or CMS. It runs from July 1, 2026 through December 31, 2027. Think of a demonstration program as a policy experiment: CMS runs it, collects data, and uses the results to decide whether to make something permanent.

This is not a permanent benefit. It's also not part of your standard Part D (prescription drug) coverage. CMS is operating it as a separate structure, which is why your Part D plan isn't managing it and why you don't have to sign up for anything. If you qualify, coverage is automatic.

The BALANCE Model, a longer-term approach that would have let Part D plans opt into obesity drug coverage starting in January 2027, was delayed indefinitely by CMS in May 2026, according to the [CMS press release from May 6](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/coming-soon-cms-provide-50-monthly-access-glp-1-medications-medicare-beneficiaries). That means for now, the Bridge is the only Medicare pathway to obesity-specific GLP-1 coverage. What happens after December 2027 is genuinely unknown.

Eligibility comes down to two routes. You qualify if you have a body mass index, or BMI, of 35 or higher. You also qualify with a BMI of 27 or higher, provided you have certain comorbidities, with uncontrolled hypertension listed as one example. The drug also has to be prescribed specifically to treat obesity, not as a diabetes medication.

That last piece matters more than it sounds. Ozempic, which contains the same active ingredient as Wegovy (semaglutide), stays under normal Part D rules because it's approved and typically prescribed for Type 2 diabetes. The Bridge is for obesity treatment specifically, so the prescribing context and indication on the prescription determine which bucket you fall into.

You also need to be enrolled in Part D to access the Bridge. Medicare Advantage, or MA, plans that include Part D coverage count. If you only have Original Medicare Parts A and B with no drug coverage, you're not eligible.

## The $50 Copay: What They're Not Telling You

Every beneficiary who qualifies pays a flat $50 per month for their GLP-1 medication under the Bridge. That's it, no tiered formulary drama, no prior authorization labyrinth under the Bridge structure itself.

The catch is significant: that $50 monthly copay does not count toward your $2,100 annual Part D out-of-pocket cap. Under the standard 2026 Part D benefit, once you've paid $2,100 out of pocket during the year, your covered drugs cost you nothing for the rest of the year. Bridge copays exist outside that accounting entirely.

For someone who hits the cap on other medications, this means paying $50 a month for their GLP-1 even after they'd otherwise pay zero for everything else. Over 12 months, that's $600 that doesn't reduce your Part D liability at all. It's not a deal-breaker for most people, but it's a real cost to factor in, especially for beneficiaries managing multiple expensive conditions.

## Which Drugs Are Covered

Only three medications are covered under the Bridge. Wegovy (semaglutide, injectable and oral), Zepbound in KwikPen form (tirzepatide, injectable), and Foundayo, which is Eli Lilly's newer GLP-1 pill. As the [Medicare Rights Center noted in their June 4 explainer](https://www.medicarerights.org/medicare-watch/2026/06/04/glp-1-weight-loss-drug-demonstration-begins-july-2026), standard diabetes GLP-1s like Ozempic, Mounjaro, and Rybelsus remain under normal Part D rules and are not part of the Bridge.

If your doctor has been prescribing you Ozempic off-label for weight loss, that won't automatically switch over to Bridge coverage. Your doctor would need to prescribe a covered Bridge medication specifically for obesity treatment.

One practical question: supply. Wegovy and Zepbound have faced persistent shortages. The Bridge covering them for a large new population of Medicare beneficiaries could tighten supply further, at least in the short term. Worth asking your pharmacy about availability before counting on a seamless July 1 start.

## What to Do Before July 1

You don't register for the Bridge, and you don't opt in. But there are things worth doing now.

Talk to your doctor. If you meet the BMI criteria and want to explore this, the conversation needs to happen before July 1 so a prescription is ready to go. Your doctor also needs to prescribe one of the three covered medications specifically for obesity treatment, and that documentation matters for coverage to work correctly.

Check your current prescriptions. If you're already on a GLP-1 for diabetes, don't assume anything changes. Your coverage path stays under Part D. If you're taking Wegovy or Zepbound already for obesity and paying out of pocket or under Part D, the Bridge could represent meaningful savings starting July 1.

Contact your Part D plan or Medicare Advantage plan with specific questions about how the Bridge interacts with your coverage. The [CMS beneficiary information page](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coverage/prescription-drug-coverage/medicare-glp-1-bridge/information-medicare-beneficiaries) is the cleanest source for current details directly from the agency.

And one more thing: if you have limited income, check whether you qualify for the Extra Help program (also called the Low Income Subsidy), which reduces Part D costs broadly. The National Council on Aging has useful guidance on that at NCOA.org.

The GLP-1 Bridge is genuinely historic. Medicare has never covered drugs prescribed solely for obesity until now, and for eligible beneficiaries, $50 a month for Wegovy or Zepbound is a real reduction from what many have been paying out of pocket. But it's a temporary, limited program with a cost structure that doesn't work like the rest of Part D. Understanding those distinctions now, before July 1, puts you in a much better position to use it well. A conversation with your doctor and a benefits counselor before the launch date is the right move.
