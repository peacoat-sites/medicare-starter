The standard Medicare Part B premium is $209.40 a month in 2027, and the annual deductible is $292. CMS announced both on November 17, 2026. In 2026 they were $202.90 and $283. After you meet the deductible, you generally pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount for doctor visits and outpatient care.

That's the amount most people pay. Your own number can be higher if your income is above the IRMAA limits or you signed up late, and lower if a state program picks up the premium.

## Part B costs at a glance

| Cost | 2026 | 2027 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard monthly premium | $202.90 | $209.40 |
| Standard premium for the year | $2,434.80 | $2,512.80 |
| Annual deductible | $283 | $292 |
| Coinsurance after the deductible | 20% | 20% |
| Monthly premium with IRMAA | $284.10 to $689.90 | $293.10 to $711.80 |

The 2027 increase is $6.50 a month, about 3.2%. That's a much smaller jump than 2026, when the premium rose $17.90 (9.7%). By law, the standard premium is set to cover roughly a quarter of Part B program costs, with general federal revenue paying the rest, so the premium follows Part B spending. For the full history back to 2015, see [Medicare costs by year](/medicare-costs-by-year/).

## What you pay after the deductible

You pay the first $292 of Part B-covered care in 2027 yourself. After that, Medicare pays 80% of the approved amount and you pay 20%. Original Medicare has no annual out-of-pocket cap on that 20%, which is the main reason most people add a Medigap policy or choose a Medicare Advantage plan.

Most preventive services, such as the yearly wellness visit and many screenings, cost nothing if your provider accepts assignment. If you see a doctor who doesn't accept assignment, they can charge up to 15% above the Medicare-approved amount. That's an excess charge, and Original Medicare doesn't pay it. Medigap Plan G covers excess charges; Plan N doesn't.

**Example:** You pay the standard 2027 premium and have $5,000 of Medicare-approved outpatient care during the year. You pay the $292 deductible, then 20% of the remaining $4,708, which is $941.60. That's $1,233.60 in cost sharing. Add $2,512.80 in premiums and Part B costs you $3,746.40 for the year before any supplemental coverage.

## Higher incomes pay more: IRMAA

If your 2025 modified adjusted gross income was above $112,000 on a single return or $224,000 on a joint return, your 2027 Part B premium includes an income-related monthly adjustment amount (IRMAA).

| Single return (2025 MAGI) | Joint return (2025 MAGI) | 2027 Part B per month |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $112,000 or less | $224,000 or less | $209.40 |
| $112,001 to $141,000 | $224,001 to $282,000 | $293.10 |
| $141,001 to $177,000 | $282,001 to $354,000 | $418.80 |
| $177,001 to $212,000 | $354,001 to $424,000 | $544.40 |
| $212,001 to $499,999 | $424,001 to $749,999 | $670.00 |
| $500,000 or more | $750,000 or more | $711.80 |

A separate Part D surcharge applies at the same income levels. For both tables, the 2026 brackets, married-filing-separately rules and how to appeal with Form SSA-44, see our guide to [Medicare IRMAA brackets](/irmaa-medicare-surcharge/), or run your numbers in the [IRMAA calculator](/irmaa-medicare-calculator/).

## When you pay more or less than the standard

**Late enrollment penalty.** If you didn't sign up when you were first eligible and didn't have a Special Enrollment Period, your premium goes up 10% for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but didn't. The penalty lasts as long as you have Part B. Two years late means paying 20% more: $251.28 a month instead of $209.40 in 2027.

**Delaying with job-based coverage.** If you or your spouse are still working and covered by that employer's group plan, you can usually delay Part B without a penalty. You then get 8 months after the job or the coverage ends to sign up. COBRA, retiree coverage and Marketplace plans don't count as active employer coverage, which catches a lot of people.

**Help from a Medicare Savings Program.** These state-run programs pay the Part B premium for people with limited income and savings. The Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) program also covers Part A and B deductibles and coinsurance. The limits are tied to the federal poverty level and some states set them higher, so apply through your state Medicaid office even if you think you're slightly over. More on eligibility in our [Medicare Savings Programs guide](/medicare-savings-programs/).

**Employer or union reimbursement.** Some retiree plans reimburse part or all of the Part B premium. If yours does, keep the paperwork, since the reimbursement usually depends on staying enrolled.

## How you pay the premium

If you get Social Security or Railroad Retirement benefits, the premium comes out of your monthly payment automatically. If you're not drawing benefits yet, Medicare sends a bill, usually every three months. You can pay through your Medicare.gov account, by mail, or with Medicare Easy Pay, which takes it from your bank account each month. Automatic payment is the safer choice, because missing bills can end your coverage.

**The hold harmless rule.** If your premium is deducted from your Social Security check and you had Part B the previous year, your premium increase can't be larger than your Social Security cost-of-living increase. Your net check won't drop because of Part B. The protection doesn't apply if you pay IRMAA, are new to Part B, or have the premium paid by your state.
