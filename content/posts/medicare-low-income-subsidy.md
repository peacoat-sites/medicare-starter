Most people who qualify for the Medicare Low Income Subsidy never apply for it. That's not an exaggeration. The Social Security Administration estimates that roughly a third of eligible people aren't enrolled, leaving real money on the table every single month. I've spent two decades watching seniors pay $50, $80, even $140 a month for prescriptions they should be getting for next to nothing, simply because nobody told them this program existed.

Let me fix that.

## What the Low Income Subsidy Actually Is

The Low Income Subsidy (LIS) is a federal program that helps people with Medicare pay for their Part D prescription drug coverage. Part D is the part of Medicare that covers medications, and it comes with premiums, deductibles, and copays that can genuinely hurt on a fixed income. The LIS, sometimes called "Extra Help" (that's the name Social Security uses when you apply), steps in and covers most or all of those costs.

What most people don't realize is that this isn't a small discount. Full Extra Help can mean $0 premiums for a benchmark Part D plan, a $0 deductible, and copays as low as $1.60 for generic drugs and around $4.80 for brand-name drugs in 2026. Those numbers are adjusted each year, but the point is: we're talking about a near-complete elimination of your drug costs if you qualify for full benefits.

There are two levels of Extra Help currently. Full Extra Help covers the most and is available automatically to people who have Medicaid, get Supplemental Security Income (SSI), or are enrolled in a Medicare Savings Program (MSP). If you're in one of those programs, you already have Extra Help and may not even know it. Partial Extra Help is for people who are above those thresholds but still have limited income and resources.

Here's where people get tripped up. The income and resource limits feel confusing at first because they change every year and there are slightly different rules depending on your situation.

For 2026, the general income limits for full Extra Help sit around 135% of the federal poverty level, and partial Extra Help extends up to around 150%. For a single person, that's roughly $20,000 to $22,000 annually, give or take. For a married couple living together, the limits are higher. I always tell people: don't rule yourself out based on a number you heard from a neighbor. Run the actual calculation.

The resource limits matter too. Resources mean things like money in checking and savings accounts, stocks, and bonds. They don't count your home, one car, personal belongings, or life insurance policies under a certain value. In 2026, the resource limit for a single person is approximately $17,220, and for married couples it's around $34,360. Again, these shift annually, so check [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) for the current figures before you assume you're over the limit.

One thing I want to be direct about: many people assume that because they own their home or have a modest IRA, they won't qualify. That assumption has cost people thousands of dollars. The rules around what counts as a "countable resource" are specific and sometimes surprising. A prepaid burial fund, for instance, doesn't count. An IRA sometimes does, sometimes doesn't, depending on whether it's accessible. Get actual guidance before you give up.

## How to Apply

Applying is free, takes about 15 to 30 minutes, and you can do it multiple ways. Social Security handles the application, not Medicare directly, which confuses a lot of people.

Your options are: apply online at ssa.gov/extrahelp, call Social Security at 1-800-772-1213, or visit your local Social Security office in person. You can also apply through your State Medicaid office. If paperwork isn't your thing, your State Health Insurance Assistance Program, better known as [SHIP](https://www.shiphelp.org/), will sit with you and help you fill it out at no charge. I cannot recommend SHIP enough. These are trained counselors who do this every day and won't try to sell you anything.

What you'll need handy: information about your income (Social Security statements, pension letters, pay stubs if you're still working), a list of your assets (bank accounts, investment accounts), and your Medicare card. You don't need to know which Part D plan you're in when you apply.

Once you submit, Social Security typically responds within a few weeks. If you're approved, your Extra Help status gets sent to Medicare, and Medicare notifies your Part D plan. There can be a lag, so keep your receipts if you pay full price at the pharmacy during the transition. You can often get that money back.

## The Benchmark Plan Problem Nobody Warns You About

This is the part where I feel like I have to slow down, because I've seen this confuse people and cost them money.

If you get full Extra Help, your premium is covered up to a "benchmark" amount set by Medicare each year. Most states have several Part D plans that fall at or below that benchmark, meaning your premium is literally $0. But if your current plan's premium is above the benchmark, you'll pay the difference out of pocket even with Extra Help.

So if you're newly approved for Extra Help, or if you're coming up on the Annual Enrollment Period (October 15 through December 7 each year), it's worth reviewing whether your current plan is still benchmark-priced. Medicare changes which plans qualify each year. I've seen people stay in the same plan on autopilot and suddenly owe $25 a month when they could be paying nothing. That's $300 a year, gone, for no reason.

People with Extra Help also get a Special Enrollment Period (SEP) that lets them switch Part D plans once per quarter, from January through September. That flexibility is genuinely useful. Most people don't know they have it.

## If You Have a Medicare Advantage Plan

A lot of seniors are in Medicare Advantage (Part C) plans, which bundle your hospital, medical, and often drug coverage together. Extra Help works with Medicare Advantage plans that include Part D drug coverage (these are called MA-PD plans). The mechanics are a little different, but the basic benefit still applies: your drug costs get reduced significantly.

One nuance worth knowing: if your Medicare Advantage plan's premium for the drug portion exceeds the Extra Help benchmark, the same situation applies as above. Some MA-PD plans have $0 premiums for the drug portion, making this a non-issue, but check the details.

If you're trying to compare plans, Medicare's Plan Finder tool at Medicare.gov lets you enter your Extra Help status and see your estimated costs. It's genuinely one of the more useful government tools I've encountered, which is saying something.

## Don't Forget the Medicare Savings Programs

I want to mention this here because Extra Help and the Medicare Savings Programs (MSPs) are closely connected, and people often apply for one without knowing about the other.

MSPs are state-run programs that help cover Medicare's own out-of-pocket costs: Part B premiums (currently $185 per month in 2026 for most people), deductibles, and sometimes copays. There are four MSP levels, Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) being the most comprehensive. If you qualify for an MSP, you automatically get full Extra Help too.

Apply for both at the same time. Apply through your state Medicaid office. Many states have simplified the application process so you only need to fill out one form. Given that the Part B premium alone is $185 a month, qualifying for QMB is worth thousands of dollars a year. I wish more people talked about this connection.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does having a 401(k) or IRA disqualify me from Extra Help?

Not automatically. Whether retirement accounts count as a "resource" depends on whether you can currently access the funds. If you're required to take minimum distributions, the account's value may count partially. If the funds are inaccessible without penalty, the rules get more complicated. The honest answer is: it depends on your specific situation, and it's worth getting help from a SHIP counselor to run the numbers accurately.

### What if my income is over the limit but just barely?

There's a gray zone. If you're close to the income limits, apply anyway. Extra Help counts certain deductions (like earned income disregards for people still working) that can bring your countable income below the limit. A denial isn't permanent either. You can reapply if your circumstances change, and you can appeal a denial if you think the calculation was wrong.

### Can I get Extra Help if I'm already enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan?

Yes. If your Medicare Advantage plan includes Part D drug coverage (most do), Extra Help applies. Your drug copays drop significantly, and your plan can't charge you more than the Extra Help copay amounts for covered drugs. Call your plan after your Extra Help is approved to confirm everything is updated correctly.

### How do I know if I already have Extra Help?

Look at your Medicare card or any recent letter from Medicare or Social Security. If you have full Medicaid, SSI, or a Medicare Savings Program, you have automatic Extra Help. You can also call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) and ask directly. It takes about five minutes.

### Does Extra Help coverage renew automatically?

If you have automatic Extra Help (through Medicaid, SSI, or an MSP), it renews as long as you stay enrolled in those programs. If you applied directly through Social Security, you'll typically receive a review letter periodically, usually once a year, to confirm your situation hasn't changed significantly. Respond to those letters promptly. Missing one can interrupt your benefits.
