Imagine you're paying $60 or $70 a month in Medicare prescription drug premiums, plus copays that add up to hundreds of dollars every quarter, and you find out after the fact that you qualified for a program that could have reduced all of that to nearly zero. I've seen this happen more times than I can count. The Medicare Low Income Subsidy, also called Extra Help, is one of the most valuable and most overlooked benefits in the entire Medicare system. Every year, the Social Security Administration estimates that hundreds of thousands of eligible people aren't enrolled. That's not a rounding error. ## What Extra Help Actually Is (And Why the Name Confusion Matters)

Here's the first thing that trips people up: this benefit has two names used interchangeably, and depending on who you ask, you'll hear one or the other. The Social Security Administration (SSA) calls it the Low Income Subsidy, or LIS. Medicare itself tends to call it Extra Help. They are the same program. I'll be honest, this naming confusion causes people to miss information that applies directly to them, because they hear one name and assume the other program is something different.

Extra Help is a federal program designed to help people with limited income and resources pay for Medicare Part D costs. Part D is Medicare's prescription drug coverage, and without help, it can get expensive fast. We're talking monthly premiums, annual deductibles, and copays at the pharmacy counter every time you pick up a prescription. Extra Help can reduce or eliminate all three of those cost layers.

What surprised me when I first really dug into this program was how significant the assistance actually is. Full Extra Help can bring your monthly Part D premium to $0 (if you choose a benchmark plan), reduce or eliminate your deductible entirely, and cap your copays at a few dollars per prescription. For someone on multiple maintenance medications, the annual savings can be substantial. The program essentially restructures your cost-sharing so that prescription drugs don't force you into choosing between medication and food. That's not an exaggeration. It's why advocates push so hard to get word out about it.

Eligibility comes down to two measurements: your income and your resources. Both matter, and both have limits that are updated every year by the SSA, so I want to be careful not to quote figures that may shift. Always verify current thresholds at [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) or directly through the SSA.

Income limits are based on the Federal Poverty Level (FPL). Generally, you must have income at or below 150% of the FPL to qualify for some level of Extra Help. The exact percentage varies depending on whether you're looking at full subsidy versus partial subsidy, and whether you're single or married.

Resources are a separate calculation. Resources include things like bank accounts, stocks, and bonds, but not your home, one vehicle, personal belongings, or life insurance policies with no cash value. There are also exclusions for burial funds up to a certain amount. The resource limit is higher for married couples than for individuals.

A few groups are automatically enrolled in Extra Help and never have to apply:

- People who have full Medicaid coverage
- People who receive Supplemental Security Income (SSI), which is a federal benefit for people with low income and limited resources
- People enrolled in a Medicare Savings Program (MSP), which is a state program that helps cover Medicare premiums and sometimes more

If you fall into any of those three categories, you should receive a purple letter from Social Security confirming your Extra Help status. If you haven't received that letter and you believe you qualify automatically, call Social Security at 1-800-772-1213 to confirm.

## Full Versus Partial Extra Help: The Difference Is Real

Not everyone who qualifies gets the same level of assistance, and this is where people get confused and sometimes disappointed. There are two tiers.

**Full Extra Help** means your premiums, deductibles, and copays are all significantly reduced. If you choose a Part D plan whose premium falls at or below the regional benchmark (a threshold set each year by Medicare), you pay $0 in monthly premiums. Your deductible is eliminated. Your copays are capped at a very low per-prescription amount, with different caps for generic versus brand-name drugs.

**Partial Extra Help** means you receive meaningful assistance but still pay a share of costs. Your premium is subsidized rather than fully covered, you may pay a reduced deductible, and your copays are lower than standard but not as low as full Extra Help recipients pay.

Your subsidy level depends on where your income and resources fall within the eligibility ranges. The SSA makes this determination when you apply, and you can appeal if you believe the decision is wrong.

## How to Apply: A Step-by-Step Breakdown

Applying is simpler than most people expect, and there are multiple ways to do it.

**Option 1: Apply online through SSA**
1. Go to the SSA's official Extra Help application page at ssa.gov/medicare/prescriptionhelp
2. Complete the application, which takes most people about 15 to 20 minutes
3. You'll need information about your income (Social Security, pension, wages if applicable) and your resources (bank balances, investment accounts)
4. Submit the application electronically and you'll receive a confirmation number
5. SSA will mail you a decision letter, typically within a few weeks

**Option 2: Call SSA directly**
Call 1-800-772-1213 (TTY: 1-800-325-0778) and a representative will take your application over the phone.

**Option 3: Visit your local Social Security office**
You can apply in person. Bring documentation of your income, resources, and any current Medicare or insurance coverage you have.

**Option 4: Work with a SHIP counselor**
This is the option I recommend most for anyone who feels uncertain about the process. [The State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free, unbiased, one-on-one counseling from trained volunteers. SHIP counselors can walk you through the application, help you understand your subsidy determination letter, and even help you pick a Part D plan that maximizes your Extra Help benefit. There is no cost and no sales pitch.

One practical note: if you apply and are denied, you can appeal. The denial letter will explain the reason and your appeal rights. Many denials are reversed on appeal, especially when someone has assets that should have been excluded but were counted by mistake.

## What Happens After You're Approved

Getting approved is great news, but there are a few things to understand about what comes next.

Your Extra Help status is tied to your Part D enrollment. You still need to be enrolled in a Medicare Part D plan (either standalone or through a Medicare Advantage plan that includes drug coverage). If you have full Extra Help and your current plan's premium is above the benchmark, you'll pay the difference, which is why choosing the right plan matters. SHIP counselors or the Medicare Plan Finder at [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) can help you find plans in your area that work within your subsidy.

Extra Help is not permanent. The SSA redetermines your eligibility annually, usually through a form mailed to you each fall. If your income or resources have changed significantly, your subsidy level may change too. If you receive a redetermination form, fill it out and return it promptly. Missing the deadline can cause a gap in your benefit.

Automatic enrollees, meaning people on Medicaid, SSI, or Medicare Savings Programs, are redetermined automatically through their states. They typically don't have to file a separate form with SSA.

One more thing that surprises people: Extra Help has no open enrollment restriction. You can apply at any time during the year. If you're approved in July, your benefit typically begins the month after approval.

## Stacking Extra Help With Other Programs

Extra Help works best when combined with other available assistance programs, and this is an area where a lot of people leave even more money on the table.

Medicare Savings Programs, as I mentioned, can automatically qualify you for Extra Help, but they also help pay your Medicare Part B premium, which in 2024 is $174.70 per month for most enrollees. If you're income-eligible for a Medicare Savings Program but not enrolled, you could be paying that monthly premium unnecessarily.

The Low Income Subsidy and the Medicare Savings Programs are administered differently (SSA versus your state Medicaid office) but they're designed to work together. When you apply for one, the agency is supposed to check your eligibility for the other. In practice, that doesn't always happen seamlessly. Be proactive. Ask your state Medicaid office about Medicare Savings Programs if you're applying for Extra Help, and vice versa.

Some states also have State Pharmaceutical Assistance Programs (SPAPs) that layer additional drug cost help on top of Extra Help. These vary widely by state, so check with your state's aging services agency or a SHIP counselor to find out what's available where you live.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Extra Help affect my Social Security benefits or other income?

No. Receiving Extra Help does not count as income and does not reduce your Social Security, SSI, or any other federal benefit. It's a cost-sharing subsidy, not income, so there is no tax impact or benefit reduction to worry about.

### I own my home. Does that disqualify me?

No. Your primary home is excluded from the resource calculation entirely. The same goes for one vehicle, household goods, personal belongings, and certain burial funds. Many people assume home ownership disqualifies them, but it doesn't. The resource test focuses on liquid financial assets like bank accounts and investments.

### Can I get Extra Help if I have employer or union retiree drug coverage?

The research here is a bit mixed depending on how your retiree coverage is structured. If your retiree plan is considered "creditable" (meaning it's at least as good as standard Medicare Part D), you may not need a separate Part D plan. However, Extra Help eligibility is based on enrolling in Part D, so if you don't enroll in Part D, you can't use the Extra Help subsidy. Talk to a SHIP counselor before making changes to retiree coverage, because the decision can be difficult to undo.

### How will I know which Part D plans I can join for $0 premium with Extra Help?

Each year, Medicare calculates a regional benchmark premium for Part D plans. If a plan's premium falls at or below that benchmark, full Extra Help recipients pay $0. The benchmark varies by state. The Medicare Plan Finder tool at Medicare.gov lets you filter plans by your Extra Help status so you can see exactly which plans cost you nothing in premium.

### What if my income or resources go up during the year?

You're required to report significant changes to the SSA. If your income or resources increase enough to push you over the eligibility thresholds, your subsidy may end. However, changes typically take effect at your next annual redetermination rather than immediately, depending on the nature of the change. When in doubt, call SSA to ask how a specific change affects your status.
