Most people who qualify for the Medicare Low Income Subsidy never apply. That's not exaggeration. The Social Security Administration says roughly a third of eligible people aren't enrolled, which means they're throwing away real money every single month. I've watched seniors pay $50, $80, sometimes $140 a month for prescriptions they should be getting for almost nothing, all because nobody told them this program existed.

Let me fix that.

## What the Low Income Subsidy Actually Is

| Benefit Type | Premium | Deductible | Generic Copay | Brand-Name Copay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full Extra Help | $0 | $0 | $1.60 | ~$4.80 |
| Standard Part D (2026) | Varies | Varies | Varies | Varies |

The Low Income Subsidy (LIS) is a federal program that helps people with Medicare pay for Part D prescription drug coverage. [Part D covers medications](/best-medicare-part-d-plans-2026/), and it comes with premiums, deductibles, and copays that'll hurt on a fixed income. The LIS, sometimes called "Extra Help" (that's what Social Security calls it when you apply), covers most or all of those costs.

Here's what most people miss: this isn't a small discount. Full Extra Help means $0 premiums for a benchmark Part D plan, a $0 deductible, and copays as low as $1.60 for generic drugs and around $4.80 for brand-name drugs in 2026. Those numbers adjust every year, but we're talking about near-complete elimination of your drug costs if you qualify for full benefits.

Two levels of Extra Help exist right now. Full Extra Help is available automatically if you have Medicaid, get Supplemental Security Income (SSI), or are enrolled in a Medicare Savings Program (MSP). If you're in one of those programs, you might already have Extra Help and not even know it. Partial Extra Help goes to people above those thresholds but still with limited income and resources.

This is where people get confused. The income and resource limits shift every year and depend on your situation.

In 2026, full Extra Help covers people at around 135% of the federal poverty level. Partial Extra Help extends to about 150%. For a single person, that's roughly $20,000 to $22,000 annually. Married couples living together get higher limits. Don't rule yourself out based on something you heard from a neighbor. Run the actual numbers first.

Resources matter too. This means money in checking and savings accounts, stocks, bonds. Your home doesn't count. Neither does one car, personal belongings, or life insurance policies under a certain value. For a single person in 2026, the resource limit is approximately $17,220. Married couples get around $34,360. Check [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) for the current figures before you assume you're disqualified.

Here's what I need to be direct about: many people assume that owning their home or having a modest IRA will automatically disqualify them. That assumption has cost people thousands. The rules around countable resources are specific and sometimes surprising. A prepaid burial fund doesn't count. An IRA sometimes does, sometimes doesn't, depending on whether it's accessible. Get actual guidance before you give up.

## How to Apply

Applying is free and takes about 15 to 30 minutes. You've got multiple ways to do it. Social Security handles the application, not Medicare, which throws a lot of people off.

You can apply online at ssa.gov/extrahelp, call Social Security at 1-800-772-1213, or go to your local Social Security office. You can also apply through your State Medicaid office. If paperwork isn't your thing, your State Health Insurance Assistance Program, or [SHIP](https://www.shiphelp.org/), will help you fill it out at no cost. I can't recommend SHIP enough. These are trained counselors who do this every day and won't try to sell you anything.

What to have ready: information about your income (Social Security statements, pension letters, pay stubs if you're working), a list of your assets (bank accounts, investment accounts), and your Medicare card. You don't need to know which Part D plan you're in when you apply.

Social Security typically responds within a few weeks. Once approved, your Extra Help status goes to Medicare, and Medicare notifies your Part D plan. There's sometimes a lag, so keep receipts if you pay full price during the transition. You can usually get that money back.

## The Benchmark Plan Problem Nobody Warns You About

I need to slow down here. I've seen this confuse people and cost them real money.

If you get full Extra Help, your premium is covered up to a "benchmark" amount Medicare sets each year. Most states have several Part D plans at or below that benchmark, meaning your premium is literally $0. But if your current plan's premium is above the benchmark, you'll pay the difference out of pocket, Extra Help or not.

So when you're newly approved for Extra Help, or if Annual Enrollment Period is coming up (October 15 through December 7 each year), check whether your current plan is still benchmark-priced. Medicare changes which plans qualify every year. I've seen people stay on autopilot and suddenly owe $25 a month when they could be paying nothing. That's $300 a year gone for no reason.

People with Extra Help also get a [Special Enrollment Period](/how-to-choose-a-medicare-part-d-prescription-drug-plan/) (SEP) letting them switch Part D plans once per quarter, January through September. Most people don't know they have it.

## If You Have a Medicare Advantage Plan

Plenty of seniors are in Medicare Advantage (Part C) plans, which bundle hospital, medical, and often drug coverage together. Extra Help works with Medicare Advantage plans that include Part D drug coverage (these are MA-PD plans). The mechanics shift a little, but the basic benefit still applies: your drug costs get reduced significantly.

One thing to know: if your Medicare Advantage plan's premium for the drug portion exceeds the Extra Help benchmark, the same situation applies as above. Some MA-PD plans have $0 premiums for drugs, making this a non-issue. Check the details.

Medicare's Plan Finder tool at Medicare.gov lets you enter your Extra Help status and see estimated costs. It's genuinely one of the more useful government tools out there.

## Don't Forget the Medicare Savings Programs

Extra Help and the Medicare Savings Programs (MSPs) are closely connected, and people often apply for one without knowing about the other.

MSPs are state-run programs that cover Medicare's own costs: Part B premiums ($185 per month in 2026 for most people), deductibles, and sometimes copays. There are four MSP levels. Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) is the most comprehensive. If you qualify for an MSP, you automatically get full Extra Help too.

Apply for both at once through your state Medicaid office. Many states simplified the process so you only need one form. Given that the Part B premium alone is $185 a month, qualifying for QMB is worth thousands of dollars a year.
