Most people assume Medicare covers hearing aids the way it covers a hip replacement or a round of chemotherapy. It doesn't. Not even close.

Original Medicare, meaning Part A (hospital insurance) and Part B (medical insurance), explicitly excludes hearing aids and routine hearing exams from coverage. That's not a gap or an oversight. It's been written into statute since 1965. You can have a diagnosed, documented hearing loss that makes it dangerous to live alone, and Medicare's traditional coverage still won't pay a dime toward the device sitting in your audiologist's display case.

That's the uncomfortable truth that gets glossed over in a lot of Medicare explainers. So let's talk about what actually exists, what it costs, and where the real options are.

## What "No Coverage" Actually Means at the Counter

The average pair of hearing aids costs somewhere between $4,000 and $7,000 out of pocket, though you can spend north of $6,000 for premium Bluetooth-enabled devices from brands like Phonak or Oticon. Over-the-counter options, which the FDA cleared for adults with mild to moderate hearing loss in 2022, run considerably cheaper, sometimes $200 to $1,500 for a pair.

When a Medicare beneficiary with traditional Part A and Part B shows up at an audiology clinic, the bill is entirely their responsibility. There's no deductible that applies. No coinsurance kicks in. The EOB (Explanation of Benefits) will come back stamped "not a covered service," and no amount of appeals will change that outcome, because the denial isn't a billing error. It's policy.

Part B does cover diagnostic hearing exams, but only when a physician orders them to rule out a medical condition, not simply to measure hearing loss for the purpose of fitting a device. That distinction matters at billing time.

Medicare Advantage plans, sometimes called Part C, are the private insurance alternative to original Medicare. The federal government requires them to cover everything original Medicare covers, but it also allows them to offer extra benefits, and hearing coverage is one of the most common extras they advertise.

This is where things get genuinely complicated.

Not all hearing benefits are equal. Some Advantage plans offer a flat annual allowance, say $500 or $1,500, toward hearing aids from an approved network of providers. Others partner with third-party programs like TruHearing or HearUSA, which negotiate group pricing with manufacturers so members can buy a hearing aid at a discount rather than retail. A few high-end plans in competitive markets like Miami or Phoenix cover two devices per year with minimal cost-sharing.

A few things to check before you assume your Advantage plan is generous:

First, look at the network. If the plan's hearing benefit only applies to in-network providers, and the audiologist you've seen for years is out of network, you'll either pay full price or switch providers. Second, check whether the allowance resets annually or every two or three years. Most quality hearing aids last five to seven years, so a biennial benefit might actually align reasonably well with replacement cycles. Third, read what device tier the allowance covers. A $1,500 allowance that only applies to entry-level aids won't go far if your audiologist recommends a mid-range device for your specific type of loss.

I've talked to seniors who enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan specifically for the hearing benefit, only to discover at the fitting appointment that their model of loss required a level of technology not covered by the plan. That's a painful surprise at $3,000 a pair.

The [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) publishes plan-level benefits data, and Medicare.gov's plan finder tool lets you filter by extra benefits including hearing. Use it before you assume, not after you enroll.

## Medicare Supplement Plans (Medigap): Don't Expect Much Here

Medigap plans, which are sold by private insurers to cover the cost-sharing gaps in original Medicare, do not cover hearing aids either. They wrap around what Medicare covers. Since Medicare doesn't cover hearing aids, neither does Medigap. A Plan G or Plan N policy is excellent for reducing your hospital and outpatient cost exposure, but it will do nothing for your audiologist's bill.

This is a common misconception I've heard from readers who purchased comprehensive Medigap coverage expecting it to act as a catch-all. It isn't one.

## The OTC Option: Real, But Right for Fewer People Than the Marketing Suggests

The 2022 FDA rule creating an over-the-counter hearing aid category genuinely changed the landscape for some people. Brands like Jabra Enhance (which Consumer Reports has tested favorably), Sony CRE-10, and Lexie by Bose now sell directly to consumers without requiring a professional exam or fitting. Prices have dropped sharply.

For mild to moderate hearing loss in an otherwise healthy adult, OTC aids can work well. If you're straining to hear conversation in a restaurant or asking people to repeat themselves on the phone, an OTC device at $600 to $900 might do most of what a $4,500 prescription device would do.

But OTC aids are not appropriate for everyone. Severe or profound hearing loss, single-sided deafness, hearing loss associated with ear disease, and many cases involving tinnitus still require a licensed audiologist and prescription-grade technology. Fitting matters enormously. An incorrectly programmed or poorly fitted aid can actually make hearing worse, or cause discomfort significant enough that the device ends up in a drawer. (That's a more common outcome than the industry likes to admit, by the way. Studies suggest roughly a third of hearing aids go unworn.)

Medicare Part B will not reimburse OTC aids any more than it reimburses prescription ones. But the price difference makes the coverage gap sting less.

## Programs That Actually Help When Coverage Falls Short

A few concrete resources worth knowing:

The [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free counseling in every state. SHIP counselors can walk you through what your specific Advantage or Medigap plan covers and help you compare alternatives during Open Enrollment (October 15 to December 7 each year). If you haven't talked to a SHIP counselor before switching plans, you're missing a free resource that could save you thousands.

Some state Medicaid programs cover hearing aids for dual-eligible beneficiaries, meaning people who qualify for both Medicare and Medicaid. Benefits vary considerably by state. California's Medi-Cal covers hearing aids. Texas Medicaid covers them under certain circumstances. If you have limited income and assets, it's worth checking whether you qualify for a Medicare Savings Program, which could open Medicaid eligibility and with it, hearing coverage.

The Hearing Loss Association of America maintains a database of financial assistance programs, including manufacturer discount programs (most major brands offer them for low-income patients), state vocational rehabilitation funding, and charitable organizations that provide aids at low or no cost.

Veterans enrolled in VA health care can often receive hearing aids through the VA at little to no cost, which is a dramatically better benefit than anything Medicare offers.

## What Enrollment Period Matters If You Want to Switch Plans

If you're on original Medicare and want hearing coverage, you'll need a Medicare Advantage plan. You can switch during the Annual Enrollment Period (AEP) from October 15 to December 7, with coverage beginning January 1. If you're already on a Medicare Advantage plan and want to switch to a different one, you can also make one change between January 1 and March 31 during the Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period.

Missing these windows means waiting up to a year, so if hearing coverage is a priority, mark those dates.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Medicare cover hearing tests?

Medicare Part B covers a diagnostic hearing and balance exam only when your doctor orders it to investigate a medical condition, not for routine hearing screenings or hearing aid fittings. The exam itself may be covered (you'd pay the Part B coinsurance, typically 20% after your deductible), but any recommendation for a hearing aid that comes out of that appointment will not be covered.

### Can I get hearing aids through Medicare Advantage?

Many Medicare Advantage plans include some hearing aid benefit, but the specifics vary enormously by plan and location. Some offer a fixed dollar allowance toward approved devices; others provide access to discounted pricing through partner networks like TruHearing. Always verify the network, the allowance amount, and which device tiers are included before assuming the benefit will meet your actual needs.

### Are over-the-counter hearing aids covered by Medicare?

No. Medicare does not cover OTC hearing aids any more than it covers prescription hearing aids. The advantage of OTC options is that their lower price point, often $300 to $1,500 for a pair, reduces how much the coverage gap costs you. They're worth considering for mild to moderate hearing loss, but they're not a fit for everyone.

### What if I can't afford hearing aids at all?

Start with your state's SHIP counselor (find yours at shiphelp.org), who can identify plan options and assistance programs you may not know about. If you have limited income, check whether you qualify for your state's Medicaid program, since many state Medicaid programs do cover hearing aids. Manufacturer assistance programs, the Hearing Loss Association of America, and Lions Clubs International also provide devices at reduced or no cost to qualifying individuals.

### Will Medicare ever cover hearing aids?

Possibly. The Medicare Hearing, Vision, and Dental Improvement Act has been introduced in Congress multiple times, most recently in recent sessions, and would add hearing coverage to original Medicare. It hasn't passed as of this writing. Some broader health care reform proposals have included hearing coverage. Whether and when that changes is genuinely uncertain, and planning your coverage around legislation that hasn't yet passed is a bad bet.

The math here is unpleasant. Two hearing aids, realistically priced, can cost more than many people's annual Social Security income. The system as it stands puts a heavy burden on people who often have limited ability to absorb it. What you can do is know your actual options, compare Advantage plans carefully before you commit, and take advantage of every free counseling resource available. A SHIP counselor won't cost you a thing and might save you considerably more than that.
