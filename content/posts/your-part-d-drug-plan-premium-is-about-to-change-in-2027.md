Most people assume their Medicare drug plan premium is basically stable year to year. Maybe it nudges up a few dollars, maybe it holds steady. What's happening right now is different, and I'll be honest, it caught a lot of people I talk to completely off guard. A federal subsidy that's been quietly holding down the cost of standalone Medicare Part D drug plans is expiring on December 31, 2026, one full year ahead of schedule. If you're one of the roughly 25 million Americans enrolled in a standalone Part D plan, your premium is almost certainly going up in 2027. And the window to do something about it closes when [Medicare Open Enrollment](/medicare-open-enrollment-period/) ends on December 7, 2026.

The program being cut is called the Part D Premium Stabilization Demonstration. The Trump administration ended it early, pulling $9.8 billion in subsidies that insurance companies had been using to keep premiums lower than they otherwise would have been. Without that subsidy, the base beneficiary premium for Part D rises from $38.99 [per month](/how-much-does-medicare-cost-per-month/) in 2026 to $41.33 in 2027. That sounds modest. But here's what surprised me when I went deeper into the numbers: that base figure doesn't tell the whole story of what individual plan holders will actually pay.

A KFF analysis published July 29, 2026, found that approximately 45% of standalone Part D enrollees are looking at monthly premium increases of $11 to $20 for 2027. That's not pocket change for someone on a fixed income. CMS Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz has said most beneficiaries will pay less than $10 more per month and has called the subsidy a "bailout" for insurers. Families USA, an advocacy group, pushed back hard, calling the termination an "unnecessary blow to seniors." As NPR reported in July 2026, the disagreement over who actually benefits from the subsidy, insurers or patients, sits right at the center of this debate.

## Who Feels This Most, and Who Is Protected

Not everyone in Medicare is equally exposed here. The people most at risk are those in [Original Medicare](/medicare-advantage-vs-original-medicare/), meaning traditional fee-for-service Medicare, who have a separate standalone Part D plan. If that's you, your plan's premium is directly tied to what insurance companies charge, and that price has been subsidized. When the subsidy disappears, the full cost lands on you.

Medicare Advantage enrollees are in a different position. Most Medicare Advantage plans bundle drug coverage in, and those plans are structured differently. The subsidy's expiration doesn't hit Advantage enrollees the same way. People who receive Extra Help, also called the Low-Income Subsidy or LIS, are also largely insulated because the government caps how much they pay regardless.

What genuinely concerned me in the research is the rural picture. PBS NewsHour reported in August 2026 that nearly 6 in 10 Part D enrollees in the most rural counties were using standalone PDPs (prescription drug plans) in 2025. Why? Because Medicare Advantage plans are thin or nonexistent in many rural areas. Those folks don't have the option of switching to an Advantage plan to soften the blow. They're locked into the standalone market, which is exactly the market getting hit hardest.

## Why This Is Happening Now, and What the Debate Looks Like

The Premium Stabilization Demonstration was created under the Biden administration as a temporary measure to soften the premium increases that followed the Inflation Reduction Act's major Part D redesign. The Trump administration cut it a year early, arguing the money was flowing to insurers rather than directly benefiting patients. CMS Administrator Oz used the word "bailout" deliberately. His argument is that insurers had been pocketing the support without passing savings through to beneficiaries in meaningful ways.

Families USA and other patient advocates see it differently. Their position, covered in reporting by Yahoo News in August 2026, is that whatever the mechanism, the subsidy was keeping costs lower for real people, and removing it means those people pay more. The research is honestly a bit mixed on exactly how much of the subsidy flowed through to premiums versus insurer profits. But for someone sitting with a drug plan bill that goes up $15 or $18 a month in January, the policy debate probably feels pretty abstract.

## What You Can Actually Do Before December 7

Open Enrollment for Medicare runs October 15 through December 7, 2026. That deadline isn't flexible. Any plan changes you make take effect January 1, 2027. So right now, before that window closes, it makes sense to do a few things.

First, pull up your current plan's 2027 premium. Plans are required to send Annual Notice of Change letters, and those should have arrived or will arrive soon. The new premium will be listed there. Then go to Medicare.gov and use the Plan Finder tool to compare what else is available in your zip code for 2027. Put your actual medications in. The cheapest premium isn't always the best deal once you factor in copays and deductibles for your specific drugs.

Second, if you think you might qualify for Extra Help (income and resource limits apply), call 1-800-MEDICARE or visit socialsecurity.gov. Extra Help can dramatically reduce what you pay for drugs and premiums. Forbes noted in July 2026 that many people who qualify for Extra Help don't know it, which is a genuinely fixable problem.

Third, if you're in a rural area, ask a State Health Insurance Assistance Program counselor, called a SHIP counselor, what standalone options exist for your county. SHIP counseling is free and unbiased. Find your local counselor at shiphelp.org.

The expiration of this subsidy isn't a catastrophe for every single Part D enrollee, but it's a real cost increase for a lot of people who are already managing tight budgets. What surprised me most in researching this is how quietly it's happening. The window to respond is small. Talk to a Medicare counselor, check your plan, and don't assume last year's choice is still the right one.
