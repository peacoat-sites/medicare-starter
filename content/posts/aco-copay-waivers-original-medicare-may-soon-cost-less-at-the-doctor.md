Something quiet happened on November 4, 2026, and most Medicare beneficiaries haven't heard about it yet. Buried inside the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) final rule for the CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule is a provision that could genuinely change what you pay at the doctor's office, starting as soon as April 1, 2027. If your doctor belongs to an Accountable Care Organization (ACO, a group of doctors and hospitals that coordinate your care and share in Medicare savings), that ACO may soon have the power to reduce or even eliminate your Part B copays entirely.

Part B is the piece of Original [Medicare that covers](/what-does-medicare-cover/) outpatient care: doctor visits, lab tests, preventive services, outpatient surgeries, and more. Right now, the standard deal is that Medicare pays 80% and you pay 20%. No cap. That 20% adds up fast, and it is a common reason people consider Medicare Advantage (MA, the private managed-care alternative) over Original Medicare. MA plans often layer in lower copays at the point of care. This new ACO copay waiver rule is a direct answer to that. CMS is giving Original Medicare a tool to compete on cost without pushing beneficiaries into a private network.

According to the [CMS fact sheet on the CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule final rule](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/calendar-year-cy-2027-medicare-physician-fee-schedule-final-rule-cms-1848-f-medicare-shared-savings), ACOs in the Medicare Shared Savings Program (MSSP) can apply to CMS for approval to waive or reduce cost-sharing for most Part B services. Applications open in early 2027, waivers take effect in Q2 2027, and the broader benchmarking changes in the same rule are projected to generate $5.5 billion in net federal savings over 10 years.

## What an ACO Is, and Why This Matters

An Accountable Care Organization is a network of doctors, hospitals, and other providers who agree to work together and be held accountable for the quality and cost of your care. If the ACO keeps costs down while meeting quality targets, CMS shares a portion of the savings with the ACO. If costs run over, some ACOs owe money back.

You don't join an ACO the way you join an MA plan. CMS assigns you to one based on where you get most of your primary care. You keep all your Original Medicare rights, see any Medicare-accepting provider, and never need a referral. The ACO works in the background. That's what makes this copay waiver a big deal. You could get a lower bill at your doctor's office without changing anything about how you use Medicare.

## What the Waiver Covers, and What It Doesn't

The rule is specific about scope. ACOs can waive or reduce cost-sharing for most Original [Medicare Part](/medicare-part-c-explained/) B items and services. That includes office visits, preventive care, outpatient procedures, mental health services, and similar care. Three categories are excluded under the final rule: durable medical equipment (DME, things like wheelchairs and walkers), prosthetics and orthotics, and Part B drugs (medications administered in a clinical setting, like infusion therapies or chemotherapy).

| Category | Waiver Allowed? |
|---|---|
| Office visits (primary care, specialists) | Yes |
| Preventive services | Yes |
| Outpatient procedures | Yes |
| Mental health services | Yes |
| Lab and diagnostic tests | Yes |
| Durable medical equipment (DME) | No |
| Prosthetics and orthotics | No |
| Part B drugs (infusions, injections) | No |

The exclusions matter if you rely heavily on DME or infusion therapy. Your 20% coinsurance on those services stays in place, at least under this rule. For most people whose main costs are office visits and outpatient care, though, the potential savings are real.

## Why ACOs Can Afford This

You might wonder how an ACO absorbs waived copays without going broke. The answer is in the numbers. As reported by [BakerLaw in their analysis of the final rule](https://www.bakerlaw.com/insights/cms-releases-the-2027-medicare-physician-fee-schedule-final-rule/), ACOs that earn shared savings have a financial cushion to work with. In performance year 2024, 476 ACOs participated in the MSSP. Of those, 75% earned shared savings, totaling $4.1 billion across the program. That's a lot of ACOs with money to put toward member benefits like waived copays.

CMS designed the MSSP so that ACOs only get to keep savings after meeting quality benchmarks, meaning the program already self-selects for well-run organizations. An ACO choosing to offer copay waivers would essentially be reinvesting some of those shared savings directly into lower costs for the people it serves. It's a reasonable trade: lower your patients' out-of-pocket costs, attract more assigned beneficiaries, generate more savings, repeat.

## How This Changes the Original Medicare vs. Medicare Advantage Calculation

For years, one of the clearest advantages of MA over Original Medicare was predictable, lower costs at the point of care. MA plans set fixed copays, say $20 for a primary care visit, $50 for a specialist, while Original Medicare left you facing 20% of whatever the service cost. If you're seeing multiple specialists or getting frequent outpatient services, that 20% adds up to real money.

This new waiver option narrows that gap considerably. If your ACO gets approved and chooses to waive your Part B copay for office visits, your out-of-pocket cost at the doctor could drop to zero. Original Medicare, without the [network restrictions](/medicare-advantage-network-restrictions/) of MA. That's a genuinely different value proposition than existed even six months ago.

That said, MA plans still offer benefits Original Medicare doesn't, like dental, vision, and hearing coverage. And not every ACO will apply for the waiver right away. Some may wait to see how early adopters manage the financial impact. The honest answer heading into 2027 is that your experience will depend heavily on who your ACO is, whether they apply, and what level of waiver they offer. If you want to get a clearer picture of your overall Medicare cost exposure right now, a tool like the [Medicare Cost Estimator](/medicare-cost-estimator/) can help you map out premiums, deductibles, and gaps across your current coverage.

## What to Do Right Now

You can't apply for this benefit yourself. The process runs through ACOs, not individual beneficiaries. But there are practical things you can do before April 2027.

First, find out whether you're assigned to an MSSP ACO. Your doctor's office can tell you, or you can check through your Medicare account at Medicare.gov. Second, ask your doctor's office whether their ACO is planning to apply for the copay waiver. Early 2027 is when ACO applications open, so practices affiliated with an ACO should have some sense of their plans by January or February. Third, don't assume the waiver applies to everything. Even if your ACO gets approved, remember the DME and Part B drug exclusions.

If you're currently weighing Original Medicare against a Medicare Advantage plan for 2027, this rule is worth factoring in, though it's still uncertain which ACOs will participate and to what degree. Talking with a licensed Medicare counselor or a State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP, a free, unbiased counseling service available in every state) advisor is a smart step before making any coverage decisions.

The broader picture here is that CMS is actively trying to make Original Medicare more financially competitive at the point of care. Whether that plays out the way the rule envisions depends on ACO uptake, beneficiary awareness, and how smoothly the approval process runs in early 2027. It's worth watching closely.
