Most people who qualify for [Medicare Savings Programs](/medicare-savings-programs/) never apply. That's not a guess. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services has acknowledged for years that enrollment in these programs sits well below eligibility, with millions of people paying premiums and cost-sharing they legally don't have to pay. The programs exist. The money is real. The paperwork is manageable. And yet.

Let me tell you what I see in my work. Someone calls me, they've been on Medicare for three or four years, they're paying $185 a month for their Part B premium, they're skipping doctor visits because the copays sting, and nobody, not their doctor's office, not their insurance agent, not their county social services office, ever mentioned that they might qualify for a program that covers that premium entirely. That call makes me furious every time.

This article is about fixing that gap for you specifically.

## What Medicare Savings Programs Actually Are

MSP is the umbrella term for four distinct programs, all federally authorized but administered by your state. They're funded jointly by federal and state dollars, which is why the income and asset limits vary slightly depending on where you live. The federal government sets a floor; states can be more generous if they choose, and some are.

The four programs, from most to least generous, are:

| Program | Official Name | What It Covers | 2026 Monthly Income Limit (Single) | 2026 Monthly Income Limit (Couple) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| QMB | Qualified Medicare Beneficiary | Part A and B premiums, deductibles, copays, coinsurance | ~$1,255 | ~$1,704 |
| SLMB | Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary | Part B premium only | ~$1,478 | ~$2,003 |
| QI | Qualifying Individual | Part B premium only (federal funding only, limited slots) | ~$1,660 | ~$2,239 |
| QDWI | Qualified Disabled and Working Individuals | Part A premium only | ~$4,545 | ~$6,132 |

A note on those numbers: they're benchmarks tied to the federal poverty level and updated annually. As of July 2026, the figures above reflect current federal guidelines, but your state may use slightly higher thresholds. Always verify with your state Medicaid office or call the State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) at 1-800-Medicare to get a local counselor who knows your state's exact rules.

Asset limits also apply, though they've been loosened considerably in recent years. Many states now exclude your home, one vehicle, and retirement accounts entirely. In my experience, the asset test eliminates far fewer people than they expect when they hear "asset limit" and assume they don't qualify.

## The QMB Program Deserves Special Attention

If you qualify for QMB, providers are prohibited by federal law from billing you for Medicare cost-sharing. Not discouraged. Prohibited. A doctor's office cannot legally charge you a copay if you're a QMB beneficiary, even if your [Medicare Advantage plan](/medicare-advantage-plan-exits-are-accelerating-into-2027/)'s EOB (Explanation of Benefits) shows a cost-sharing amount. This is one of the most routinely violated rules in all of Medicare administration.

I've had readers email me after being billed $40 copays at urgent care, and they're QMB. They paid because nobody told them not to. You're allowed to dispute those charges and get your money back. If a provider insists, file a complaint through Medicare.gov or call 1-800-MEDICARE.

The practical upside of QMB is massive. Your Part B premium alone is $185.00 [per month](/average-medicare-costs-per-month/) in 2026. That's $2,220 a year. Add a Part A deductible of $1,676 per benefit period, and you can see how QMB translates to thousands of dollars in annual savings for someone who uses Medicare regularly.

Here's a worked example from a case I helped with last spring:

Margaret, 72, single, lives in Ohio, income from Social Security of $1,181/month, has a small savings account at about $6,800 → Applied for QMB at her county Job and Family Services office in April 2026 → Qualified. Her Part B premium of $185/month stopped being deducted from her Social Security check starting in June. Her copays at her primary care doctor and her cardiologist dropped to zero. Estimated annual savings: over $3,400.

She had been on Medicare for four years.

## SLMB and QI: The Overlooked Middle Ground

Most of the coverage on MSPs focuses on QMB because it's the most dramatic. But SLMB and QI serve a lot of people, and both programs do something simple and valuable: they pay your Part B premium.

If you're a single person earning around $1,300 to $1,660 a month, you're almost certainly above the QMB threshold, but you might land squarely in SLMB or QI territory. The QI program is slightly more generous on income limits, but it's funded only through federal discretionary dollars, which means states can have waiting lists or stop accepting new enrollees mid-year. SLMB doesn't have that limitation.

One thing most guides won't tell you: the QI program requires you to reapply every single year, and your eligibility doesn't carry over automatically the way QMB or SLMB can. I've seen people lose their QI coverage because they assumed the renewal was handled. It's not. Set a calendar reminder for October of each year.

Another worked example:

Gerald and his wife Ruth, both 69, living in Arizona, combined Social Security income of $3,100/month → Called SHIP Arizona in February 2026 after seeing an ad, expected to be told they earned too much → Qualified for SLMB as a couple. Both Part B premiums covered, saving them $370/month, or $4,440 a year → They'd been paying out of pocket since 2022.

## How to Actually Apply (Without Getting Tangled Up)

You apply at your state Medicaid office. That might be called the Department of Social Services, the Division of Medical Assistance, the Department of Human Services, or something else entirely depending on your state. Medicare.gov has a state-by-state contact finder that'll get you to the right place fast.

What you'll typically need to bring or submit:

- Proof of Medicare enrollment (your red, white, and blue Medicare card)
- Proof of income (Social Security award letter, any pension statements, recent bank statements)
- Proof of residency (utility bill, lease, or driver's license with current address)
- Social Security number

The interview, if your state requires one, is not an interrogation. The caseworker asks you to confirm the income and asset information you submitted. In my experience, the whole appointment takes about 25 minutes. They often ask whether you have any life insurance policies with a cash value over a certain amount, which surprises people. Most term life policies don't count. Whole life with significant cash value might.

If you're denied, appeal. I mean that. Denial rates on initial applications run higher than they should, partly due to caseworker error and partly due to applicants not submitting all documents in one packet. The State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) offers free help at every stage of this process, including appeals, and their counselors are specifically trained in MSPs. Find your local SHIP counselor at shiphelp.org.

Processing time varies. Some states get you an answer in two weeks. Others take 45 days. If approved, benefits are often retroactive to your application date, so there's no benefit to waiting.

## The Extra Help Connection

Qualifying for QMB, SLMB, or QI automatically qualifies you for Extra Help, the federal program that caps prescription drug costs under Part D. In 2026, full Extra Help means your drug copays are capped at $4.90 for generics and $12.15 for brand-name drugs, with no coverage gap (the old "donut hole"). For someone on multiple medications, that alone can be worth $2,000 or more annually.

You don't have to apply for Extra Help separately if you're enrolled in an MSP. Social Security is notified automatically. It's one of the cleaner handoffs in the Medicare system, which, as anyone who's dealt with Medicare billing knows, doesn't always communicate internally all that well.
