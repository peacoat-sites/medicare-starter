Sixty-five arrives whether you're ready to retire or not. And if you're still working, still covered by your employer's health plan, and suddenly getting Medicare signup reminders in the mail, you've probably got a nagging feeling that you're supposed to do *something* but you're not quite sure what. I've seen that confusion turn into very expensive mistakes, and I'd rather you not make them.

Let me be direct: the rules around Medicare and active employment coverage are genuinely complicated, and most of the generic advice floating around online doesn't account for the details that actually matter to your situation. So let's slow down and work through this together.

## You Have More Time Than You Think (Probably)

Here's the thing most people panic about: the standard Medicare enrollment window is the 7-month Initial Enrollment Period (IEP) centered on your 65th birthday. Miss it, the story goes, and you'll face permanent late-enrollment penalties. That's true in some situations. But if you're still working and covered by a qualifying employer health plan, the rules are different, and often much more forgiving.

If you work for a company with 20 or more employees and you're enrolled in that company's group health plan, your employer plan is your "primary" insurance. Medicare would be secondary. In that scenario, you can actually delay enrolling in Part B (which covers doctor visits and outpatient care) without any penalty at all. You get what's called a Special Enrollment Period (SEP) that lasts for 8 months after you stop working or after your employer coverage ends, whichever comes first.

Part A is a different story. Part A covers hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, and some home health services. If you've worked at least 10 years (40 quarters) paying Medicare taxes, Part A is premium-free. Most people in this situation sign up for Part A at 65 even if they're still working, because it costs nothing and could help cover a hospital stay if your employer plan doesn't cover 100%. There's a meaningful exception I'll get to in a moment, but generally: sign up for Part A, hold off on Part B if you're still actively covered through a large employer.

If your company has fewer than 20 employees, the math flips completely. Medicare becomes primary even if you're still working, which means your small employer plan is basically supplemental. In that case, you really do need to enroll in both Part A and Part B at 65, or you'll be leaving your primary coverage on the table. Some small employers won't even tell you this. I've talked to people who found out the hard way, after a claim was denied, that their small-group plan had been paying as if Medicare was primary for months.

This is the one I wish someone had told me about years ago, and it catches a lot of high earners off guard.

If you or your employer contributes to an HSA (Health Savings Account) on your behalf, enrolling in any part of Medicare, including Part A, makes you ineligible to keep contributing to that account. The moment you're enrolled in Medicare, contributions to your HSA must stop or you'll owe taxes and a penalty on the excess.

What trips people up is the "look-back" rule. When you finally do enroll in Part A after delaying, Medicare can make your Part A coverage retroactive for up to 6 months. So if you enrolled in Part A in October and contributions were still going into your HSA in May, you may owe taxes on those contributions even though you thought you were fine at the time.

The practical fix: if you want to keep contributing to your HSA past 65, you need to delay *all* parts of Medicare, including Part A, and stop HSA contributions at least 6 months before you plan to enroll in Part A. Yes, that means giving up those tax-advantaged contributions a little early. It's still often worth it if you've got a high-deductible plan and a solid HSA balance building up, but you need to run those numbers intentionally, not just drift into it.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services has detailed guidance on this at [cms.gov](https://www.cms.gov/), but honestly the IRS publication on HSAs is where the enrollment timing rules are really spelled out. If your accountant hasn't flagged this for you, bring it up yourself.

## Part D: The Prescription Drug Coverage Question

Part D is Medicare's prescription drug coverage. Unlike Part B, the late enrollment penalty for Part D is based on how long you went without "creditable" drug coverage, meaning coverage from another source that's at least as good as the standard Part D benefit. Most large employer plans qualify as creditable, which means you can safely delay Part D enrollment while you're still working and covered.

When you leave your employer and enroll in Part D, you'll want a letter from your employer confirming your drug coverage was creditable for the period you were enrolled. Keep that letter. Medicare may ask for it, and without it you could face penalties that stick with you permanently.

Right now, Part D premiums vary significantly by plan and region. You can use Medicare's plan comparison tool at Medicare.gov to see exactly what plans are available in your zip code and compare drug formularies against your actual medication list. Don't assume the cheapest premium is the best deal; do the math on your specific prescriptions.

## Making the Decision: What Actually Matters

Here's where I'll give you an honest opinion: this is not a decision to make based on a generic checklist. It depends on four things specific to your situation.

First, your employer's size. Twenty employees is the dividing line. Know which side you're on.

Second, the quality of your employer coverage. If your group plan has a $6,000 deductible and mediocre network, Part B at $185 a month (the standard 2026 premium) might actually be worth adding as a secondary payer to reduce your out-of-pocket costs. Run an actual cost comparison before assuming your employer plan is the better deal just because it's "free."

Third, whether you have an HSA and how much it matters to you. The tax savings on HSA contributions can be substantial, especially if you're in a high tax bracket. That's worth delaying Medicare enrollment over in some cases.

Fourth, your health situation. If you're managing a chronic condition or anticipate significant healthcare use, having Medicare as a secondary payer could genuinely save you money even if you're technically not required to enroll yet.

AARP's Medicare resource center at [aarp.org](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/) has a good employer coverage coordination tool that walks through some of these scenarios, and it's free to use.

One thing I'd push back on: the idea that you should always delay Medicare if you can. That's often true, but not always. I've seen people carrying employer coverage with high deductibles and limited networks who would have been better served by enrolling in Part B and letting Medicare pick up what the employer plan left behind. "You can wait" and "you should wait" are two different things.

## When You Finally Do Stop Working

The 8-month Special Enrollment Period starts the day your employment ends or your employer coverage ends, whichever happens first. Not the day you *request* Medicare enrollment, the day coverage ends. This matters because you want to time your Medicare enrollment so your new coverage starts without a gap.

A common mistake: people assume the SEP starts when they retire, so they think they have 8 months to figure it out. But then they realize Medicare Part B coverage doesn't start immediately after you apply; there's a processing lag. Apply a few months before your employer coverage ends if you can. If you're aiming for a January 1 Part B start date, you generally need to apply by October 1 of the prior year.

Also, COBRA is not creditable coverage for purposes of avoiding the Medicare late enrollment penalty. If you leave work, turn down Part B, and take COBRA instead, the clock on penalty-free enrollment doesn't pause. That's one of the most expensive misconceptions I come across. COBRA can run $700 or $800 a month for a single person and it doesn't protect you from Medicare penalties. It's almost never the right bridge strategy.
