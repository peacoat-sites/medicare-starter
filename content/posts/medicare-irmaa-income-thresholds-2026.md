Most people discover IRMAA the worst possible way: they get their Medicare premium letter in the fall, see a number that's $100 or $200 higher than they expected, and spend the next week convinced Social Security made a clerical error. They didn't. That's IRMAA doing exactly what it was designed to do, and the surprise is almost always avoidable.

IRMAA stands for Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount. It's the surcharge that higher-income Medicare beneficiaries pay on top of the standard Part B and Part D premiums. After two decades of walking seniors through their Medicare options, I still think IRMAA is one of the most misunderstood pieces of the whole program. Not because it's complicated in theory, but because the income year it's based on trips people up every single time.

## How IRMAA Actually Works (And the Part Everyone Gets Wrong)

Here's what catches almost everyone off guard: Medicare doesn't look at what you earned this year to decide your surcharge. It reaches back two years. Your 2026 IRMAA surcharge is based on your 2024 income, specifically your Modified Adjusted Gross Income, or MAGI. MAGI for Medicare purposes is your Adjusted Gross Income plus any tax-exempt interest income you received. That's the number on line 11 of your Form 1040, plus line 2a.

What surprised me, even after seeing it hundreds of times, is how often this catches retirees who sold a home, took a large IRA distribution, or converted funds to a Roth IRA in 2024. They're past that financial event, they've moved on, and then IRMAA shows up in 2026 like an uninvited houseguest reminding them of it. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (https://www.cms.gov/) uses your tax return data directly from the IRS, so there's no paperwork you submit yourself. The process is automatic, which is convenient until the number isn't what you want to see.

The Social Security Administration sends out the IRMAA determination letters, typically in November or December before the coverage year begins. If you're already on Medicare and your income crossed a threshold, you'll get a notice. If you're newly enrolling, it'll be factored into your first premium bill.

## The 2026 IRMAA Thresholds, Spelled Out

Here's what the brackets actually look like for 2026. I want to be upfront: these figures reflect the thresholds as adjusted for inflation and released by CMS. If your situation is at or near a boundary, please verify the exact current numbers at Medicare.gov, since small adjustments do happen.

For **Part B**, the standard premium sits at $185.00 per month in 2026 for people below the IRMAA threshold. Once your income crosses the first tier, that number goes up. The brackets for individuals (with married filing jointly thresholds in parentheses) work like this:

**Tier 1:** MAGI up to $106,000 ($212,000 for joint filers). No IRMAA surcharge. You pay the standard $185.00 per month.

**Tier 2:** MAGI between $106,001 and $133,000 ($212,001 to $266,000 joint). Your Part B premium jumps to approximately $259.00 per month.

**Tier 3:** MAGI between $133,001 and $167,000 ($266,001 to $334,000 joint). Premium moves to approximately $370.00 per month.

**Tier 4:** MAGI between $167,001 and $200,000 ($334,001 to $400,000 joint). Premium reaches approximately $480.90 per month.

**Tier 5:** MAGI between $200,001 and $500,000 ($400,001 to $750,000 joint). Premium climbs to approximately $591.90 per month.

**Tier 6:** MAGI above $500,000 (above $750,000 joint). Premium hits approximately $628.90 per month.

The Part D IRMAA surcharge works differently. It's added on top of whatever your plan's premium already is, so the total impact depends on which plan you have. The 2026 Part D surcharges range from roughly $13.70 per month at Tier 2 all the way up to approximately $85.80 per month at Tier 6. Those are added directly to your plan premium, and Social Security collects them separately from your plan's normal billing.

One thing I always flag for people who are married but filing separately: the IRS and Medicare treat you differently from joint filers in a way that is genuinely punishing. If you're married filing separately with MAGI above $106,000, you move straight to Tier 5 rates. There's no gradual climb through the lower tiers. It's a design quirk that occasionally surprises couples who file separately for legitimate tax planning reasons.

## Why Bracket Cliffs Matter More Than People Realize

I've sat across from people who were absolutely devastated to learn that earning $1,000 over an IRMAA threshold could cost them more than $900 in additional annual premiums. That's not an exaggeration. At some tier transitions, crossing the line by a dollar puts you into a category that costs significantly more per month. This is what tax professionals call a "cliff," and it's one of the few places in personal finance where the math is that stark.

This is why year-end income planning matters so much for Medicare-age clients. Strategies like delaying a Roth conversion, managing capital gains realizations, or being thoughtful about the timing of IRA withdrawals can keep someone below a threshold. I'm not a financial advisor and I'm not prescribing anything here, but I'd strongly encourage anyone approaching these thresholds to talk with a CPA or a fee-only financial planner before December 31st of the year in question.

What surprised me when I started looking more closely at this is how many people also overlook the interaction between IRMAA and the sale of a primary residence. Normally, you can exclude up to $250,000 in gain ($500,000 for married couples) from taxable income when selling a home you've lived in for at least two of the last five years. But even excluded gain doesn't disappear from your gross income picture in every scenario, and any gain beyond the exclusion absolutely shows up in MAGI. Someone who sells a vacation property or a rental can get blindsided badly.

## You Can Appeal, and It's Worth Knowing How

Here's something a lot of people don't realize: IRMAA isn't necessarily permanent, and you can appeal if your income has dropped since the year Medicare is looking at. The formal term is a "Life-Changing Event" appeal, and the IRS has a specific list of qualifying events: marriage, divorce, death of a spouse, loss of income-producing property, work stoppage, work reduction, or loss of pension income.

The appeal is filed using SSA Form SSA-44. You submit it to your local Social Security office along with documentation of the qualifying event and proof of your more recent, lower income. If approved, Social Security will use a more recent year's income to set your premium rather than the standard two-year lookback.

I want to be direct about one thing: "my income dropped because I retired" does qualify, as long as you can document it. A lot of people assume it doesn't, because retirement feels like a choice rather than a "change." It does qualify. I've seen this appeal succeed many times for people in their first year or two of retirement who had high W-2 income in 2024 but are now living on Social Security and modest withdrawals.

If you want help with any of this, your state's State Health Insurance Assistance Program (https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free one-on-one counseling from trained volunteers. They can't file the appeal for you, but they can walk you through the form and what to expect.

## The Bigger Picture: IRMAA and Retirement Income Planning

The research here is genuinely mixed on how much impact IRMAA has on retirement income strategies overall, because it depends so heavily on individual circumstances. But here's what I'll say clearly: for people with income hovering near a threshold, the dollar impact is real enough that it deserves attention, not as an afterthought, but as part of actual planning conversations.

Some people deliberately manage income to stay under $106,000 individually. Others decide the tax benefits of a Roth conversion outweigh a temporary IRMAA bump and pay the surcharge for one or two years knowingly. Neither approach is wrong. But the worst outcome is what I see most: someone triggers a threshold accidentally, doesn't understand why their premium jumped, and then doesn't know they might be able to appeal or adjust course going forward.

If you're enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan (Part C) rather than Original Medicare, you still pay the Part B premium, which includes any IRMAA surcharge. Some people assume that switching to Medicare Advantage makes IRMAA go away. It doesn't.

If you're anywhere near an IRMAA threshold, pull your 2024 tax return and do the math before the premium letter arrives. Don't let November's mail be the first time you find out.
