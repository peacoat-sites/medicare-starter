Imagine you've just turned 65, you've signed up for Medicare, and you're feeling pretty good about your coverage. Then your dentist mentions you need a crown, your eye doctor recommends new glasses, and your doctor says you'd benefit from three months of physical therapy at a skilled nursing facility. You pull out your Medicare card, confident it'll handle things. It won't. Not most of it, anyway. This is the moment millions of seniors discover the hard way that Medicare, as generous as it is, leaves some surprisingly large gaps in coverage. Knowing those gaps before you need care is the difference between a manageable bill and a financial shock.

## The Big Four: Dental, Vision, Hearing, and Long-Term Care

Let's start with the coverage gaps that catch the most people off guard, because they involve everyday health needs that almost every senior will face.

**Dental care** is almost entirely excluded from Original Medicare (Part A and Part B). Medicare won't pay for routine cleanings, fillings, extractions, crowns, bridges, dentures, or implants. The only exception is dental work that's directly tied to a covered medical procedure, like tooth extraction before certain jaw surgeries. Given that a single dental implant can run well over $3,000, and full dentures often cost $1,500 or more per plate, this is a gap worth taking seriously.

**Vision care** follows a similar pattern. Medicare doesn't cover routine eye exams for glasses or contact lenses, and it won't pay for the glasses or contacts themselves either. There is one important exception: if you have a medical eye condition like cataracts, glaucoma, or macular degeneration, Medicare Part B will cover the treatment. After cataract surgery, Medicare will even cover one pair of standard eyeglasses or contact lenses. But if you just need your annual eye exam and a new prescription, you're paying out of pocket.

**Hearing aids and exams** are another big gap. Medicare does not cover hearing aids or the fitting exams for them. Hearing aids can cost anywhere from $1,000 to $6,000 or more per pair, and they typically need replacement every few years. Routine hearing exams aren't covered either, unless your doctor orders one as part of diagnosing a medical problem.

**Long-term care** is where the stakes get truly high. Medicare covers short-term skilled nursing facility care under specific conditions (more on that in a moment), but it does not cover custodial care. Custodial care means help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, eating, and moving around. That's exactly what most people in nursing homes or assisted living facilities need. Medicaid covers long-term custodial care for people who qualify financially, but Medicare simply doesn't. According to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services at [CMS.gov](https://www.cms.gov/), this distinction between skilled care and custodial care is one of the most misunderstood aspects of the entire program.

People often assume Medicare covers nursing home stays broadly. The reality is much narrower.

Medicare Part A will help pay for care in a skilled nursing facility (SNF) only if all of these conditions are met: you had a qualifying hospital stay of at least three consecutive days (not counting the discharge day), you're admitted to the SNF within 30 days of that hospital stay, and you need skilled care like physical therapy, occupational therapy, or IV medication management.

Even then, the coverage has strict limits. Days 1 through 20 are covered fully, meaning no copay from you. Days 21 through 100 require a significant daily copay (the amount adjusts annually, so check [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) for the current figure). After day 100, Medicare pays nothing. The full cost of a nursing home falls entirely on you or your other coverage.

And here's the part that surprises people the most: the moment you stop needing "skilled" care and only need help with daily activities, Medicare stops paying, even if you're still in the facility.

## Prescription Drugs, Over-the-Counter Items, and Routine Care

Original Medicare (Part A and Part B) does not cover most prescription drugs taken at home. That's what Medicare Part D is for. If you don't enroll in a Part D plan when you're first eligible and you don't have other creditable drug coverage (like from an employer plan), you'll face a late enrollment penalty that lasts the rest of your life on Medicare. This is one of the most costly mistakes I've seen people make.

Over-the-counter medications, vitamins, and supplements aren't covered at all, even if your doctor recommends them.

Routine physical exams beyond the "Welcome to Medicare" preventive visit and the annual wellness visit aren't covered the way many people expect, either. Those wellness visits focus on preventive screenings and creating a health plan. They're not the same as a full physical examination where your doctor diagnoses and treats specific complaints. If your doctor does significant problem-focused work during what you thought was your wellness visit, Part B may apply a copay or coinsurance to that portion.

Routine foot care is largely not covered. Trimming toenails, removing corns and calluses, and treating flat feet fall outside Medicare's scope. There are exceptions for people with diabetes or certain circulatory conditions, where foot exams become medically necessary.

Acupuncture is a somewhat newer story. Medicare does cover up to 12 acupuncture sessions per year for chronic low back pain under specific guidelines. But acupuncture for other conditions is not covered.

Chiropractic care is partially covered. Medicare Part B pays for manual spinal manipulation by a chiropractor, but it won't pay for X-rays, massage, or any other services the chiropractor performs.

## Overseas Travel and Cosmetic Procedures

If you travel internationally, this matters a lot. Original Medicare generally does not cover health care you receive outside the United States. There are very narrow exceptions for emergencies near the Canadian or Mexican border, or on a ship within U.S. territorial waters. But a medical emergency in Italy, Japan, or anywhere else abroad? Medicare won't touch it.

Some Medicare Supplement Insurance plans (called Medigap plans) offer a foreign travel emergency benefit. If you're a frequent traveler, this is one feature worth comparing carefully when shopping for a Medigap plan. You can review and compare Medigap options through [Medicare.gov's plan finder tool](https://www.medicare.gov/find-a-plan/questions/home.aspx).

Cosmetic surgery is not covered, with one exception: if surgery is needed to repair an injury or to correct a part of the body that's malformed from disease. Purely elective procedures like facelifts, liposuction, or hair transplants are entirely out of pocket.

## Your Options for Filling the Gaps

Knowing what Medicare doesn't cover is only half the battle. The other half is understanding how to protect yourself.

Here's a practical overview of your main options:

**Option 1: Medicare Advantage (Part C)**
Medicare Advantage plans are offered by private insurers and must cover everything Original Medicare covers. Many of them, however, add extra benefits that Original Medicare doesn't offer, including routine dental, vision, and hearing coverage. The scope and quality of those extras vary widely by plan and by location. Always read the plan's Summary of Benefits carefully before enrolling.

**Option 2: Medigap (Medicare Supplement Insurance)**
Medigap plans cover costs that Original Medicare doesn't pay, like copays, coinsurance, and deductibles. They don't typically add dental, vision, or hearing benefits. But they can dramatically reduce your out-of-pocket costs for covered services and may include that foreign travel emergency benefit.

**Option 3: Standalone Dental, Vision, and Hearing Plans**
Private insurers sell standalone plans for each of these. They vary significantly in what they cover and what they cost. Read the fine print on annual maximums and waiting periods before you buy.

**Option 4: Long-Term Care Insurance**
If long-term custodial care is your biggest worry, long-term care insurance (or hybrid life insurance policies with LTC riders) can help. These policies are best purchased before your 60s, as premiums rise steeply with age and health issues can disqualify you. A financial advisor with LTC expertise can help you evaluate whether a policy makes sense for your situation. **Step-by-step: How to audit your own coverage gaps**

1. Make a list of your current health needs: prescriptions, specialists you see, any expected procedures.
2. Check each item against what Medicare Part A and Part B actually cover. The Medicare.gov coverage database is searchable and easy to use.
3. Identify which needs fall into the gap categories above.
4. Compare Medicare Advantage plans in your area using the Medicare Plan Finder at Medicare.gov to see which extras are offered.
5. If you prefer Original Medicare, get quotes on Medigap and standalone dental/vision/hearing plans.
6. Speak with a licensed insurance counselor or a State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) counselor. SHIP counselors provide free, unbiased help. Find yours at shiphelp.org.
