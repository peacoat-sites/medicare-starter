Most people assume [Medicare enrollment](/medicare-enrollment-at-65/) is simple. You turn 65, you sign up. Done. I thought that too, until I spent a week helping my neighbor Carol sort out a penalty she'd been paying for three years because nobody told her the rules had a sharp edge hidden in the fine print.

The [Medicare Initial Enrollment](/medicare-initial-enrollment-period-when-to-sign-up-at-65/) Period, what the government calls your IEP, is a seven-month window that opens around your 65th birthday. Miss it without a qualifying reason, and you can face a late enrollment penalty that follows you for life. Not a one-time fee. Every single month, for as long as you have Medicare. I've seen people lose hundreds of dollars a year over a misunderstanding that could have been cleared up in a single phone call.

What surprised me was how many people get the timing wrong in ways that seem completely logical. The rules have a small but important twist most folks don't discover until it's too late.

## The Seven-Month Window, Explained Plainly

Your IEP runs for exactly seven months. Three months before the month you turn 65. Your actual birthday month. Then three months after. If you turn 65 on September 15th, your window opens June 1st and closes December 31st.

Here's where people trip up, including me the first time I walked someone through this. When you sign up matters as much as whether you sign up. Enroll in those three months before your birthday month, and your Part B (outpatient coverage, doctor visits, labs) starts the first day of your birthday month. Sign up during your birthday month itself, and your coverage is delayed by one full month. Sign up in the first month after your birthday, two months. Second month after, two months delay. Third month after, three months.

That delay isn't a technicality. If you're counting on Medicare to replace employer coverage the day you retire, a two-month gap can cost thousands of dollars out-of-pocket if something goes wrong.

Part A (hospital coverage) is usually premium-free if you or your spouse paid Medicare taxes for at least 10 years, so most people sign up for that without hesitation. Part B is where the real decision-making happens, and where the penalties bite.

## What the Late Enrollment Penalty Actually Costs

Let me be honest here: the penalty isn't a scare tactic. It's real money and it compounds over time in a way that catches people off guard.

For Part B, the penalty is 10% of the standard premium for every 12-month period you should have had coverage but didn't. As of July 2026, the standard Part B premium is $185.00 per month. If you went 24 months past your IEP without enrolling and had no qualifying [Special Enrollment Period](/medicare-special-enrollment-period/), your penalty would be 20% of $185, which is $37 tacked on every month, bringing your base premium to $222 each month. That's $444 a year extra, permanently. Not until you hit some ceiling. Every year you have Medicare.

For Part A (for the minority who didn't work enough quarters and must pay a premium), the penalty is 10% of the premium for twice the number of years you delayed. I don't have great numbers on exactly how many people pay Part A premiums, so I won't guess at a typical figure there, but the mechanism is worth knowing.

The Part D (prescription drug coverage) penalty is calculated differently. It's 1% of the national base beneficiary premium multiplied by the number of months you lacked creditable drug coverage. That premium changes each year, so the exact monthly penalty shifts. Currently it's roughly a few dollars per month for every year of the gap, but it adds up and it also never goes away.

## The "I Was Still Working" Situation

Here's the scenario I see most often. Someone retires at 67 or 68. They've had employer group health insurance the whole time. They think they missed their IEP. They panic.

Good news: if you were covered by a group health plan through your own current employment (or a spouse's), you likely qualify for a Special Enrollment Period, the SEP. This gives you eight months from the date your employment or coverage ends, whichever comes first, to sign up for Part B without penalty.

The critical word is "current." COBRA coverage and retiree coverage do not count as active employer coverage for this purpose. I've had people tell me confidently that they've had "employer coverage" for years, and when we dig into it, it's COBRA they've been paying $700 a month for. That situation does not protect you from the late penalty. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (cms.gov) is very clear on this distinction, and it's one of the most common misunderstandings I run into.

Scenario: Retired teacher, age 68, covered by her husband's active job insurance through a large company. She turns 65, skips Part B, stays on his plan. He retires at 70.
Action: She enrolls in Part B within eight months of his retirement.
Result: No late penalty at all. She pays the standard $185 monthly premium because her gap was covered by a legitimate SEP.

Contrast that with: Same woman, same age, but she's been on COBRA since her husband's company downsized when she was 66. She waits until 68 to sign up for Part B.
Action: Enrolls, believing she had continuous coverage.
Result: 24 months of uncovered time, 20% permanent penalty, extra $37/month, $444/year forever. A costly confusion.

## How to Actually Sign Up

Three ways to enroll, and all three work.

Online at Social Security's website (ssa.gov) is honestly the easiest if you're comfortable with it. The process takes about 10-15 minutes if you have your Social Security number handy, and you get a confirmation right away. Medicare.gov has a tool that walks you through eligibility and timing, and I'd bookmark that page before you do anything else.

By phone at 1-800-772-1213. TTY users can call 1-800-325-0778. Wait times vary. If you call on a Tuesday or Wednesday mid-morning, the hold times tend to be shorter in my experience, though that's not an official tip from anywhere, just something I've observed over many years.

In person at your local Social Security Administration office. Bring your birth certificate, proof of citizenship or legal residency, and your Social Security card. The clerk will ask whether you want Part A and Part B, or just Part A. Have an answer ready. If you're still working and want to delay Part B, say so clearly and confirm that your employer coverage counts as qualifying coverage.

One thing only people who've been through this know: you'll receive your red, white, and blue Medicare card in the mail roughly three to four weeks after you enroll. It does not arrive quickly. If you're coordinating a start date with a job change, plan accordingly. Don't schedule anything to happen the day your coverage kicks in if the card hasn't arrived.

## Automatic Enrollment vs. Active Enrollment

If you're already receiving Social Security retirement benefits when you turn 65, Medicare will generally enroll you in Part A and Part B automatically. Your card arrives in the mail roughly three months before your birthday. That's a nice convenience, but watch the Part B piece. You're automatically enrolled, which means you'll be charged $185/month unless you actively opt out. If you're still working and have qualifying employer coverage, you'll want to decline Part B until you retire. The paperwork for that comes with your card.

If you're not yet receiving Social Security benefits at 65 (meaning you're still working and waiting to claim your Social Security retirement income), you'll need to actively enroll in Medicare yourself. It won't happen automatically. This trips up a lot of people who've been heads-down in their careers and assume the government has it handled.
