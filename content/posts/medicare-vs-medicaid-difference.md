Every week, I get a call from someone who says something like: "My neighbor told me I'm on the wrong program and I've been overpaying for years." Nine times out of ten, what they've stumbled on is the single most common source of confusion in all of American healthcare: Medicare and Medicaid sound almost identical, they're both government health programs, and they can even overlap, but they are fundamentally different animals. One is based on age and work history. The other is based on income. Mixing them up can cost you real money and real access to care.

Let's sort this out once and for all.

---

## What Each Program Actually Is

Medicare is a federal health insurance program. The federal government runs it, sets the rules for it, and funds it primarily through payroll taxes you paid over your working life. Most people become eligible at age 65, though younger people with certain disabilities or end-stage renal disease (permanent kidney failure requiring dialysis or a transplant) can qualify earlier. Eligibility is not based on how much money you have. A retired CEO and a retired teacher with the same work history qualify under the same rules.

Medicaid is different in almost every structural way. It's a joint program, meaning the federal government and each state government share the funding and the administration. Your state sets many of its own rules within federal guidelines, which is why Medicaid in Texas looks different from Medicaid in New York. Eligibility is based primarily on income and sometimes assets, not age. Families, children, pregnant women, and people with disabilities of any age can qualify if their income falls below certain thresholds. For seniors specifically, Medicaid often serves as a safety net for things Medicare doesn't fully cover.

The simplest way I've ever explained it: Medicare is what you earned. Medicaid is what you qualify for based on financial need.

Medicare is divided into four main parts, and knowing which does what matters enormously when you're making coverage decisions.

**Part A** covers hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care (following a qualifying hospital stay), hospice care, and some home health services. If you or your spouse worked and paid Medicare taxes for at least 40 calendar quarters (about 10 years), you get Part A with no monthly premium. This is the "earned" piece most people picture when they think of Medicare.

**Part B** covers outpatient care: doctor visits, preventive screenings, lab work, durable medical equipment, and many outpatient procedures. Part B has a monthly premium. In 2024, the standard premium is $174.70 per month, though higher-income beneficiaries pay more through a surcharge called IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount).

**Part C**, often called Medicare Advantage, is an alternative way to get your Medicare benefits. Instead of the federal government paying your providers directly, you enroll through a private insurance company that contracts with Medicare. These plans often bundle Parts A, B, and sometimes D together, and many include extras like dental and vision. The trade-off is that you typically work within a network of providers.

**Part D** is prescription drug coverage, offered through private insurers who contract with Medicare. It's optional to add on, but if you skip it when first eligible and don't have creditable coverage elsewhere, you'll face a late enrollment penalty for as long as you have Part D in the future.

You can compare current plans, premiums, and drug costs directly at [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/), which is the official source and always the right place to start.

## How Medicaid Works: State-by-State Variation

Medicaid doesn't have four tidy parts. Each state runs its program under its own name, which is one reason people get confused. In California it's called Medi-Cal. In Massachusetts it's MassHealth. In other states it's simply Medicaid. Different names, same federal framework underneath.

What Medicaid covers tends to be comprehensive: doctor visits, hospital care, long-term care (including nursing home care), mental health services, prescription drugs, and more. For low-income seniors and people with disabilities, Medicaid often picks up costs that Medicare leaves behind, like long-term nursing home stays, which Medicare only covers for a limited time after a qualifying hospital stay.

Income thresholds vary by state and by the category of person applying. Adults without dependent children in expansion states (those that accepted the Affordable Care Act's Medicaid expansion) can generally qualify at income levels up to 138% of the federal poverty level (FPL). For seniors, the income and asset rules for full Medicaid benefits are often stricter and more complex.

If you're not sure whether you qualify or what the rules are in your state, the [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free, unbiased counseling in every state. These are trained counselors, not salespeople. I've referred hundreds of clients there over the years, and it's always good advice.

## When Both Programs Apply: Dual Eligibility

Here's something that surprises a lot of people: you can be on both Medicare and Medicaid at the same time. People in this situation are called "dual eligibles" or sometimes "Medicare-Medicaid beneficiaries."

For dual-eligible individuals, Medicare is the primary payer. It pays first for covered services. Medicaid then acts as secondary insurance and may cover costs that Medicare doesn't, including premiums, deductibles, and copayments, depending on your income level.

There are actually several tiers of dual eligibility:

| Category | What Medicaid Covers |
|---|---|
| Full dual eligible | Full Medicaid benefits, including long-term care and help with Medicare cost-sharing |
| Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) | Medicare Part A and Part B premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance |
| Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB) | Part B premium only |
| Qualifying Individual (QI) | Part B premium (limited slots available, first-come basis) |
| Qualified Disabled and Working Individual (QDWI) | Part A premium only |

The QMB program is particularly powerful and underused. If you qualify, providers are actually prohibited by federal law from billing you for Medicare cost-sharing on covered services. That's not just polite policy; it's illegal for them to send you that bill. Many QMB-eligible seniors don't know this and pay out-of-pocket when they legally don't have to.

## A Practical Guide: How to Figure Out Where You Stand

Whether you're helping a parent, figuring out your own situation, or just making sure you're enrolled in the right things, here's a step-by-step approach.

**Step 1: Confirm your Medicare status.**
Check your red, white, and blue Medicare card (or your digital equivalent in your Medicare account). The card shows which parts you have and your effective date. If you don't have a card or aren't sure, call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) or log in at Medicare.gov.

**Step 2: Know your income and asset situation.**
Medicaid eligibility is income-based. Gather your recent tax return, Social Security award letter, and any documentation of savings or assets. For seniors applying for full Medicaid (especially for nursing home coverage), asset rules can be complex and vary significantly by state.

**Step 3: Contact your state Medicaid office.**
You can find your state's office through Medicaid.gov. Don't rely on a neighbor's account of the rules. Medicaid rules change, vary by state, and depend on your specific household situation.

**Step 4: Check for Medicare Savings Programs.**
These are the QMB, SLMB, QI, and QDWI programs described in the table above. They're administered through Medicaid but exist specifically to help Medicare beneficiaries afford their Medicare costs. Many people who qualify never apply because they don't know these programs exist.

**Step 5: Talk to a SHIP counselor.**
This is free, confidential, and available in every state. A SHIP counselor can help you cross-reference Medicare and Medicaid rules, identify whether you're missing out on benefits, and help you apply if needed.

**Step 6: Consider professional legal advice for complex situations.**
If long-term care planning, Medicaid spend-down strategies, or estate planning are involved, an elder law attorney is worth every penny. Medicaid's look-back rules for asset transfers are unforgiving, and a mistake can have a multi-year impact on eligibility.

## The Biggest Mistakes I See People Make

After two decades of working with seniors and their families, the same errors come up again and again.

The first is assuming Medicare covers nursing home care indefinitely. It doesn't. Medicare covers skilled nursing facility care for up to 100 days following a qualifying hospital stay of at least three days, and the coverage conditions are strict. Days 21 through 100 come with a significant daily coinsurance charge. After 100 days, Medicare coverage ends entirely. Long-term nursing home care is largely a Medicaid benefit, and the planning required to access it, without impoverishing a spouse or losing everything, is genuinely complex.

The second is not applying for Medicare Savings Programs out of pride or confusion. There's no shame in using a program you qualify for. These programs exist precisely because Medicare's costs can be crushing on a fixed income.

The third is procrastinating on Part D enrollment. I've seen clients come to me with a late enrollment penalty that's been accumulating for seven or eight years. That penalty follows you for life.

The fourth, and maybe the most costly, is making asset transfers without understanding Medicaid's five-year look-back period. If you give money to your children (or anyone else) within five years of applying for Medicaid long-term care benefits, those transfers can create a penalty period during which you're ineligible for benefits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I have Medicare and Medicaid at the same time?

Yes. This is called being "dual eligible" and it's more common than most people realize. About 12 million Americans are enrolled in both programs. Medicare pays first for covered services, and Medicaid may cover some or all of the remaining costs depending on your income level and which Medicaid program you qualify for.

### If I'm on Medicare, do I still need to apply for Medicaid separately?

Yes. Medicare and Medicaid are separate programs with separate applications. Being enrolled in Medicare does not automatically enroll you in Medicaid. You apply for Medicaid through your state's Medicaid agency. There's no shame in applying if you think you may qualify; that's what the program is for.

### Does Medicaid cover prescription drugs for Medicare beneficiaries?

If you're on both Medicare and Medicaid, your prescription drugs are generally covered through Medicare Part D, not Medicaid. You'll automatically be enrolled in a Part D plan (called an auto-enrollment or "facilitated enrollment" plan) if you're full dual eligible and don't choose a plan yourself. These plans have low or zero premiums and limited cost-sharing for covered drugs.

### What's the difference between Medicare Supplement (Medigap) and Medicaid?

Medicare Supplement insurance, commonly called Medigap, is a private insurance product you purchase to help cover Medicare's out-of-pocket costs. It has nothing to do with income eligibility. Medicaid is a government program based on financial need. If your income is too high for Medicaid but you want help with Medicare cost-sharing, a Medigap policy is one option. You can find a helpful overview of Medigap options in [this guide to Medicare supplement insurance](https://www.medicare.gov/supplements-other-insurance/whats-medicare-supplement-insurance-medigap) on Medicare.gov.

### Will Medicare or Medicaid pay for home care?

Medicare covers short-term skilled home health care (like a nurse visiting after a hospitalization) under specific conditions. It doesn't cover custodial home care, meaning help with bathing, dressing, and daily activities, unless it's combined with skilled care. Medicaid, especially through Home and Community Based Services (HCBS) waivers, can cover long-term custodial home care for people who qualify financially. The rules vary significantly by state, so checking with your state Medicaid office or a SHIP counselor is essential.

The bottom line is that these two programs serve different purposes, work in very different ways, and require separate attention. Understanding where one ends and the other begins could save you thousands of dollars and open doors to benefits you didn't know you had. You don't have to figure it out alone. Free help is available, and starting with an accurate picture of your own situation is always the right first move.
