Medicare cost-sharing splits into two buckets: flat-dollar copays and percentage-based coinsurance. That distinction sounds small until you're facing a $40,000 hospital bill and you realize you're on the hook for 20% of it, not a tidy $30 copay. Understanding which applies where, and how to protect yourself from the open-ended ones, is one of the most practical things you can do with your Medicare knowledge.

A copay is a fixed dollar amount. You pay it at the point of service, and that's that. Coinsurance is a percentage of the approved cost, which means your share grows with the size of the service. Original Medicare (Parts A and B) leans heavily on coinsurance, and that's exactly why so many people add supplemental coverage.

## How Each One Actually Works

A copay behaves like a vending machine: drop in the required amount, get your service. You'll see these most often in Medicare Advantage (Part C) plans and Part D (prescription drug coverage). Your plan might charge a $10 copay for a primary care visit, $45 for a specialist, $0 for certain generics. Predictable. Budgetable.

Coinsurance is different in character. It's a share of a cost you don't know in advance. Under Part B, Original Medicare pays 80% of the Medicare-approved amount for most outpatient services, doctors, lab work, durable medical equipment, and you owe the remaining 20%. If the approved amount for a procedure is $500, you pay $100. If it's $5,000, you pay $1,000. There's no annual out-of-pocket ceiling built into Original [Medicare Parts](/medicare-part-d-explained/) A and B, which is the detail most people miss.

Part A works a bit differently, and it trips people up. The hospital cost-sharing under Part A isn't a percentage, it's structured in tiers based on how long you're admitted within a single "benefit period" (a spell of illness that begins when you're admitted to a hospital or skilled nursing facility and ends after you've been out of a hospital or SNF for 60 consecutive days).

## The Numbers You Need to Know for 2026

| Situation | What You Pay (2026) |
|---|---|
| Part B coinsurance (most outpatient services) | 20% of Medicare-approved amount |
| Part B deductible (must meet before the 20% kicks in) | $283 |
| Part A hospital days 1-60 | $0 coinsurance (after $1,736 per-benefit-period deductible) |
| Part A hospital days 61-90 | $434/day |
| Part A hospital lifetime reserve days (91+) | $868/day |
| Skilled nursing facility (SNF) days 1-20 | $0 |
| SNF days 21-100 | $217/day |
| Part D out-of-pocket cap | $2,100 for the year |

A few things worth calling out in that table. First, there's no cap on the number of benefit periods you can have in a year, so if you're hospitalized multiple times, that $1,736 deductible can hit you more than once. Second, lifetime reserve days are exactly that, lifetime: you get 60 of them total across your entire Medicare history. Third, the Part D $2,100 cap (created by the Inflation Reduction Act) is the most significant consumer protection change to Part D in years. After you hit that ceiling, your covered drugs cost you $0 for the rest of the year.

## Why the Distinction Matters Most in Practice

Here's where things get practical. The choice between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage is, in large part, a choice between coinsurance risk and copay predictability, and neither is universally better.

With Original Medicare, you face open-ended 20% coinsurance on outpatient care. A single outpatient surgery can produce a bill where your share is several thousand dollars. Most people manage this by adding a Medigap (Medicare Supplement) policy, which typically covers all or most of that 20% Part B coinsurance. [Medigap Plan](/what-is-a-medigap-plan/) G, for example, covers Part B coinsurance entirely after the $283 deductible. Plan F (available only to those eligible before January 1, 2020) covers both the deductible and coinsurance.

[Medigap Plans](/best-medigap-plans-2026/) K and L work differently. Plan K covers 50% of Part B coinsurance (not 100%), but it also caps your out-of-pocket costs at $8,000 for 2026. Plan L covers 75% of Part B coinsurance with a $4,000 cap. These are worth considering if your priority is protection from a catastrophic bill rather than eliminating every smaller cost.

Medicare Advantage plans flip the model. Instead of the 20% Part B coinsurance, you generally pay fixed copays: a dollar amount per visit, per procedure, per admission. That predictability appeals to people on fixed incomes. The tradeoff is that you're usually limited to the plan's network, and prior authorization requirements can create friction for certain procedures or specialists.

**Example:** Suppose someone in Original Medicare has outpatient knee surgery. The Medicare-approved amount is $8,000. After meeting the $283 annual Part B deductible, their 20% coinsurance comes to $1,600 (($8,000 - $283 already met = $8,000 of which 20% is $1,600, assuming the deductible was already satisfied). With a Medigap Plan G, that $1,600 would be covered. With a Medicare Advantage plan, the same procedure might carry a $400 copay instead, though it would need to be performed in-network.

## A Misconception Worth Clearing Up

Many people assume coinsurance and copays serve as a full accounting of what they'll owe. They don't, necessarily, in one specific situation: "excess charges."

If a doctor doesn't accept Medicare assignment (meaning they don't agree to accept Medicare's approved amount as payment in full), they can charge up to 15% above the Medicare-approved rate. That 15% is called a Part B excess charge, and your 20% coinsurance is calculated on the approved amount, not on the actual billed amount. The excess charge sits on top. Most Medigap plans, particularly Plans F and G, cover excess charges. Original Medicare and most other Medigap plans don't. If your doctor doesn't accept assignment, your bill can be higher than your coinsurance alone would suggest. You can check whether a specific doctor accepts assignment using the physician search tool at [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/).
