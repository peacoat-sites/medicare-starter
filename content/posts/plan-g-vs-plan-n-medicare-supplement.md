Imagine you've just retired, you're sitting across from an insurance agent, and they slide two brochures across the table: Plan G and Plan N. Both promise to cover most of what Original Medicare leaves behind. Both look pretty good on paper. But the premiums are different, the out-of-pocket exposure is different, and the agent is watching the clock. You don't want to pick the wrong one and pay for it for years. This is the exact situation I've seen hundreds of times over two decades of helping seniors sort through their Medicare options, and the good news is that once you understand what each plan actually does, the choice usually becomes much clearer.

## What Medigap Is and Why It Matters

Before comparing the two plans side by side, let's make sure we're on the same page about what we're even talking about. Medigap is another name for Medicare Supplement Insurance. It's private health insurance designed to work alongside Original Medicare, which is Parts A and B, to cover costs that Medicare doesn't fully pay.

Here's the thing people often don't realize: Original Medicare is not free once you start using it. Part A covers hospital stays, but you owe a deductible each benefit period, which in 2024 is $1,632. Part B covers outpatient care, but it only pays 80% of approved costs after your annual deductible. You're responsible for the other 20%, and there's no cap on that 20% exposure. A serious illness could leave you with tens of thousands of dollars in coinsurance.

That's the gap Medigap fills. The [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) standardizes Medigap plans by letter, which means a Plan G sold by Aetna covers exactly the same benefits as a Plan G sold by Blue Cross Blue Shield. The only difference between insurers is the premium and, sometimes, the quality of customer service. This standardization is genuinely useful because it means you can shop on price alone once you've chosen a plan letter.

Plan G and Plan N are currently the two most popular Medigap options for new Medicare enrollees, largely because Plan F, which used to be the most popular, was closed to people who became eligible for Medicare on or after January 1, 2020. If you're newly eligible, G and N are where your real decision sits.

Plan G is about as comprehensive as it gets for new enrollees. Once you pay your Part B deductible, which is $240 in 2024, Plan G covers virtually everything else Original Medicare leaves behind.

Here's what Plan G covers:

- **Part A hospital coinsurance and hospital costs** up to an additional 365 days after Medicare benefits are exhausted
- **Part A deductible** (that $1,632 per benefit period we mentioned)
- **Part A hospice care coinsurance or copayments**
- **Part B coinsurance or copayments** (the 20% you'd otherwise owe)
- **Part B excess charges** (more on this in a moment)
- **Skilled nursing facility care coinsurance**
- **First three pints of blood**
- **80% of foreign travel emergency costs** (up to plan limits)

The one thing Plan G doesn't cover is your Part B deductible. That's it. Pay that $240 once at the start of the year, and for the rest of the year, you essentially have no out-of-pocket costs for Medicare-covered services. None. This predictability is exactly why many people choose Plan G, especially if they have chronic conditions or expect to use their benefits frequently.

The "Part B excess charges" benefit deserves a special mention because it trips people up. Some doctors don't accept Medicare assignment, meaning they can charge up to 15% more than the Medicare-approved amount. Plan G pays that extra 15%. Plan N does not. If you live in a state that doesn't restrict excess charges (most states don't), and you want to see any doctor who accepts Medicare, Plan G gives you that freedom without a surprise bill.

## Plan N: Lower Premiums With Some Cost-Sharing

Plan N covers most of the same things as Plan G, but it asks you to share a small portion of the costs in exchange for a lower monthly premium. That trade-off is the heart of the G vs. N decision.

Here's where Plan N differs from Plan G:

- **You pay up to a $20 copay for office visits.** Preventive visits are typically copay-free, but regular office visits can cost you up to $20 each.
- **You pay up to a $50 copay for emergency room visits.** This copay is waived if you're admitted to the hospital.
- **Plan N does not cover Part B excess charges.** If you see a doctor who doesn't accept Medicare assignment, you'll owe that extra amount out of pocket.
- **You still pay the Part B deductible**, just like with Plan G.

The premium savings with Plan N are real. Depending on your age, location, and the insurance company, Plan N premiums can run $20 to $60 or more per month less than Plan G. Over a year, that's potentially $240 to $720 back in your pocket.

The question is whether those savings outpace the copays and any excess charges you might encounter. In my experience, healthy people who rarely visit the doctor often come out ahead with Plan N. People managing ongoing health conditions, especially those who see specialists frequently, often find Plan G the better deal because those $20 copays add up fast.

## How to Actually Decide Between Plan G and Plan N

This is where I want to give you a practical framework, not just a list of features. Here's how I'd walk through the decision with a client sitting in my office:

**Step 1: Estimate your doctor visit frequency.**
Think about how many times you visited a doctor or specialist in the past year, not counting preventive visits. If the honest answer is fewer than 10 times, Plan N's copays are probably manageable. If you have conditions that send you to the office regularly, those $20 copays can erode your premium savings quickly.

**Step 2: Know your doctors.**
Call your primary care doctor and any specialists you see regularly. Ask if they accept Medicare assignment. If they all do, and you have no plans to seek care outside that circle, the excess charge gap in Plan N may not matter to you at all. If you want the flexibility to see any Medicare-accepting doctor without worrying about extra charges, Plan G gives you that.

**Step 3: Get quotes from multiple companies.**
Because Medigap benefits are standardized, the premium is the differentiator. Get quotes for both Plan G and Plan N from at least three to four insurers. Your state's [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free, unbiased help comparing options, and I cannot recommend them highly enough. They're trained counselors with no sales incentive.

**Step 4: Do the math honestly.**
Calculate the annual premium difference between Plan G and Plan N. Then estimate your realistic annual copay exposure with Plan N, based on Steps 1 and 2. If the premium savings are larger than your expected copay costs, Plan N wins. If they're close or Plan G comes out ahead, pay the extra premium for the peace of mind.

**Step 5: Think about your tolerance for uncertainty.**
Some people genuinely sleep better knowing their coverage is airtight. If unexpected bills cause you serious stress, that's worth something real, even if Plan G costs a bit more. Health insurance isn't just a financial product; it's also peace of mind.

## Enrollment Timing and Why It Changes Everything

One thing I want you to understand clearly: when you enroll matters as much as which plan you choose. During your Medigap Open Enrollment Period, which runs for six months starting the month you're both 65 and enrolled in Part B, insurers cannot reject you or charge you more based on your health history. You can get Plan G or Plan N at the same rate as someone in perfect health.

Outside that window, insurers in most states can use medical underwriting. They can ask about your health history and charge you more, or decline you altogether. This is not a technicality. I've seen people wait a few years, develop a health condition, and then find they can't get Medigap coverage at any price.

If you're approaching Medicare eligibility right now, this timing issue deserves serious attention. Don't let the plan comparison distract you from getting enrolled at the right time. A good plan you can actually qualify for beats a great plan you can't.
