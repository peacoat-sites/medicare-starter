You've just retired, sitting across from an insurance agent, and they slide two brochures at you: Plan G and Plan N. Both promise to cover most of what Original Medicare leaves behind. Both look decent on paper. But the premiums differ, the out-of-pocket exposure differs, and the agent's watching the clock. You don't want to pick wrong and pay for it for years.

I've seen this exact scenario play out hundreds of times over two decades helping seniors sort through Medicare options. The good news: once you understand what each plan actually does, the choice usually becomes much clearer.

## What Medigap Is and Why It Matters

Let's start with the basics. Medigap is another name for [Medicare Supplement Insurance](/best-medigap-plans-2026/), private health insurance designed to work alongside Original Medicare (Parts A and B) and cover costs Medicare doesn't fully pay.

Here's what people often miss: Original Medicare isn't free once you start using it. Part A covers hospital stays, but you owe a deductible each benefit period ($1,632 in 2024). Part B covers outpatient care, but it only pays 80% of approved costs after your annual deductible. You're responsible for the other 20%, with no cap on that exposure. A serious illness could leave you with tens of thousands of dollars in coinsurance.

That's the gap Medigap fills. The [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) standardizes Medigap plans by letter, so a Plan G from Aetna covers exactly what a Plan G from Blue Cross Blue Shield covers. The only difference between insurers is the premium and sometimes customer service quality. This standardization matters because you can shop purely on price once you've chosen a plan letter.

Plan G and Plan N are the two most popular Medigap options for new Medicare enrollees. Plan F, historically the go-to, closed to people who became eligible for Medicare on or after January 1, 2020. If you're newly eligible, G and N are where your real decision sits.

Plan G is about as comprehensive as it gets for new enrollees. Pay your Part B deductible ($240 in 2024), and Plan G covers virtually everything else Original Medicare leaves behind.

What Plan G covers:

- **Part A hospital coinsurance and hospital costs** up to an additional 365 days after Medicare benefits are exhausted
- **Part A deductible** (that $1,632 per benefit period)
- **Part A hospice care coinsurance or copayments**
- **Part B coinsurance or copayments** (the 20% you'd otherwise owe)
- **[Part B excess charges](/medigap-plan-g-vs-plan-n-comparison/)** (more below)
- **Skilled nursing facility care coinsurance**
- **First three pints of blood**
- **80% of foreign travel emergency costs** (up to plan limits)

The one thing Plan G doesn't cover? Your Part B deductible. Pay that $240 once at the start of the year, and for the rest of the year you essentially have zero out-of-pocket costs for Medicare-covered services. This predictability is exactly why many people choose Plan G, especially if they have chronic conditions or expect to use benefits frequently.

The "Part B excess charges" benefit deserves special mention because it catches people off guard. Some doctors don't accept Medicare assignment, meaning they can charge up to 15% more than the Medicare-approved amount. Plan G pays that extra 15%. Plan N does not. If you live in a state without restrictions on excess charges (most don't), and you want to see any Medicare-accepting doctor, Plan G gives you that freedom without surprise bills.

## Plan N: Lower Premiums With Some Cost-Sharing

Plan N covers most of the same things as Plan G, but asks you to share small portions of costs in exchange for lower monthly premiums. That trade-off is the heart of this decision.

Where Plan N differs:

- **You pay up to a $20 copay for office visits.** Preventive visits are typically copay-free, but regular office visits cost you up to $20 each.
- **You pay up to a $50 copay for emergency room visits.** This copay disappears if you're admitted to the hospital.
- **Plan N does not cover Part B excess charges.** See a doctor who doesn't accept Medicare assignment, and you owe that extra amount out of pocket.
- **You still pay the Part B deductible**, same as Plan G.

The premium savings are real. Depending on your age, location, and insurer, Plan N premiums run $20 to $60 or more per month less than Plan G. That's $240 to $720 a year.

But do those savings beat the copays and excess charges? In my experience, healthy people who rarely see doctors often come out ahead with Plan N. People managing ongoing conditions, especially those seeing specialists frequently, often find Plan G the better deal because those $20 copays accumulate fast.

## How to Actually Decide Between Plan G and Plan N

Here's a practical framework I'd walk through with a client in my office:

**Step 1: Estimate your doctor visit frequency.**
Think about how many times you visited a doctor or specialist last year, not counting preventive visits. Fewer than 10? Plan N's copays are probably manageable. Regular visits for ongoing conditions? Those $20 copays eat into savings quickly.

**Step 2: Know your doctors.**
Call your primary care doctor and any specialists you see. Ask if they accept Medicare assignment. If they all do and you have no plans to seek care elsewhere, the excess charge gap in Plan N may not matter. If you want flexibility to see any Medicare-accepting doctor, Plan G gives you that.

**Step 3: Get quotes from multiple companies.**
Because benefits are standardized, premium is what differs. Get quotes for both Plan G and Plan N from at least three or four insurers. Your state's [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free, unbiased help. They're trained counselors with zero sales incentive, and I can't recommend them highly enough.

**Step 4: Do the math honestly.**
Calculate the annual premium difference between Plan G and Plan N. Then estimate your realistic annual copay exposure with Plan N based on Steps 1 and 2. If premium savings exceed expected copay costs, Plan N wins. If they're close or Plan G comes out ahead, pay the extra premium for peace of mind.

**Step 5: Think about your tolerance for uncertainty.**
Some people genuinely sleep better knowing their coverage is airtight. If unexpected bills cause serious stress, that's worth real money, even if Plan G costs more. Health insurance isn't just financial. It's also peace of mind.

## Enrollment Timing and Why It Changes Everything

Here's something critical: when you enroll matters as much as which plan you choose. During your Medigap Open Enrollment Period (six months starting the month you're both 65 and enrolled in Part B), insurers can't reject you or charge more based on health history. You get Plan G or Plan N at the same rate as someone in perfect health.

Outside that window, insurers in most states can use medical underwriting. They can ask about your health history, charge you more, or decline you altogether. This isn't a technicality. I've seen people wait a few years, develop a health condition, and then find they can't get Medigap coverage at any price.

If you're approaching Medicare eligibility, pay serious attention to this timing. Don't let plan comparisons distract you from enrolling at the right time. A good plan you can actually qualify for beats a great plan you can't get.

Choosing between Plan G and Plan N doesn't have to feel overwhelming. You're weighing a higher premium against higher, somewhat unpredictable out-of-pocket risk. For people who want simplicity and see doctors regularly, Plan G often wins. For healthy, infrequent healthcare users comfortable with modest cost-sharing, Plan N's savings can meaningfully add up over time. Get real quotes, talk honestly to your doctors' offices, and reach out to your local SHIP counselor for personalized guidance. You don't have to figure this out alone.
