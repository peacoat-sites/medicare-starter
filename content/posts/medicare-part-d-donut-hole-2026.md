Something changed with Medicare Part D in 2026 that a lot of people still don't fully understand, and I want to make sure you're not one of them come January.

For years, the "donut hole" was one of the most confusing and genuinely painful parts of Medicare prescription drug coverage. Seniors would hit a spending threshold in the middle of the year, watch their drug costs spike dramatically, and feel completely blindsided. I've sat across from people in their seventies who cried when they realized what was happening to their pharmacy bills. It was a real problem.

Here's where things stand now: the traditional coverage gap, the donut hole as it existed for most of the past two decades, has been restructured significantly thanks to the Inflation Reduction Act. But "restructured" doesn't mean "gone," and it definitely doesn't mean your costs are the same as they were last year. You might be wondering whether the donut hole still applies to you at all in 2026. The honest answer is: it depends on your plan, your drugs, and where you fall in the new benefit structure. Let me walk you through all of it.

## What the Donut Hole Actually Was (and Why It Mattered So Much)

The coverage gap was built into Part D from the beginning when the program launched in 2006. The original design had you paying relatively low costs in the initial coverage phase, then falling into a gap where you suddenly owed a much larger share of drug costs, then eventually reaching catastrophic coverage where costs dropped again. That middle phase, where costs spiked, became known as the donut hole.

At its worst, people in the coverage gap were paying 100% of their drug costs out of pocket until they reached the catastrophic threshold. Discounts came in over the years to soften it. But the structural problem remained: a predictable cost cliff that hit mid-year, often right when people were least prepared for it.

The Inflation Reduction Act changed this substantially, with changes phasing in through 2024 and 2025. By 2026, the benefit structure looks meaningfully different than it did even a few years ago.

Think of Part D coverage in three phases now, not four.

The old four-phase model (deductible, initial coverage, coverage gap, catastrophic) has been compressed. Today you have: the deductible phase, the initial coverage phase, and then a new catastrophic phase. The gap in the middle, where your costs used to spike, has been restructured.

In 2026, once you reach the out-of-pocket threshold (currently set at $2,000 for the year), your cost-sharing drops significantly. That $2,000 cap on out-of-pocket drug spending is the single biggest improvement for beneficiaries in decades. Before this change, reaching catastrophic coverage required spending roughly $8,000 or more out of pocket. People with serious conditions who needed expensive specialty drugs were getting devastated.

The $2,000 out-of-pocket cap is real. It's the law. But there are things people miss about how it works.

First, what counts toward that $2,000 isn't just what you personally pay. Your deductible payments count. Your copays and coinsurance in the initial coverage phase count. Manufacturer discounts on brand-name drugs in the coverage gap count toward your out-of-pocket total, even though you didn't technically pay that money. This is a detail that confuses a lot of people, and it works in your favor.

Second, your monthly premium does NOT count toward that $2,000. Only what you pay at the pharmacy counter (and the manufacturer discounts just mentioned) applies to the threshold.

Third, and this is the part people most often overlook: individual plans can still structure their cost-sharing differently within these rules. Two Part D plans might both comply with 2026 regulations and still have very different copays for the same drug in the same phase. The $2,000 cap is a ceiling on your total exposure, but the path to get there varies by plan.

## The Donut Hole in 2026: Does It Still Exist?

Technically, yes. The coverage gap still exists as a defined phase in the Part D benefit structure. You can still enter it.

What's changed is that the automatic cost spike that used to define it has been largely addressed. In 2026, your coinsurance in the coverage gap for brand-name drugs is 25%. Manufacturers of brand-name drugs are required to pay a 70% discount during the gap. Your plan covers the remaining 5%. So you're paying 25% during the coverage gap, which is actually the same as what you pay during the initial coverage phase for many plans.

For generic drugs in the coverage gap, you pay 25% coinsurance. Before the Inflation Reduction Act reforms, generics in the gap had been a bigger cost burden than brand names in some situations, because generic manufacturers didn't have the same discount obligation. That disparity has narrowed.

So the "hole" is less of a hole than it used to be. But here's my honest take on this: calling it "fixed" overstates things. If you're on a high-cost specialty medication and you hit the coverage gap before reaching the $2,000 cap, you're still going to feel it. The benefit structure helps. The $2,000 cap helps enormously. But the experience of managing Part D costs mid-year is still complex, and it still requires planning.

## How to Know Where You Stand Right Now

The most important thing you can do is look at your current plan's Summary of Benefits and the Evidence of Coverage document. Your plan is required to send you an updated Evidence of Coverage each year. If you've moved it to the recycling bin, you can find it on Medicare.gov or by calling your plan directly.

What you're looking for specifically:

Your plan's deductible (the maximum allowed in 2026 is $590, though some plans waive it entirely for certain drug tiers). Your tier structure, meaning which tier covers your drugs. Your coinsurance or copay amounts at each phase. And whether your plan uses standard benefit design or an alternative design, because plans have flexibility here.

If you take brand-name drugs, especially specialty tier drugs, check whether those drugs have any Extra Help or Low Income Subsidy (LIS) coverage available to you. LIS is a federal program that helps people with limited income and resources pay Part D costs, and it's genuinely underused. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services estimates that hundreds of thousands of eligible people aren't enrolled. If your annual income is roughly at or below 150% of the federal poverty level, you may qualify. Check eligibility at Medicare.gov or through your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP).

AARP's Medicare resource center (aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance) also has solid, plain-English guidance on LIS eligibility and application, and I've pointed many readers there when they needed a starting point.

## The Medicare Prescription Payment Plan: One More Thing You Should Know

Starting in 2025 and continuing through 2026, there's a new option called the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan, sometimes called the M3P. This lets you spread your out-of-pocket drug costs across the calendar year rather than paying large amounts all at once.

Here's what that means practically. If you have expensive drugs and you hit the $2,000 out-of-pocket cap in March, under the old system you'd have paid that $2,000 in the first few months of the year. With the M3P, you can elect to have your plan spread those costs in monthly installments across January through December.

This doesn't change how much you owe. It changes when you pay it. For people on fixed incomes, that distinction matters enormously.

You have to actively opt in to the M3P each year. Your plan must offer it, and enrollment periods apply. Ask your Part D plan about it during Open Enrollment (October 15 through December 7), or call 1-800-MEDICARE if you want to understand which plans in your area offer it.
