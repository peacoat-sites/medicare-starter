## What the Donut Hole Actually Is (And What It Isn't in 2026)

Let's back up and be honest about something. The phrase "donut hole" has been floating around Medicare conversations for so long that many people treat it as a fixed, scary thing, like a pothole that never gets filled. The reality is that Congress has been chipping away at it for years, and 2026 marks a genuinely meaningful milestone.

Here's the basic structure of Part D drug coverage. Your plan pays a share of your drug costs, you pay a share, and that relationship shifts at different spending thresholds. There are four stages: the deductible phase, initial coverage, the coverage gap (the donut hole), and catastrophic coverage.

For 2026, the deductible for Part D plans can be no higher than $590. Once you meet that, you enter initial coverage, where you pay your normal copays or coinsurance. The donut hole has historically begun when your total drug costs (what you and the plan paid together) hit a certain dollar threshold. In 2026, that threshold is $5,030 in total drug costs.

Now here's where things get genuinely interesting.

Before 2024, once you fell into the coverage gap, you paid a much higher percentage of drug costs out of pocket, sometimes 25% or more for generics, and higher for brand-names. Starting in 2024, as a result of the Inflation Reduction Act, a dramatic shift happened: your out-of-pocket costs in the donut hole are now capped in a completely new way. In 2025, a $2,000 annual out-of-pocket cap was introduced. For 2026, that $2,000 cap remains in place, but the framework around it has been refined.

What this means practically: once you spend $2,000 in out-of-pocket drug costs during 2026, you move into catastrophic coverage and pay $0 for covered drugs for the rest of the year. The donut hole still technically exists as a phase in the coverage timeline, but its financial sting is almost entirely removed for most beneficiaries.

A quick note on that table: the $5,030 figure represents your total drug costs (plan + you combined), while the $2,000 figure is what you personally pay out of pocket. These are different calculations, which trips people up constantly. Your plan's spending counts toward reaching the coverage gap threshold, but only your own out-of-pocket spending counts toward the $2,000 cap.

Also worth knowing: the Medicare Extra Help program (also called Low Income Subsidy, or LIS) can dramatically reduce these costs for qualifying beneficiaries. If your income and resources are limited, you may pay little to nothing in any phase. Check Medicare.gov to see if you qualify. It's one of the most underutilized benefits in the entire program, and I've seen clients leave thousands of dollars on the table simply because they didn't know to ask.

## How the $2,000 Out-of-Pocket Cap Changes Everything

I'll be honest: when the Inflation Reduction Act passed and I started explaining these changes to people, the most common reaction was skepticism. "Is this real? There's actually a cap now?" Yes. It's real, and for people on expensive specialty drugs or multiple brand-name medications, it's transformative.

Before the cap existed, a beneficiary on two or three high-cost drugs could spend $5,000, $7,000, or more in a single year on drug costs alone. That's not a hypothetical. That was reality for people on certain cancer drugs, MS medications, or treatments for rheumatoid arthritis.

The $2,000 cap means that no matter how expensive your covered Part D drugs are, you won't pay more than $2,000 out of pocket in 2026 for those drugs. Period.

There's also a new payment option called the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan (sometimes called the "smoothing" option) that lets you spread those out-of-pocket costs over monthly installments rather than paying large sums upfront. If you take an expensive medication early in the year and would otherwise face a big January bill, this option lets you pay $166 or so per month instead of several hundred dollars at once. Your pharmacist or plan can explain enrollment, or you can explore it at Medicare.gov.

One caveat: the $2,000 cap applies to Part D covered drugs. Drugs excluded from your plan's formulary (the approved drug list), or drugs you buy outside your network without special circumstances, may not count toward it. Reading your plan's Evidence of Coverage document matters.

## Who Still Feels the Donut Hole in 2026

Here's the part most articles gloss over: despite the improvements, not everyone is in the clear.

The coverage gap phase still involves cost-sharing that varies by plan. Between $5,030 in total costs and your $2,000 out-of-pocket limit, individual plans set their own cost-sharing rules. Some plans are more generous than others in this zone. A plan might charge you 25% coinsurance on brand-name drugs in the gap, while another charges a flat copay. Those differences can add up.

The $2,000 cap also resets every January 1. If you're managing a chronic condition and reach $2,000 by March, you'll enjoy free drugs for nine months. But you'll start over at zero the following year. For people with very high drug costs, that annual reset can still create cash flow challenges early in the year, which is exactly why the smoothing payment plan was designed.

Generic drugs, which the vast majority of Part D enrollees use exclusively, are less affected by all of this drama. If you're only on generics, your copays in the gap tend to be modest, and you may never spend anywhere close to $2,000 out of pocket. The biggest impact of the new cap is felt by people on specialty or brand-name drugs.

## How to Protect Yourself: A Practical Step-by-Step Plan

You don't need to be a Medicare expert to handle this smartly. You just need a few focused actions each year.

**Step 1: Review your plan during Open Enrollment (October 15 to December 7)**
Your current plan's formulary can change every year. A drug that was Tier 2 in 2025 might be Tier 3 in 2026, which affects your costs at every phase. Use the Medicare Plan Finder at Medicare.gov to compare plans based on your specific drug list.

**Step 2: Look at your Explanation of Benefits (EOB) statements**
Your Part D plan sends these monthly. They show exactly where you are in the coverage phases. Track your cumulative out-of-pocket spending so you know when you'll hit the $2,000 threshold.

**Step 3: Ask your doctor about therapeutic alternatives**
If you're on a brand-name drug, ask whether a generic equivalent exists. A therapeutic switch might cut your costs dramatically. Your pharmacist is also an excellent (and often underused) resource for this.

**Step 4: Look into the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan**
If you take a high-cost specialty drug, ask your plan about the smoothing option. Enrolling before you fill an expensive prescription early in the year gives you the most cash flow benefit.

**Step 5: Apply for Extra Help if you might qualify**
The income and asset limits are higher than many people assume. A single person with limited savings and income may qualify even if they don't think of themselves as "low income." Apply through the Social Security Administration or check eligibility at Medicare.gov.

**Step 6: Use a Medicare counselor**
Every state has a free State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) office with trained counselors. They charge nothing and have no financial stake in what plan you choose. Find yours at shiphelp.org.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the donut hole still exist in 2026?

Technically yes, the coverage gap phase is still part of the Part D structure in 2026. But the financial impact has been radically reduced. The $2,000 out-of-pocket cap means that even when you're in the gap, your total annual spending on covered drugs is limited. For most people, the coverage gap no longer represents the financial crisis it once did.

### What counts toward my $2,000 out-of-pocket limit?

Your own deductible payments, your copays and coinsurance during initial coverage, and your cost-sharing in the coverage gap all count. What doesn't count: your plan's premium, costs for drugs not on your plan's formulary, and costs for drugs purchased outside your plan's network in most situations. The research here is mixed on edge cases, so if you have a specific drug situation, confirm with your plan directly.

### Can I use manufacturer coupons or patient assistance programs to lower my costs?

This is a complicated area. Before 2024, manufacturer copay cards for brand-name drugs did not count toward your out-of-pocket costs in Medicare. The rules changed with the Inflation Reduction Act, and now certain manufacturer contributions can count. However, the details depend on the specific program and drug. Ask your pharmacist or plan before assuming a coupon will help your threshold calculations.

### What if I have both Medicare and Medicaid (dual eligible)?

If you qualify for both programs (called "dual eligible"), you're automatically enrolled in Extra Help and pay very little for Part D drugs. The donut hole effectively doesn't apply to you. If your income and assets change and you think you might now qualify for Medicaid, contact your state Medicaid office.

### My pharmacist told me I'm entering the coverage gap. What should I do right now?

Don't panic, and don't skip doses. The first thing to do is ask your pharmacist to print out your year-to-date spending summary so you can see how far you are from the $2,000 cap. Then contact your plan to understand exactly what your cost-sharing looks like in the gap for your specific drugs. If costs are still a concern, call your SHIP counselor or check whether Extra Help is available to you.

The donut hole story in 2026 is genuinely a better story than it was five years ago, and that's not spin. Real structural changes have given millions of beneficiaries real protection. But "better" doesn't mean "simple," and it certainly doesn't mean you can set your drug coverage on autopilot. The people who come out ahead are the ones who check their plan every fall, track their spending through the year, and ask questions when something doesn't add up. Your drugs are too important, and the money is too real, to leave those details to chance.
