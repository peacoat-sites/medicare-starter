Medicare covers a lot, but it doesn't cover everything, and the gap that surprises people most is what you owe during a hospital stay. The good news: once you understand how the cost-per-day structure works, you can plan for it. The short answer is that [Medicare Part](/medicare-part-b-premium/) A uses a "benefit period" system with a one-time deductible, then daily coinsurance that kicks in after day 60. That coinsurance can be steep.

Part A's hospital cost structure has nothing to do with how long your stay is per se. It resets based on benefit periods, not calendar years. That's the piece most people miss, and it's the reason a second hospitalization can cost you a second full deductible within the same year.

## How the Benefit Period Works

A benefit period starts the day you're admitted to a hospital as an inpatient and ends once you've gone 60 consecutive days without receiving inpatient hospital or skilled nursing facility (SNF) care. There's no limit to how many benefit periods you can have in your lifetime. That's good in one way, coverage never permanently runs out, but it also means the deductible resets each time.

The practical implication: if you're hospitalized in January, discharged, readmitted in February without 60 days in between, you're still in the same benefit period. No second deductible. But if you're hospitalized in January and again in September with 60 clean days in between, you owe the deductible twice. Two hospitalizations, two deductibles, same calendar year.

## The Day-by-Day Cost Structure

Here's where the daily costs actually land. Medicare Part A divides a hospital stay into three tiers, and the numbers change each year. Rather than cite figures that may already be shifting, I'd strongly recommend checking Medicare.gov for the current amounts, but the structure itself is stable.

| Stay Length | What You Pay | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1-60 | Deductible only (paid once per benefit period) | No daily coinsurance during this window |
| Days 61-90 | Daily coinsurance per day | Applies to each of the 30 days in this window |
| Days 91-150 | Higher daily coinsurance ("lifetime reserve days") | You have exactly 60 lifetime reserve days total, ever |
| Day 151 and beyond | 100% of costs | Medicare Part A pays nothing |

The jump from the days 61-90 tier to the lifetime reserve tier is significant. And once you've used your 60 lifetime reserve days, they're gone permanently. This is not a figure that resets annually or per benefit period.

**Example:** Assume the Part A deductible is $1,632 (a figure CMS published for a recent year; verify the current amount at Medicare.gov). Someone admitted for a 75-day stay would owe: the full deductible once, plus 15 days of daily coinsurance at the days 61-90 rate. If that rate is roughly $408/day (again, verify current figures), those 15 days add about $6,120. Total out-of-pocket before any supplemental coverage: approximately $7,752. That's a real number for a stay most people would consider "just" two and a half months.

## Skilled Nursing Facility Costs Are Different

A common misconception: people assume that if Medicare covers their hospital stay, it also covers any rehab or nursing care afterward without additional cost. That's not right.

Part A does cover skilled nursing facility care, but only under specific conditions (you must have had a qualifying three-day inpatient hospital stay, among other requirements), and the cost structure is distinct from hospital costs:

- Days 1-20 in a SNF: $0 coinsurance (the SNF benefit does not require you to pay an additional SNF deductible, though the qualifying hospital stay must have occurred)
- Days 21-100: Daily coinsurance (a different, lower rate than the hospital coinsurance)
- Day 101 and beyond: Medicare pays nothing

The SNF benefit also resets with benefit periods, same as hospital coverage.

## Who Pays These Costs?

Most people with Medicare don't face these charges directly because they have additional coverage. The main options:

**Medigap (Medicare Supplement Insurance)** plans cover some or all of the hospital coinsurance, depending on the plan letter. Plan G, for example, covers the Part A coinsurance and hospital costs up to an additional 365 days after Medicare benefits run out. Plan N covers coinsurance but with some cost-sharing differences. These plans are standardized federally, so "Plan G" means the same coverage from any insurer, the only variable is the premium. The [AARP Medicare resource center](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/) has solid side-by-side plan comparisons if you want to review your options.

**Medicare Advantage (Part C)** plans replace Original Medicare and have their own cost-sharing structures. Some have per-day hospital copays that can actually be lower than Original Medicare's coinsurance during long stays. Some are higher. You can't compare them on a simple per-day basis without reading the Summary of Benefits for the specific plan.

**Employer or retiree coverage** may coordinate with Medicare and cover some or all of the gaps. Call your benefits administrator to ask specifically about hospital cost-sharing.

If you're uncertain which coverage fills the gap best in your situation, a free one-on-one consultation with a State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) counselor is worth more than most articles. Find your local SHIP at [shiphelp.org](https://www.shiphelp.org/).

## The 60-Day Gap Rule, Practically Speaking

Here's something that catches people off guard. If you're discharged to a skilled nursing facility after a hospital stay, the SNF days count toward your benefit period. You don't get a fresh 60-day benefit period clock just because you moved from hospital to SNF. The 60 consecutive days without inpatient care restarts only after you leave all institutional care.

**Example:** Someone spends 10 days in the hospital, then 25 days in a SNF, then goes home. The benefit period clock starts ticking the day they leave the SNF. Sixty days later, if they haven't been readmitted to any inpatient setting, a new benefit period would begin on any future hospitalization.

This matters most for people with chronic conditions who cycle in and out of care. If you're in this situation, tracking benefit periods isn't just academic, it directly affects whether you owe another deductible.
