Most people choosing between Medigap Plan G and Plan N are not confused about the Medicare basics anymore. They've done their homework. What they're stuck on is a specific question that sounds simple but isn't: "Is the cheaper plan actually worth it, or will I regret saving that money?"

That's exactly the right question to be sitting with. And I'm going to help you think through it the way I would with someone sitting across from me at my kitchen table.

## What These Two Plans Actually Cover (And What Separates Them)

Both Plan G and Plan N are Medigap (Medicare Supplement Insurance) plans. Medigap plans are sold by private insurance companies to fill in the gaps that Original Medicare (Part A and Part B) leaves behind, things like hospital deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments that would otherwise come out of your pocket.

Plan G is about as close to comprehensive coverage as you can get in today's Medigap world. Since Plan C and Plan F were closed to new enrollees in 2020, Plan G became the top-tier option for most people. Here's what it covers:

- The Medicare Part A (hospital insurance) deductible: $1,676 in 2025
- Part A coinsurance and hospital costs up to an additional 365 days after Medicare benefits run out
- Part A hospice care coinsurance or copayments
- Part B (outpatient insurance) coinsurance or copayments
- Part B excess charges (more on this in a moment)
- Skilled nursing facility care coinsurance
- The first three pints of blood
- 80% of foreign travel emergency costs, up to plan limits

The one thing Plan G does not cover is the Medicare Part B deductible, which is $257 in 2025. You pay that once a year and then Plan G takes over completely.

Plan N covers everything Plan G covers, with three differences. Plan N does not cover Part B excess charges. It has copayments of up to $20 for some office visits and up to $50 for emergency room visits that don't result in a hospital admission. And some Plan N policies may also require you to pay the Part B deductible, though this varies by insurer, so you'll want to check the specific policy.

That's the whole comparison, really. The question is whether those three differences matter for your life.

Here's what I tell people who ask me about cost first: the premium difference between Plan G and Plan N is not fixed. It depends on your age, where you live, which insurance company you choose, and whether you're applying through open enrollment or going through underwriting.

In many markets, Plan N runs $30 to $80 a month less than Plan G for a 65-year-old. In some regions I've seen the gap closer to $20. In others, it's over $100. Rates in Florida, for instance, tend to run much higher across the board compared to, say, Ohio or Indiana. A 65-year-old woman in Miami might pay $195 a month for Plan G and $145 for Plan N. That same woman in Columbus, Ohio might see $140 for Plan G and $105 for Plan N. The math changes depending on where you are.

This is why I'd encourage you to actually price both plans in your specific ZIP code rather than trusting any number you read in a general article, including this one. The [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) has a Medigap comparison tool on Medicare.gov, and it's genuinely useful for getting a real-world picture of what insurers in your area are charging.

If Plan N is $50 a month less expensive than Plan G, that's $600 a year. Over five years, that's $3,000 in premium savings, before you account for any out-of-pocket costs on Plan N. Whether those savings hold up depends on how you use your coverage.

## The Part B Excess Charge Problem (This Is the Sleeper Issue)

Most people I talk to are familiar with the copay difference. They're less familiar with excess charges, and I think that's where Plan N buyers sometimes get surprised later.

Medicare sets what it considers an approved amount for every medical service. Most doctors who accept Medicare have "accepted assignment," which means they've agreed not to charge more than that approved amount. But doctors are legally allowed to charge up to 15% above what Medicare approves. That extra 15% is called a Part B excess charge.

Plan G covers excess charges completely. Plan N does not, and you pay 100% of any excess charge yourself.

Now, here's where opinions diverge. Some counselors will say excess charges are rare and you shouldn't let fear of them drive you to a more expensive plan. And they're not entirely wrong. Excess charges have become less common as more physicians accept assignment. According to CMS data, the majority of Medicare-participating providers do not bill excess charges.

But. If you live somewhere with a high concentration of concierge practices or academic medical centers, or if you're dealing with a serious specialist situation, excess charges can add up quickly. I've sat with people who got surprised by $300 or $400 in excess charges after a few specialist visits they didn't anticipate. That's not catastrophic, but it erodes the Plan N savings.

My honest take: if you are in a major metropolitan area with lots of specialists, or if you have a chronic condition that sends you to a lot of different providers, find out whether the doctors you regularly see accept Medicare assignment before you commit to Plan N. You can check any specific doctor at Medicare.gov's physician search tool. It takes about two minutes and it's information worth having.

## Who Plan N Actually Works Well For

Plan N genuinely is a good fit for some people, and I don't want the nuances above to make it sound like a bad deal. It's not.

If you're in reasonably good health at 65, you see your primary care doctor a few times a year, your specialists (if any) all accept Medicare assignment, and you have a small emergency fund available for the occasional $20 copay, Plan N can represent real savings over time. The people I've seen happiest with Plan N are generally active, healthy retirees who wanted coverage for the catastrophic stuff, which both plans provide equally well, without paying for every-dollar coverage they don't need.

There's also a behavioral finance argument for Plan N that I find mildly compelling. Some research (it's not conclusive, but it's consistent enough to mention) suggests that people with first-dollar coverage use slightly more healthcare than they otherwise would. A small copay can prompt people to think twice about whether a visit is truly necessary. Whether that's a feature or a bug depends on your philosophy.

What I'd caution against is choosing Plan N purely because it's cheaper without thinking through your actual health picture. The $600 annual savings can vanish quickly if you have a year with several specialist visits, a trip to the ER that doesn't lead to admission, or a handful of excess charges.

## Who Should Strongly Consider Plan G

Honestly? Most people I work with who have even moderate health complexity end up being better served by Plan G.

If you're managing diabetes, heart disease, COPD, or any condition that involves frequent specialist care, Plan G's predictability is worth a lot. There's something real about knowing that once you've paid your $257 Part B deductible for the year, you're done. No copays, no excess charge surprises, no running calculations in your head every time you schedule an appointment.

People who've had cancer in the past tend to gravitate toward Plan G for this reason. Not because they expect their cancer to return, but because their care pattern already involves a lot of providers, a lot of visits, and a lot of specialists. The math works out more cleanly with Plan G even if the premium is higher.

There's also a timing consideration that doesn't get talked about enough. If you're enrolling during your Medigap Open Enrollment Period (the six-month window that starts when you turn 65 and are enrolled in Part B), insurers cannot charge you more based on your health history. That's when locking in Plan G at a reasonable rate makes the most sense, because if you start with Plan N and later want to switch to Plan G, you may face medical underwriting and could be denied or charged significantly more.

The [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) offers free one-on-one counseling to help you think through exactly this kind of timing decision, and I cannot recommend it enough. These are trained volunteer counselors, not salespeople. They don't earn a commission. It's one of the genuinely good resources available to Medicare beneficiaries, and most people don't know it exists.

## A Practical Way to Think Through Your Decision

Don't try to predict the future precisely. Instead, run two scenarios.

First: assume a "light use" year. You see your doctor three times, have one specialist visit, no ER trips. Add up what you'd pay out of pocket under Plan N (copays, any deductibles), subtract the premium savings versus Plan G, and see where you land.

Second: assume a more active medical year. An ER visit that doesn't turn into an admission. Four or five specialist visits with a provider who may bill excess charges. One or two more copays than you expected. Run the same math.

If Plan N still comes out ahead or roughly equal in the second scenario, it's probably the right choice for you. If Plan G closes the gap significantly in a busier health year, that peace of mind may be worth the higher premium.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I switch from Plan N to Plan G later if I want to?

You can try, but it's not guaranteed. Outside of your initial Medigap Open Enrollment Period, most states allow insurers to use medical underwriting, which means they can deny your application or charge you more based on your health history. A handful of states (Connecticut, Massachusetts, New York, Maine) have more protective rules. Check your state's rules before assuming a switch will be easy.

### Do Plan G and Plan N cover prescription drugs?

Neither plan covers prescription drugs. Medigap plans don't include drug coverage. For that, you'd need a separate Part D (prescription drug) plan, which you enroll in through a private insurer. Medicare.gov has a plan finder tool where you can compare Part D options based on the specific drugs you take.

### Are Plan G and Plan N the same regardless of which insurance company I buy from?

The benefits are standardized by federal law, so a Plan G from Aetna covers exactly the same things as a Plan G from Mutual of Omaha. What differs is the premium, the company's financial stability, their customer service reputation, and how they set rates as you age. It's worth comparing a few companies, not just going with whichever name you recognize first.

### Is Plan N available everywhere?

Plan N is widely available, but not every insurer offers it in every state or ZIP code. A few states (Massachusetts, Minnesota, and Wisconsin) have their own standardized Medigap systems that don't use the standard lettered plan structure at all. If you're in one of those states, you'll want to work with a counselor who understands your state's specific plan options.

### If I'm healthy now, is it smarter to wait and see which plan I need?

This is the one piece of advice I push back on most often. Your Medigap Open Enrollment Period, when you have guaranteed issue rights regardless of health, only lasts six months. Once it closes, you lose that protection in most states. Making a decision based on your health right now is actually the worst time to wait. Get enrolled in the plan that makes sense for your current situation and financial comfort level, knowing that Plan G offers more predictable costs if anything changes.
