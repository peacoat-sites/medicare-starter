You've just been told by your doctor that your work injury isn't going to get better. Maybe you hurt your back lifting equipment, lost partial use of your hand in a machine accident, or developed a permanent lung condition from years of chemical exposure. The workers' comp adjuster is suddenly calling more often, throwing around terms like "permanent and stationary" or "maximum medical improvement," and someone mentioned you might be eligible for a settlement. You don't know whether to feel relieved or terrified. ## What "Permanent Disability" Actually Means in Workers' Comp

Let's cut through the jargon first, because the terminology alone trips people up.

When your treating doctor says you've reached **maximum medical improvement (MMI)**, it means your condition has stabilized. You're not expected to get significantly better with further treatment. That doesn't necessarily mean you're 100% disabled or unable to work at all. It simply means the injury has reached its endpoint, medically speaking.

At that point, a workers' comp doctor, called an **Agreed Medical Examiner (AME)** or **Qualified Medical Evaluator (QME)** depending on your state and situation, will typically assess what's called your **permanent disability rating**. This rating is expressed as a percentage and reflects how much your injury limits your ability to work and function. A 20% permanent disability rating is very different from an 80% rating, and those numbers directly drive your settlement value.

What most people don't realize is that this rating is not automatically fair or final. Doctors who perform these evaluations work within specific guidelines, and those guidelines aren't always generous. The rating assigned to you can be contested. I've seen clients who were initially rated at 30% permanent disability get that number revised significantly upward after their attorney pushed for a second opinion or a more detailed functional capacity evaluation.

The two main categories you'll encounter are:

**Permanent Partial Disability (PPD):** You have a lasting impairment, but you can still do some form of work. This is the most common outcome in workers' comp permanent disability cases.

**Permanent Total Disability (PTD):** Your injury prevents you from performing any substantial gainful employment. This is rarer, harder to prove, and typically results in higher compensation.

Your state matters enormously here. Workers' comp is state-regulated, not federal (with a few exceptions like federal employees or longshore workers). A 50% permanent disability rating in California, for example, pays very differently than the same rating in Texas or Florida.

When your case reaches the permanent disability stage, you generally have two paths forward, and understanding both before choosing is critical.

**Stipulation with Request for Award (Stip):** This is an ongoing agreement where the insurance carrier pays you weekly permanent disability benefits for a set number of weeks, based on your rating. Your medical care often remains open, meaning the insurer continues to pay for treatment related to your injury. If your condition worsens, you may be able to go back and request more benefits.

**Compromise and Release (C&R):** This is a lump-sum settlement that closes out your entire claim, usually including future medical care. You get a check. The case is done. The insurance company's obligation ends.

The C&R is the one that scares me most when people rush into it. That lump sum can feel like a windfall, but once you sign, there's no going back. If your condition worsens and you need surgery five years from now, you're paying out of pocket unless you have other coverage.

Here's a practical way to think about it: a Stip keeps the door open; a C&R closes it permanently. Neither is inherently better. The right choice depends on your age, your health trajectory, whether you have Medicare or other health insurance, and your financial situation.

And speaking of Medicare: if you're a Medicare beneficiary or likely to become one within 30 months, a C&R settlement may require a **Medicare Set-Aside (MSA)**. The [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) has specific rules about protecting Medicare's interests in workers' comp settlements, and failing to account for this correctly can result in Medicare refusing to pay your injury-related medical bills until you've spent down your settlement funds. That's not a technicality. That's thousands of dollars of real-world risk.

## How Settlement Amounts Are Calculated (And Why the First Offer Is Usually Low)

Insurance adjusters are not your advocates. Their job is to close claims for as little money as possible. That's not a criticism; it's just the reality of how insurance companies operate. So the first offer you receive is almost never the best offer.

Permanent disability settlement values are influenced by several factors:

- Your permanent disability rating (the percentage)
- Your age and pre-injury wages
- Your state's specific benefit formula and maximum weekly benefit caps
- The strength of your medical documentation
- Whether there's any dispute about whether your injury is work-related
- Your future medical needs and their projected cost
- Any temporary disability benefits already paid

In a Compromise and Release, the lump sum is typically calculated to represent the present value of all future benefits the insurer would have owed you, discounted because they're paying it all upfront. They will also factor in their litigation risk. If there are weaknesses in your claim, they'll use those to drive the number down. If your documentation is airtight and your attorney is aggressive, that changes the calculation.

I can't give you a specific dollar figure to expect, and anyone who gives you a confident number without reviewing your actual case file is guessing. What I can tell you is that unrepresented claimants, on average, accept lower settlements. The data on this is consistent across multiple state studies.

## Step-by-Step: What to Do When You've Reached Maximum Medical Improvement

Here's a practical sequence to follow once your doctor has declared you at MMI and a permanent disability rating is on the table.

**Step 1: Get the full medical report.** Request a complete copy of your MMI determination and the permanent disability rating report. Read it carefully. Look for errors in your job description, your physical limitations, or your documented symptoms.

**Step 2: Don't accept or reject anything yet.** Insurance companies sometimes pressure injured workers to respond quickly. You almost always have time. Use it.

**Step 3: Consult a workers' comp attorney before signing anything.** Most workers' comp attorneys work on contingency, meaning they take a percentage of your settlement and you pay nothing upfront. The consultation is usually free. There is no reason not to get a professional opinion.

**Step 4: Understand your future medical needs.** Talk to your treating doctor about what ongoing care you're likely to need. Physical therapy? Pain management? Possible surgery? This matters enormously in calculating whether a C&R settlement is actually adequate.

**Step 5: Consider a Medicare Set-Aside if applicable.** If you're on Medicare or Medicare-eligible, consult with an attorney or MSA allocation company about whether your settlement needs one. CMS has review thresholds, but even if your settlement falls below them, an MSA may still be advisable.

**Step 6: If you're unsure, contact your state's SHIP program.** The [State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)](https://www.shiphelp.org/) provides free, unbiased counseling on Medicare and how it intersects with other benefits, including workers' comp settlements. This is especially valuable if you're approaching Medicare eligibility.

**Step 7: Review the settlement documents line by line.** Understand exactly what rights you're releasing. Some C&R agreements release only the workers' comp claim; others attempt to release related claims. Have an attorney explain every paragraph.

## What Happens to Medicare and Social Security If You Settle

This is where permanent disability workers' comp settlements get genuinely complicated, and where I've seen people get hurt badly by not knowing the rules.

**Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI):** If you're receiving SSDI and you receive a workers' comp settlement, your SSDI benefit may be reduced through a provision called the **workers' comp offset**. The Social Security Administration (SSA) limits the combined total of your SSDI and workers' comp benefits to 80% of your pre-disability earnings. If your lump-sum settlement pushes you over that threshold, SSA may prorate the settlement over your life expectancy and reduce your monthly SSDI check accordingly. Structuring your settlement language carefully can sometimes minimize this impact.

**Medicare coordination:** As mentioned above, CMS requires that Medicare's future interests be protected when workers' comp settles a claim that includes future medical expenses. If your case involves ongoing medical treatment, the settlement may need to allocate a specific portion to cover future injury-related care before Medicare steps in. Getting this wrong doesn't just affect you now; it can affect your coverage for years.

Neither of these issues is a reason to avoid settling. They're reasons to settle carefully, with professional guidance.

## FAQ

### Can I get a workers' comp settlement if I can still work?

Yes. Permanent Partial Disability settlements are specifically designed for people who have a lasting impairment but can still perform some type of work. Your settlement reflects the degree of your impairment and its impact on your earning capacity, not whether you can work at all. Many people return to work, sometimes in a different role or with accommodations, and still receive a PPD settlement.

### Do I need an attorney to settle a permanent disability workers' comp case?

You're not legally required to have one, but I'd strongly encourage it. The workers' comp system is complicated, the insurance company has professional adjusters and attorneys working against your interests, and studies consistently show that represented claimants receive higher settlements. Most workers' comp attorneys charge a contingency fee of around 10 to 15 percent, though this varies by state and is often capped by state law. The consultation is free. Use it.

### How long does a permanent disability workers' comp settlement take?

This varies widely. Once you've reached MMI and a rating has been established, a straightforward case might settle in a few months. Contested cases, especially those involving disputed ratings, future medical care, or Medicare Set-Aside requirements, can take a year or longer. Pushing your attorney for regular updates and responding promptly to document requests on your end can help keep things moving.

### Will a workers' comp settlement affect my health insurance?

A Compromise and Release settlement that closes your medical care means the workers' comp insurer no longer pays for treatment related to your injury. If you don't have other health insurance, that's a serious gap. If you're on Medicare, the Medicare Set-Aside arrangement is designed to address this gap for injury-related care. If you're not yet Medicare-eligible, you'll need to account for ongoing medical costs through private insurance, marketplace coverage, or Medicaid.

### What if I disagree with my permanent disability rating?

You have the right to challenge it. Depending on your state, this might involve requesting a second opinion from a different qualified medical evaluator, submitting a declaration of readiness to proceed to a workers' comp hearing, or having your attorney depose the rating physician. A contested rating case takes longer but can result in a significantly higher benefit if the original rating was genuinely too low. Don't assume the first number you're given is correct.
