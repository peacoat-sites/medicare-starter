Original Medicare covers a lot. But the gaps it leaves behind are real, and for many people, surprisingly expensive. The two biggest holes are the lack of a cap on out-of-pocket costs under [traditional Medicare](/traditional-medicare-has-no-spending-cap-a-new-bill-would-change-that/) and the near-complete absence of dental, vision, and hearing coverage. If you're only relying on Parts A and B, a serious illness or a couple of dental crowns can cost you thousands more than you planned.

Understanding where Original Medicare stops paying is not just academic. It's the reason Medigap (Medicare Supplement Insurance) and [Medicare Advantage plans](/best-medicare-advantage-plans-by-state/) exist, and knowing the specific gaps is the only way to decide which fix actually fits your situation.

## The Part A Gaps (Hospital Coverage)

Part A covers inpatient hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice, and some [home health](/medicare-home-health-care-cost/). The gaps are structured in a way that surprises almost everyone.

First, the hospital deductible. Part A charges a deductible per *benefit period*, not per calendar year. A benefit period starts the day you're admitted to a hospital or skilled nursing facility and ends after you've been out of a hospital or skilled nursing facility for 60 consecutive days. If you're hospitalized twice in one year with less than 60 days between stays, those count as the same benefit period and you pay one deductible. But if you're hospitalized, recover at home for 61 days, and then go back, you owe a second deductible. For 2026, verify the current deductible amount at [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) since it adjusts each year. The structure matters more than the exact dollar amount: it's not like a car insurance deductible that resets January 1 and then you're done.

After the deductible, you pay nothing for hospital days 1-60. Days 61-90 cost you a daily coinsurance amount. After day 90, you tap into a one-time reserve of 60 "lifetime reserve days," each with a higher daily coinsurance. Use all 60 and exhaust your benefit period days? You're on the hook for all hospital costs. That scenario is rare, but it's not impossible for someone with a prolonged illness.

Skilled nursing facility care has its own coinsurance structure. You pay nothing for the first 20 days, then daily coinsurance for days 21-100, and then everything after day 100. A long rehabilitation stay can get expensive fast.

## The Part B Gaps (Outpatient Coverage)

Part B covers doctor visits, outpatient procedures, durable medical equipment (think wheelchairs and oxygen), and preventive services. The gaps here are simpler to explain but just as significant.

You pay the Part B deductible once per year, then 20% of Medicare-approved costs for most services. That 20% sounds manageable until you do the math on a major outpatient procedure or a cancer treatment plan. There's no annual cap on what that 20% can add up to under Original Medicare alone.

**Example:** Assume you have a knee replacement done as an outpatient procedure. If Medicare's approved cost is $25,000, your 20% share is $5,000, plus the Part B deductible. If you then need cardiac imaging later that year, you owe 20% of that too. With two or three significant medical events in a year, your Part B exposure can exceed $10,000 or more, all with no ceiling.

Also worth knowing: if a provider doesn't accept Medicare assignment (meaning they don't agree to Medicare's approved rates), they can charge up to 15% above the Medicare-approved amount. That's called an "excess charge." Most providers do accept assignment, but not all, particularly some specialists and anesthesiologists.

## The Services Medicare Barely Touches

Routine dental care, most vision care, and hearing aids. These three categories cover a huge portion of what seniors actually need, and Original Medicare almost entirely excludes them.

Part A will pay for dental work that's incidental to a covered inpatient procedure (say, examining your mouth before heart surgery). Part B covers some eye exams for diabetic retinopathy or glaucoma, and pays for eyeglasses after certain cataract surgeries. That's essentially it. No cleanings, no fillings, no dentures, no routine eye exams, no hearing aids. The gap here is enormous in both health and financial terms.

| Service | Original Medicare Covers? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Routine dental exams | No | No coverage under Parts A or B |
| Dentures or implants | No | Even medically necessary cases rarely qualify |
| Routine vision exams | No | Exceptions: diabetic eye disease, post-cataract glasses |
| Hearing aids | No | Medicare Advantage plans vary widely here |
| Glasses or contacts | No | Limited exception after cataract surgery |
| Preventive dental (cleanings) | No | None under Original Medicare |

Medicare Advantage (Part C) plans from private insurers frequently include dental, vision, and hearing benefits, but the coverage depth varies dramatically. Some plans cover only basic cleanings and exams; others include major dental work up to a dollar limit. AARP's Medicare resource center at [aarp.org](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/) has good side-by-side explanations of what different plan types typically include, which can help you evaluate what you're actually getting before you enroll.

## Prescription Drugs: The Part D Gap

Part D covers outpatient prescription drugs through standalone plans (if you have Original Medicare) or through Medicare Advantage plans that include drug coverage. The gap that people talk about most is the coverage gap, historically called the "donut hole."

The good news: through legislation, the worst of the donut hole has been closed for most beneficiaries. As of 2026, there's a $2,100 annual out-of-pocket cap on covered Part D drugs. That's a meaningful change that limits your exposure for high-cost medications. What still catches people off guard is that the cap applies to covered drugs on your plan's formulary. If your drug isn't covered, you're paying full price, completely outside the cap structure.

Drug costs also depend heavily on which tier your medication falls into on your plan's formulary, whether you use a preferred pharmacy, and whether you qualify for Extra Help (also called the Low Income Subsidy). If your income and assets are limited, Extra Help can dramatically reduce your premiums, deductibles, and copays for Part D. You apply through the Social Security Administration, not Medicare, which surprises a lot of people.

## How People Actually Fill These Gaps

There are two main paths, and they don't mix.

**Medigap (Medicare Supplement Insurance):** These are standardized plans (labeled Plan A through Plan N in most states) sold by private insurers that work alongside Original Medicare. They typically cover all or most of your Part A and Part B cost-sharing, and some plans cover excess charges. They do not add dental, vision, or hearing benefits. Premiums vary by plan type, insurer, your age, and where you live. The best time to buy a Medigap policy is during your Medigap Open Enrollment Period, which runs for six months starting the month you turn 65 and are enrolled in Part B. During that window, insurers can't deny you coverage or charge you more based on health conditions. Miss it, and in most states you can be turned down or charged more based on your health history.

**Medicare Advantage (Part C):** These are all-in-one plans from private insurers approved by Medicare. They cover everything Parts A and B cover, usually include Part D drug coverage, and often add dental, vision, and hearing benefits. The trade-off is that you typically work within a network of providers, and prior authorization requirements can slow down care. You can't have both a Medigap plan and Medicare Advantage simultaneously. That's not a gray area.

A common mistake is assuming Medicare Advantage is always cheaper. The monthly premium may be low, but your actual costs depend on your copays, coinsurance, network restrictions, and how often you use care. Someone with chronic conditions who sees multiple specialists may fare better with a Medigap plan despite the higher premium.
