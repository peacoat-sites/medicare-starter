Picture this: you just turned 65, you have two envelopes on the kitchen table, and both promise to "fill the gaps" in Original Medicare. One has a $0 monthly premium. The other has a premium that could top $200 a month. Most people grab the free one without a second thought. I've seen that choice come back to bite people in very expensive ways, and I've also seen it work out beautifully. The difference almost always comes down to one thing: understanding what you're actually comparing before you sign anything.

Medicare Supplement insurance (often called Medigap) and Medicare Advantage (Part C) are not the same product dressed in different clothes. They work in completely different ways, they carry completely different cost structures, and the "right" answer depends entirely on your health, your finances, and where you live. Let's break it down honestly.

---

## How Each Option Actually Works

Before you can compare costs, you need a clear picture of what each plan does.

Original Medicare, meaning Part A (hospital insurance) and Part B (medical insurance), pays its share of your bills and then sends you the remainder. That remainder can be substantial. Part A has a per-benefit-period deductible, Part B has a 20% coinsurance with no annual out-of-pocket cap, and neither covers things like routine dental, vision, or hearing. If you want to understand the full scope of what Original Medicare covers and doesn't cover, the [Medicare Part B Explained](/medicare-part-b-explained/) guide on this site is a great place to start.

**Medigap** sits on top of Original Medicare. You keep Original Medicare as your primary coverage. The Medigap policy, sold by a private insurer, pays some or all of the costs that Medicare leaves behind, depending on which standardized plan letter you choose. Plans are standardized by the federal government, which means a Plan G from one insurer covers exactly the same benefits as a Plan G from another insurer.

**Medicare Advantage** takes a different approach entirely. Instead of adding to Original Medicare, it replaces it. A private insurance company contracts with [the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) to deliver your Medicare benefits, usually through a network of doctors and hospitals. Most Advantage plans bundle in Part D (prescription drug coverage) and extras like dental and vision. In exchange for those perks, you generally agree to work within a defined provider network and pay cost-sharing (copays, coinsurance, deductibles) at the time of service.

This is where things get real. Cost comparisons between these two paths are genuinely tricky because Medigap and Medicare Advantage charge you money at different times and in different ways.

**Medigap: Higher predictable monthly costs, lower surprise costs**

With a Medigap policy, your out-of-pocket exposure at the doctor's office or hospital is very limited, sometimes zero after you meet a small deductible. The trade-off is a real monthly premium you pay regardless of whether you use any healthcare that month. Premiums vary significantly by plan letter, your age, your state, your gender, and the insurer you choose. To get a realistic sense of what you'd actually pay, the [how much does Medigap cost](/how-much-does-medigap-cost/) breakdown on this site walks through pricing factors in detail.

The premium for a comprehensive plan like Plan G can feel steep, especially early in retirement when you might be healthy. But you're also buying certainty. I've worked with clients who paid that premium for five years barely using it, and then had a hospitalization in year six that would have cost them thousands under an Advantage plan. The premium suddenly looked like a bargain.

**Medicare Advantage: Low or $0 monthly premium, variable point-of-service costs**

The appeal is obvious. Many Advantage plans charge $0 in monthly premium beyond what you already pay for Part B. But "free" is a framing, not a reality. You still pay:

- The standard Part B premium each month (set by Medicare)
- Copays each time you see a doctor, visit a specialist, go to the ER, or get a procedure
- Coinsurance for hospital stays
- Costs that add up toward your plan's annual maximum out-of-pocket (MOOP) limit

The MOOP is important. Medicare requires all Advantage plans to cap your out-of-pocket spending, but that cap can be as high as several thousand dollars per year for in-network care, and higher or unlimited for out-of-network. If you have a serious illness or injury, you could hit that cap, and that is real money.

**A simple side-by-side comparison**

| Factor | Medigap (e.g., Plan G) | Medicare Advantage |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly premium | Higher (varies by plan/location) | Often $0 or low |
| Doctor choice | Any Medicare-accepting provider in the U.S. | Usually limited to network |
| Referrals needed | No | Often yes (HMO plans) |
| Out-of-pocket per service | Very low to $0 | Copays/coinsurance each visit |
| Annual out-of-pocket cap | Effectively very low | Required cap, but can be high |
| Prescription drug coverage | Separate Part D plan needed | Usually included |
| Extras (dental, vision) | Not included | Often included |
| Predictability of costs | High | Lower, depends on usage |

## Who Tends to Do Better With Each Option

Neither path is objectively superior. People who do well with Medigap tend to share a few characteristics: they have or anticipate significant healthcare needs, they travel frequently and want access to any Medicare-accepting provider nationwide, they strongly value knowing their costs in advance, or they simply dislike the idea of prior authorizations and network restrictions.

People who do well with Medicare Advantage tend to be relatively healthy and not heavy utilizers of care, or they live in an area with robust Advantage plan networks. They may also genuinely benefit from the bundled extras like dental and vision, which Medigap doesn't touch. And for people on tight fixed incomes, that lower monthly premium isn't trivial; keeping more cash available each month matters.

Geography matters more than most people expect. In major metro areas, Advantage networks are often wide and well-staffed. In rural areas, network limitations can become a serious problem. I've had clients in small towns who enrolled in an Advantage plan and discovered their local hospital wasn't covered.

If you're also juggling employer-sponsored coverage or other insurance, the decision tree gets more complicated. The [Medicare and employer insurance](/medicare-and-employer-insurance/) article on this site covers coordination-of-benefits rules that could affect your math.

## The Hidden Costs People Overlook

Sticker price comparisons miss several real-world factors that can flip the math entirely.

**Underwriting and guaranteed issue.** When you first enroll in Medicare and sign up for Medigap during your initial open enrollment window (the six months starting the month you turn 65 and are enrolled in Part B), insurers cannot turn you down or charge you more based on health conditions. Miss that window, and in most states you can be denied coverage or charged significantly more. Medicare Advantage has no such underwriting. You can join or switch during annual enrollment regardless of your health. This asymmetry is huge. Many people choose Advantage when they're healthy, develop a serious condition, and then can't get into a Medigap plan affordably. That's not a hypothetical; [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) explains these enrollment protections in detail, and reading them before you make a decision is genuinely worthwhile.

**Prescription drug costs.** Medigap doesn't cover drugs. You'll need a separate Part D plan, which adds another monthly premium and its own cost-sharing. Advantage plans usually bundle drug coverage in, but the formularies (the list of covered drugs) vary widely. If you take specific medications, check whether they're covered under any plan you're considering before you commit.

**Prior authorization.** Advantage plans can and do require prior authorization for certain procedures, specialist visits, and medications. This isn't just a bureaucratic annoyance; denials can delay care and create financial uncertainty. Medigap has no prior authorization requirements because it follows Original Medicare's coverage rules.

**Long-term cost trajectory.** Your health needs at 65 may look nothing like your health needs at 75 or 82. Medigap premiums increase as you age, which is a real cost. But Advantage plan copays can accumulate heavily if your health declines and you need frequent care. Running the numbers for a scenario of moderate to heavy healthcare use is not pessimistic planning; it's smart planning.

## Step-by-Step: How to Compare Your Actual Options

Doing this right takes maybe two hours, but it's two hours that could save you thousands. Here's a practical process:

1. **Get your current provider list.** Write down every doctor, specialist, and hospital you currently use or expect to use. This is your baseline.

2. **Check Advantage plan networks first.** On [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/), use the Plan Finder tool to search Advantage plans available in your zip code. For each plan you're considering, verify that your key providers are in-network.

3. **Estimate your healthcare usage.** Think honestly about how often you see doctors, whether you have any chronic conditions, and whether you've had hospitalizations or surgeries in recent years. If you use a lot of care, run the math on copays adding up toward the out-of-pocket max.

4. **Get Medigap quotes for your top plan letters.** Plan G is the most comprehensive plan available to new enrollees. Plan N is a popular option with slightly lower premiums and small copays. You can read a thorough comparison in the [Medicare Supplement Plan N Explained](/medicare-supplement-plan-n-explained/) guide. Get quotes from multiple insurers; premiums for the same plan letter vary more than people expect.

5. **Add the Part D cost to your Medigap math.** Look up stand-alone Part D plans in your area and factor that premium into your Medigap total monthly cost.

6. **Compare total annual cost in two scenarios:** one where you use moderate care (a few doctor visits, no hospitalizations) and one where you use heavy care (a hospitalization, specialist visits, imaging). The gap between these two scenarios often tells you a lot about which path carries more financial risk for you personally.

7. **Talk to a licensed independent insurance counselor or your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP).** SHIP provides free, unbiased counseling in every state. An independent broker who represents multiple insurers can also help you see your actual options without steering you toward one company.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I switch from Medicare Advantage to Medigap later if I change my mind?

You can technically apply for Medigap at any time, but unless you have a guaranteed issue right (triggered by specific life events, like your Advantage plan leaving your area), insurers in most states can review your medical history and either deny you coverage or charge you more. This is one of the biggest reasons to think carefully before leaving Medigap for Advantage in the first place. Your initial enrollment window is your strongest protection.

### Does Medicare Advantage always include dental and vision coverage?

Many Advantage plans include some dental and vision benefits, but "some" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Coverage limits, networks, and what's actually covered vary enormously from plan to plan. Some plans offer a modest annual dental allowance that covers cleanings but little else. Always read the Evidence of Coverage document for specifics before you assume the benefits will meet your needs.

### Is Medigap worth the higher premium if I'm currently healthy?

This is the most common question I hear, and there's no universal answer. What I tell clients is this: you're not just paying for the care you need today; you're buying access to guaranteed, stable cost-sharing for as long as you hold the policy. Whether that peace of mind is worth the premium depends on your financial cushion, your risk tolerance, and your health history. Someone with a strong family history of serious illness might feel very differently than someone with a clean bill of health at 65.

### What's the difference between an HMO and PPO under Medicare Advantage?

A Health Maintenance Organization (HMO) Advantage plan typically requires you to choose a primary care physician, get referrals to see specialists, and stay within the plan's network except in true emergencies. A Preferred Provider Organization (PPO) plan gives you more flexibility to see out-of-network providers, usually at a higher cost-sharing level. PPO plans tend to have higher premiums than HMOs. For a deeper look at how Advantage plan types work, the [Medicare Part C Explained](/medicare-part-c-explained/) article covers the structure in more detail.

### Does the $0 premium on many Advantage plans mean I pay nothing for Medicare?

No. You still pay the Part B premium every month regardless of which coverage path you choose. In 2025, the standard Part B premium is set by Medicare and applies to nearly all enrollees. The $0 premium advertised by many Advantage plans refers only to the plan's own additional premium on top of Part B. You always owe Part B.

The cost comparison between Medigap and Medicare Advantage isn't a math problem with one right answer. It's a personal finance and health decision that deserves real attention. Spending an hour with the Medicare Plan Finder, getting a few Medigap quotes, and talking through your specific situation with a SHIP counselor or independent broker will put you in a far stronger position than choosing based on the premium line alone. The "free" plan and the "expensive" plan can both be the wrong choice depending on who's choosing, and the right choice can save you far more than the premium difference over time.
