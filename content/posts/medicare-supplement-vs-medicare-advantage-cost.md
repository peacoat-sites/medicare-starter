Most people get this comparison backwards. They look at the monthly premium first, see that Medicare Advantage plans often cost $0 or close to it, and assume they're getting a better deal than the $150-$250 a month they'd pay for a Medigap (Medicare Supplement) plan. That math is understandable. It's also incomplete in a way that can cost you thousands when you're already sick.

Let me walk through what the actual cost difference looks like, when each option wins, and why the "right" answer depends almost entirely on how much healthcare you use.

## The Real Price Tag on a $0 Premium Plan

Medicare Advantage plans are sold by private insurers and must cover at least what Original Medicare covers, but they do it through a network and a cost-sharing structure that looks a lot more like employer insurance. UnitedHealthcare, Humana, Aetna, and other big carriers dominate this market. Many of their plans in 2024 carry a $0 monthly premium, which sounds extraordinary until you read the fine print on what you owe when you actually use the plan.

Here's what's buried in those plan documents: copays, coinsurance, and an out-of-pocket maximum that in 2024 can legally run as high as $8,850 for in-network care (and higher if you factor in out-of-network costs, which some plans have no cap on at all). That maximum exists, which is better than Original Medicare alone, but it's a number you'd hit surprisingly fast with a hospital stay, cancer treatment, or anything that requires specialist visits month after month.

A single inpatient hospital stay? You might owe $350 to $500 per day for the first few days under a typical Advantage plan. Outpatient surgery can mean 20% coinsurance on every line item. I've seen readers reach their out-of-pocket max in February.

The plan isn't cheating you. Those are the disclosed terms. But a $0 premium is genuinely $0 only in a year you barely use healthcare.

Medigap works differently at a structural level. You keep Original Medicare (Part A and Part B), and the supplement plan pays costs that Medicare leaves behind: deductibles, coinsurance, sometimes copays, depending on the plan letter you choose.

Plan G is the most popular option for people new to Medicare today (Plan F was the gold standard but closed to new enrollees in 2020). With Plan G in 2024, you're responsible for the Part B deductible ($240), and then the plan covers essentially everything else Medicare approves. That's it. No network to worry about, no referrals required, no prior authorization on most services.

The premium for Plan G varies by age, location, and insurer, but in most of the country you're looking at roughly $120 to $200 per month for someone in their mid-60s. That's $1,440 to $2,400 per year before you use a single service. Add the $240 Part B deductible and your worst-case annual out-of-pocket on covered services is essentially your premium.

Compare that to Advantage's worst case: up to $8,850 out-of-pocket, plus whatever you paid in premiums.

That's not a small difference. That's potentially $6,000 to $7,000 more exposure with the cheaper-looking plan.

## The Break-Even Math (And When Advantage Still Wins)

Here's where I'll be honest about the other side: if you're healthy and don't use much healthcare, Medicare Advantage can absolutely come out ahead financially. The math isn't difficult.

Say you choose a $0 premium Advantage plan and pay $500 in copays across the year. Total cost: $500. A Medigap Plan G at $150/month would have cost you $2,040 (premiums plus the Part B deductible), even though you barely used it. Advantage won by $1,540.

That's a real saving. Younger Medicare enrollees in good health make this calculation and it's not wrong.

The problem is that healthcare costs aren't linear and they tend to increase with age. The [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/) projects that Medicare beneficiaries' average healthcare spending rises significantly in their 70s and 80s. A plan structure that works well at 65 might be punishing at 78, when you're managing multiple conditions and seeing specialists regularly.

There's another wrinkle: if you try to switch from Medicare Advantage back to a Medigap plan after your initial enrollment window, you may face medical underwriting in most states. That means the insurer can review your health history and either charge you more or decline to cover you. So the "I'll switch later if I get sick" strategy can backfire badly. The [AARP Medicare resource center](https://www.aarp.org/health/medicare-insurance/) has good plain-language coverage of these switching rules, and it's worth reading before you decide.

## The Costs Nobody Mentions in the Brochure

A few line items that tend to get glossed over in any comparison:

Prescription drugs. Medigap plans don't cover drugs. You'll buy a standalone Part D plan on top of your supplement, usually $20 to $50 per month depending on your medications. Many Advantage plans bundle Part D in, which is part of why they can price so attractively. Factor that in before any comparison.

Dental, vision, hearing. Original Medicare covers almost none of this. Many Advantage plans include some dental and vision benefits, which is a genuine advantage, though the coverage limits are usually modest ($1,000 to $2,000 annually for dental is common). Medigap covers none of it. If you have significant dental needs, this can shift the math.

Travel and location. Medigap lets you see any doctor who accepts Medicare, nationwide, no referral needed. If you split time between Florida and Minnesota, or travel internationally, Medigap gives you flexibility that most Advantage plans can't match (some Advantage plans do offer emergency out-of-area coverage, but routine care almost always has to stay in network).

Prior authorization. This one frustrates people more than almost anything else. Advantage plans can and do require prior authorization for procedures, certain specialists, and equipment. A 2023 Senate Finance Committee report found that prior authorization denials in Medicare Advantage have increased substantially year over year. Medigap has essentially none of this. If Medicare approves it, your supplement pays.

## Which One Is Actually Cheaper Over Time?

Honestly, for someone who's managing chronic conditions, sees multiple specialists, or expects to need significant care in the coming years, Medigap's predictable cost structure is almost always the better financial bet. The premium certainty matters. You know your maximum annual exposure before January 1st. That's genuinely valuable when you're on a fixed income.

For someone in excellent health, 65 to 70, with few prescriptions and minimal specialist needs, Medicare Advantage can save real money in the short term. The question is whether you want to optimize for today or build in protection for the years when the math reverses.

One practical middle path: if you're in a state like New York or Connecticut that allows guaranteed issue for Medigap (meaning insurers can't deny you or charge more based on health), you have more flexibility to start with Advantage and switch later. In most other states, that flexibility disappears after your initial enrollment window closes.

If you're still shopping plans and want to compare specific options side by side, Medicare.gov's plan finder tool is the most reliable place to start. For a deeper read on how supplement plans are structured, AARP publishes a free Medicare guide each year that I recommend without hesitation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Medicare Advantage really free if it has a $0 premium?

No. The premium is $0, but you pay copays, coinsurance, and potentially thousands in out-of-pocket costs when you use care. The 2024 out-of-pocket maximum for in-network Medicare Advantage costs can reach $8,850, and out-of-network costs may have a separate or no cap at all.

### Can I switch from Medicare Advantage to a Medigap plan whenever I want?

In most states, no. Outside of your initial enrollment window and a few special circumstances, switching to Medigap typically requires medical underwriting, meaning insurers can deny coverage or charge higher premiums based on your health history. A few states (New York, Connecticut, Massachusetts) have stronger consumer protections here.

### Do Medigap plans cover prescription drugs?

No. Medigap plans cover Medicare cost-sharing (deductibles, copays, coinsurance) but not prescription drugs. If you have a Medigap plan, you'll need to buy a standalone Medicare Part D plan for drug coverage, typically $20 to $50 per month depending on your medications.

### Which is better for someone with a serious chronic illness?

Most of the time, a Medigap plan is financially safer for people managing ongoing conditions like heart disease, diabetes requiring specialist oversight, or cancer. The predictable cost structure and absence of prior authorization or network restrictions usually matter more than the premium savings from Advantage in those situations.

### Why do Medicare Advantage plans cost so little if the coverage is real?

The federal government pays private insurers a set amount per enrollee to provide Medicare-equivalent coverage. Insurers that manage costs efficiently (through networks, prior authorization, and healthy enrollment pools) can price premiums at $0 and still profit. It's not a trick, but it does explain why cost control is built into the product's DNA in ways that Medigap's isn't.
