Medicare does not cover 100 percent of your healthcare costs. Full stop. That's the single most important thing you can know going in, and it's the thing the system never quite states plainly enough.

Most people come into Medicare expecting something close to full coverage because they've paid into it their entire working lives. That's a completely reasonable assumption. It's also wrong, and finding out the hard way, after a hospital stay or a surgery, costs real money. I've talked with hundreds of seniors over the years who were blindsided by bills they genuinely didn't know were coming.

So let's be specific about what Medicare actually covers, where the gaps are, and what you can do about them.

---

## The Basic Structure: Why 100% Was Never the Design

Original Medicare, the federal program administered by the [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/), is made up of two main parts. Part A covers hospital stays, skilled nursing facility (SNF) care, hospice, and some home health services. Part B covers doctor visits, outpatient procedures, lab work, durable medical equipment, and preventive services.

Here's what most explainers gloss over: Part B covers 80 percent of approved costs for most services, after you've met your annual deductible (which is $240 in 2024). You pay the remaining 20 percent. There is no cap on that 20 percent. None. If you have a $200,000 cancer treatment, your share of that could theoretically be $40,000, and Medicare won't stop you from owing it.

Part A has its own cost-sharing structure that works differently. There's a deductible per benefit period (not per year, which is its own source of confusion), and then daily coinsurance charges kick in after a certain number of hospital days. For a long hospitalization, those daily charges add up fast.

The point is: Original Medicare was designed as a cost-sharing program, not comprehensive coverage. The government covers a significant chunk; you cover the rest. That structure has been baked in since 1965.

The 20 percent Part B coinsurance gets the most attention, but there are other gaps worth knowing about.

**Prescription drugs.** Original Medicare (Part A and Part B) doesn't cover most outpatient prescription drugs. That's what Part D is for. Part D is a separate plan you purchase from a private insurer. Without it, you pay full price at the pharmacy. This seems obvious once you know it, but a surprising number of newly enrolled beneficiaries miss the enrollment window and get hit with a lifetime late-enrollment penalty.

**Dental, vision, and hearing.** Original Medicare doesn't cover routine dental care, eyeglasses, or hearing aids. None of it. This is a significant gap that gets almost no attention relative to how much it costs seniors. Hearing aids alone can run $3,000 to $7,000 a pair. AARP and other advocacy groups have been pushing for coverage expansion for years, and it remains a political football.

**Skilled nursing facility care beyond 20 days.** Part A covers the first 20 days of a skilled nursing facility stay at 100 percent (after you've met the Part A deductible for the benefit period). Days 21 through 100 come with a daily coinsurance charge ($200 per day in 2024). After day 100, Medicare pays nothing. If you or a family member is in a rehabilitation facility after a major surgery, those costs can become significant very quickly.

**Foreign travel.** With very few exceptions, Medicare doesn't cover care you receive outside the United States. If you spend winters in another country, or travel internationally, you need supplemental coverage or travel health insurance.

## Three Ways People Actually Fill the Gaps

This is where the real decision-making happens, and honestly, the options aren't equally good for everyone. Here's how I'd break it down.

**Medicare Supplement Insurance (Medigap).** These are private insurance plans sold to work alongside Original Medicare. A good Medigap plan, specifically Plan G (the most popular option after Plan C and F were closed to new enrollees), covers the Part A deductible, the Part B coinsurance, skilled nursing coinsurance, and foreign travel emergency care. With Plan G, your out-of-pocket exposure on covered services shrinks to just the Part B deductible (that $240) and then essentially nothing else for Medicare-covered care.

Medigap Plan G is, in my view, the closest thing to "100 percent coverage" that actually exists within the Medicare system for covered services. It won't cover dental, vision, hearing, or Part D drugs, and you'll still need separate coverage for those. But for hospital and doctor costs, it provides real financial predictability.

The catch: Medigap premiums vary by location, age, and insurer. In many parts of the country, Plan G runs $120 to $200 per month for a 65-year-old. That's a real cost. You also need to buy a separate Part D drug plan.

**Medicare Advantage (Part C).** These are all-in-one private plans that replace Original Medicare entirely. They're required to cover at least what Original Medicare covers, and many include extra benefits like dental, vision, hearing, and Part D drug coverage. Many have $0 premiums.

Here's where I'll give you a frank opinion: Medicare Advantage plans look great on paper and can work well if you're healthy and stay within your plan's network. But they use prior authorization for many procedures, meaning the plan can delay or deny coverage for treatments your doctor recommends. Networks can be narrow. And if you develop a serious illness, you may find the cost-sharing and approval processes considerably more difficult than Original Medicare with a Medigap supplement.

That's not a reason to avoid Advantage plans entirely; they're the right choice for a lot of people. But the "zero premium" framing papers over real trade-offs.

**Medicaid.** If your income and assets are below certain thresholds, you may qualify for both Medicare and Medicaid simultaneously. People in this category are called "dual eligibles," and Medicaid can cover Medicare's premiums, deductibles, and cost-sharing. If you qualify, this is genuinely as close to comprehensive coverage as Medicare gets. [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) has a tool to check if you might qualify for programs that help cover costs.

## What "100 Percent" Actually Looks Like in Practice

Let me give you a realistic scenario. Suppose you're 68, enrolled in Original Medicare with Plan G Medigap and a Part D drug plan. You have a hip replacement. Here's roughly how costs flow:

The surgery, hospital stay, and follow-up care get billed to Medicare. Medicare applies its approved amounts. You pay the Part B deductible for the year ($240 if you haven't already paid it). Plan G covers the rest of the Medicare-approved costs. You pay your Part D plan's cost-sharing for any prescriptions.

What you don't pay: 20 percent of the surgeon's bill, the hospital coinsurance, the skilled nursing coinsurance if you need rehab. Those go to Plan G.

What Medicare still won't cover regardless of your Medigap plan: any dentistry related to the procedure (if applicable), hearing aids if you need them post-anesthesia (it happens), or drugs that fall outside your Part D formulary.

No plan makes everything free. But with the right combination, "100 percent of covered services" is achievable for most routine and catastrophic medical care.
