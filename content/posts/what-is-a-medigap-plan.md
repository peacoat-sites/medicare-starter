Imagine you just turned 65, enrolled in Original Medicare, and felt pretty good about your coverage, until your neighbor mentioned she paid almost nothing out of pocket after her knee replacement, while your cousin paid thousands for the same surgery. Same Medicare. Wildly different bills. The difference, in most cases, comes down to one thing: your neighbor probably had a Medigap plan, and your cousin probably didn't.

## What Medigap Actually Is (And What People Get Wrong About It)

Most people assume Medicare covers "most" of their healthcare costs. I'll be honest, that assumption costs seniors real money every year. Original Medicare, which is Part A (hospital insurance) and Part B (medical insurance), actually leaves some significant gaps. We're talking deductibles, copayments, coinsurance, and in some cases, no out-of-pocket maximum at all. That last part is the one that shocks people most.

Medigap, also called Medicare Supplement Insurance, is private health insurance designed specifically to fill those gaps. You pay a monthly premium to a private insurance company, and in return, that plan picks up costs that Original Medicare leaves behind. The name is literal. It supplements Medicare. It doesn't replace it.

What surprised me when I first started helping clients with this was how standardized Medigap plans actually are. The federal government, through the [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://www.cms.gov/), defines exactly what each plan type must cover. So a Plan G from Humana and a Plan G from Blue Cross Blue Shield have to offer the same core benefits. The only real difference between carriers is price and customer service.

One important thing to understand upfront: Medigap only works alongside Original Medicare. You cannot use a Medigap plan if you're enrolled in Medicare Advantage (Part C). These are two completely different paths.

There are ten standardized Medigap plan types available in most states, identified by letters: A, B, C, D, F, G, K, L, M, and N. A few things to know before we go through them.

Plans C and F are no longer available to people who became eligible for Medicare on or after January 1, 2020. If you were already enrolled in one before that date, you can keep it. But new enrollees can't sign up for those plans anymore, because they covered the Part B deductible, which Congress decided to phase out for new enrollees.

| Foreign travel emergency | No | Yes (80%) | No | Yes (80%) |
| Out-of-pocket limit (2024) | None | None | ~$7,060 | None |

Plan G is the most popular option for new enrollees right now. The only thing it doesn't cover compared to the old Plan F is the Part B deductible, which in 2024 is $240 per year. You pay that once annually and then Plan G handles the rest. For many people, the premium difference between Plan F (if they qualify) and Plan G is less than that deductible anyway.

Plan N is worth a close look if you're relatively healthy and want a lower monthly premium. You'll pay small copays (up to $20 for office visits, up to $50 for emergency room visits that don't result in inpatient admission), and you're exposed to Part B excess charges, which are the extra amount a doctor who doesn't accept Medicare assignment can legally charge. Not all doctors bill excess charges, but it's a real risk in some specialties and markets.

## When You Can (And Can't) Enroll Without Medical Underwriting

This is where things get genuinely important and where I've seen people make expensive, hard-to-fix mistakes.

Your golden window is called the Medigap Open Enrollment Period. It starts the month you turn 65 AND are enrolled in Medicare Part B, and it lasts for six months. During this window, insurance companies cannot deny you coverage, charge you more because of a health condition, or make you wait for benefits. They have to take you, period.

Outside of that window, in most states, insurers can use medical underwriting. That means they can look at your health history, charge higher premiums, deny you coverage entirely, or impose waiting periods for pre-existing conditions. If you have diabetes, heart disease, a history of cancer, or dozens of other conditions, you might find it very difficult or very expensive to get a Medigap plan outside of open enrollment.

There are some Protected Enrollment Rights (also called guaranteed issue rights) that kick in under specific circumstances, like if you lose employer coverage, if your Medicare Advantage plan leaves your area, or if you move. [Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/) maintains an updated list of exactly which situations qualify. It's worth reviewing if you're considering switching.

A few states, including New York, Massachusetts, and Connecticut, have their own continuous open enrollment rules that are more consumer-friendly. If you live in one of those states, the picture is different. But don't assume, check.

## How Medigap Pricing Works (And Why the Same Plan Varies So Much)

You might get quotes for the same Plan G from three different companies and see premiums ranging from $90 to $250 per month. That range is real. How is that possible if the benefits are identical?

The answer is in how companies set their premium pricing structures. There are three methods:

**Community-rated:** Everyone pays the same premium regardless of age. Premiums can still increase over time due to inflation, but your age alone doesn't raise your rate. Generally better for older enrollees.

**Issue-age-rated:** Your premium is based on your age when you first buy the policy. Younger buyers lock in lower rates. Premiums can still increase, but not because you got older.

**Attained-age-rated:** Your premium increases as you age. Often the lowest starting premiums, but the costs grow over time. This is the most common pricing model and the one that can surprise people 10 to 15 years into retirement.

Knowing which pricing method a company uses is genuinely important for long-term planning. An attained-age plan might look cheap at 65 and become expensive by 75. I've seen clients who didn't ask this question end up with sticker shock in their late 70s.

Beyond the pricing method, companies also have different loss ratios, administrative costs, and claims histories that affect how they raise rates over time. Looking at a company's rate increase history over the past five years is something brokers can usually pull for you.

## A Step-by-Step Guide to Choosing and Enrolling in a Medigap Plan

If you're approaching 65 or currently in your open enrollment window, here's how to approach this practically:

1. **Confirm your Medicare Part B start date.** Your Medigap open enrollment window starts the month Part B becomes effective, not necessarily your birthday month. These can differ.

2. **Decide whether Medigap or Medicare Advantage fits your situation.** These two paths are fundamentally different. Medigap gives you maximum flexibility with providers nationwide and predictable costs. Medicare Advantage typically has lower premiums but network restrictions and variable out-of-pocket costs. There's no single right answer.

3. **Pick your plan letter.** For most healthy people starting today, Plan G or Plan N are the most logical starting points. Plan G offers near-comprehensive coverage. Plan N offers meaningful savings if you're comfortable with small copays and the excess charge exposure.

4. **Get quotes from multiple carriers.** Use a licensed broker who works with multiple companies, not a captive agent who only sells one company's products. The premiums vary significantly, and a broker can show you rate increase histories.

5. **Ask the pricing model question directly.** "Is this plan community-rated, issue-age-rated, or attained-age-rated?" Write down the answer.

6. **Check financial strength ratings.** Look up the insurer on AM Best or Moody's. You want a company that will be around and solvent for decades.

7. **Apply and confirm your effective date.** Make sure your Medigap coverage starts on the same date your Part B starts, or shortly after. You don't want a gap.

8. **Remember Part D.** Medigap plans do not cover prescription drugs. You'll need to enroll in a separate Part D plan for medication coverage. Don't overlook this step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Medigap cover prescription drugs?

No. Medigap plans do not include prescription drug coverage. That's handled separately through Medicare Part D plans, which you purchase from private insurers. If you want comprehensive coverage, you'll need both a Medigap plan and a standalone Part D plan. The two work alongside Original Medicare.

### Can I have both a Medigap plan and Medicare Advantage at the same time?

No, and the rules here are firm. Medigap only works with Original Medicare (Parts A and B). If you're enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan, you can't use a Medigap policy. Insurers are actually prohibited from selling you a Medigap plan if they know you're in Medicare Advantage. These are separate coverage approaches, not layers you can stack.

### What happens if I miss my Medigap open enrollment window?

In most states, you can still apply for a Medigap plan after your open enrollment period ends, but insurers can use medical underwriting. That means they can reject your application, charge higher premiums based on your health history, or impose waiting periods. The research here is genuinely mixed on how often people get rejected versus approved at higher rates. It depends heavily on your state, the insurer, and your specific health history. This is why acting during the open enrollment window matters so much.

### Are Medigap premiums tax deductible?

Potentially, yes. Medigap premiums may be deductible as a medical expense on your federal taxes, but only if your total unreimbursed medical expenses exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, and only if you itemize deductions rather than taking the standard deduction. For most seniors, this threshold isn't reached, but it's worth discussing with a tax professional if you have significant medical costs.

### Does Medigap cover dental, vision, or hearing?

Standard Medigap plans do not cover dental, vision, hearing, or long-term care. Those are notable gaps that Original Medicare also doesn't cover. Some insurers offer riders or bundled packages that add some of those benefits, but they're not standardized Medigap benefits. If dental and vision coverage are important to you, you'll need to look at standalone dental and vision plans separately.
