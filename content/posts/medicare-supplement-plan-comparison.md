## What Medigap Actually Is (And What It Isn't)

[Original Medicare](/ai-prior-auth-in-original-medicare-what-wiser-has-done-so-far/), meaning Part A (hospital insurance) and Part B (medical insurance), covers a lot. But it leaves gaps. Real ones. Part A has a hospital deductible of $1,676 per benefit period as of 2026. Part B has a 20% coinsurance that has no cap. No cap. That means if you're diagnosed with something serious and rack up $200,000 in outpatient bills, you're on the hook for $40,000. I've seen it happen. The person who comes to mind most clearly was a retired schoolteacher named Margaret, from a small town in Ohio. She had original Medicare and no supplement. One cardiac event, and her savings took a hit she never fully recovered from.

Medigap policies are sold by private insurance companies and are designed to pay those leftover costs that original Medicare doesn't cover. They're standardized by the federal government, which means Plan G from Aetna covers exactly the same benefits as Plan G from Mutual of Omaha. The only real differences between companies are price, customer service reputation, and how they calculate premium increases over time.

One thing Medigap is NOT: it's not Part D (prescription drug coverage), and it doesn't work with Medicare Advantage (Part C). If you enroll in a Medicare Advantage plan, you don't need, and actually can't use, a Medigap policy alongside it. I see people get tripped up on this regularly.

There are technically ten Medigap plan letters still available to new enrollees: A, B, D, G, G-High Deductible, K, L, M, and N. (Plans C and F exist but are only available to people who became Medicare-eligible before January 1, 2020.)

Honestly? Most people are choosing between Plan G and Plan N. Those two together account for the overwhelming majority of new Medigap enrollments, and for good reason.

*Plan N covers Part B coinsurance except for copays up to $20 per office visit and up to $50 for emergency room visits that don't result in inpatient admission.

The reason Plan G has become the dominant choice since Plan F's phase-out is simple: it covers virtually everything except the Part B deductible ($257 in 2026). You pay that one annual deductible yourself, and then Medicare and your Medigap policy handle the rest. Full stop. Many people find that mental simplicity alone is worth the slightly higher premium compared to Plan N.

Plan N makes sense if you're generally healthy, don't see specialists frequently, and want to save $30-$60/month on premium in exchange for accepting those small office visit copays and some exposure to Part B excess charges. The excess charge piece matters more than people think. Doctors who don't accept Medicare assignment can charge up to 15% above the Medicare-approved amount. If you see a lot of out-of-network providers, Plan G's coverage of those excess charges starts to look very valuable.

## The Enrollment Window You Absolutely Cannot Afford to Miss

Six months. That's your window. It starts the month you turn 65 and are enrolled in Medicare Part B. During those six months, you have guaranteed issue rights under federal law, which means no insurance company can reject you, charge you more, or make you wait for coverage because of a pre-existing condition. Hypertension, diabetes, cancer history, doesn't matter. They have to take you, at their standard rate.

After that window closes, the rules change completely. Most states allow insurers to use medical underwriting, meaning they can ask about your health history and either deny you or charge significantly higher premiums. I've talked with people who developed a condition, tried to switch plans two years later, and couldn't get coverage at any reasonable price. It's one of the most preventable financial mistakes I see.

There are some limited special guaranteed-issue situations: losing employer coverage, moving out of your plan's service area, your insurer going bankrupt. But don't count on these. Enroll when you first become eligible if at all possible.

A couple of scenarios to put numbers to this:

**Scenario 1:** A 65-year-old woman in Phoenix enrolls in Plan G during her guaranteed issue window at $158/month. One year later she's diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes. Her premium stays the same. She continues to have full coverage. Total premium investment in year one: $1,896. Her total out-of-pocket for Medicare costs that year: $257 (the Part B deductible). Without Medigap, her cost-sharing for multiple specialist visits and lab work would have exceeded $1,400.

**Scenario 2:** A 68-year-old man in Tampa waits, feels healthy, skips Medigap. At 71 he has a hip replacement. Hospital stay plus post-acute skilled nursing facility care generates approximately $9,800 in Medicare cost-sharing. He now tries to enroll in Plan G but is declined due to a cardiac history identified during the hip surgery workup. He ends up in a high-risk pool paying $340/month.

**Scenario 3:** A 65-year-old in Chicago chooses Plan N at $119/month instead of Plan G at $161/month, saving $42/month ($504/year). She has 8 specialist visits annually, paying $20 each ($160/year in copays). Net savings versus Plan G: $344/year, as long as she doesn't encounter Part B excess charges.

## What No One Tells You About Pricing

Here's what used to trip me up when I was newer to this work: the plan letter is standardized, but the premium calculation method is not. Insurance companies use three different pricing structures, and it matters a lot for what you'll pay decades from now.

**Community-rated** plans charge everyone the same premium regardless of age. Tends to cost more at 65, but doesn't increase simply because you're getting older.

**Issue-age-rated** plans base your premium on the age when you first bought the policy. Premiums don't increase as you age, though they can still increase for inflation and other factors.

**Attained-age-rated** plans base your premium on your current age. Usually the cheapest at 65, but premiums increase every year as you get older. Over a 20-year retirement, attained-age plans frequently end up being the most expensive option.

The catch is that most companies selling Medigap use attained-age pricing because it's the most attractive entry price. When I look at rate filings from carriers, the difference between a 65-year-old's premium and a 75-year-old's premium for the same plan letter can be 40-60% more. That's a meaningful long-term cost that doesn't show up in the comparison chart you see at enrollment.

AARP's Medicare resource center has a plain-language guide on pricing structures that I point readers to often, because understanding this distinction genuinely changes what "cheap" means over a 20-year horizon.

## How to Actually Compare Plans in Your Area

Don't rely on a single quote. I've watched people pay $50/month more than necessary because they got one quote, thought it seemed reasonable, and signed up. Identical plan letters with identical benefits. Just different prices.

The official Medicare Plan Finder at Medicare.gov lets you compare Medigap policies available in your ZIP code. You can also call your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) at any point. SHIP counselors are paid by the state, not by insurance companies, so there's zero incentive to steer you toward any particular carrier. (Find your state's SHIP program at shiphelp.org.) I genuinely believe everyone approaching Medicare eligibility should have at least one conversation with a SHIP counselor before they sign anything.

When you're comparing quotes, get at least three companies for the same plan letter. Look at the carrier's rate increase history. A company that has raised rates 8-10% annually for the past five years is a different proposition from one averaging 3-4%. Some state insurance departments publish this history online; your state's insurance commissioner website is the place to check.

One small detail that trips people up during the application process: you'll almost always be asked for your Medicare Beneficiary Identifier (MBI) number, which replaced Social Security numbers on Medicare cards a few years back. Have your red, white, and blue Medicare card in front of you when you call.
