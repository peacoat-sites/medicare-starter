Most people sit down to compare [Medicare supplement](/best-medicare-supplement-companies/) plans and walk away more confused than when they started. I've watched it happen in my office probably a thousand times. Someone comes in with a stack of mailers, all promising "the best coverage," and they genuinely can't tell whether Plan G and Plan N are meaningfully different or whether one company's Plan G is somehow better than another's. It's a fair question, and the answer is more useful than most of what you'll read online.

Here's what I want you to understand before we get into anything else: Medicare Supplement insurance (also called Medigap) plans are standardized by the federal government. That means a Plan G sold by Mutual of Omaha covers exactly the same things as a Plan G sold by AARP/UnitedHealthcare or Blue Cross Blue Shield of Texas. Exactly. The federal law governing standardization doesn't allow any wiggle room there. What differs between companies is the premium you pay, the customer service you get, and occasionally some small extras like a gym membership or a nurse hotline. So when you're comparing [Medigap plans](/best-medigap-plans-2026/), you're really doing two separate comparisons: which plan letter is right for you, and then which company to buy that letter from.

## The Plans That Actually Matter in 2026

There are technically ten standardized [Medigap plan](/what-is-a-medigap-plan/) letters available in most states: A, B, C, D, F, G, K, L, M, and N. But I'll be straight with you: most people should focus on two or three of them. Plans C and F are no longer available to people who became eligible for Medicare after January 1, 2020, so if you're newly enrolling, those are off the table.

Plan G is the gold standard right now. It covers everything Medicare Part A (hospital insurance) and Part B (medical insurance) don't pay, with one exception: the Part B deductible, which is $257 in 2026. You pay that once per year, and then Plan G picks up the rest. No copays at the doctor, no 20% coinsurance on outpatient care, no Part A hospital deductible of $1,676 per benefit period. For people who see specialists regularly or who've had a serious illness, Plan G is the closest thing to "set it and forget it" coverage.

Plan N costs less per month, usually $30 to $70 less than Plan G depending on where you live. The trade-off is that you'll pay up to $20 for office visits and up to $50 for emergency room visits that don't result in a hospital admission. There's also something called "excess charges" that Plan N doesn't cover but Plan G does. Excess charges happen when a provider doesn't accept Medicare's approved amount as full payment; they can legally charge up to 15% more. In practice, this is mostly a concern in a handful of states, and most doctors accept Medicare assignment. But it's something to check in your area before choosing Plan N.

Plan K and Plan L are cost-sharing plans with annual out-of-pocket limits. They're cheaper monthly but leave you paying a portion of many costs. I rarely see these work well for people with ongoing health needs. Plan A and B cover very little beyond what Original Medicare already provides. Honestly, if someone's steering you toward Plan A or Plan B as your main supplement, get a second opinion.

## How Premiums Actually Work (and Why Your Neighbor Pays Less)

This is where I see people get genuinely tripped up. Two people with identical Plan G policies can pay very different premiums, and it's not just because of age. Insurance companies use one of three pricing methods:

**Issue-age rated:** Your premium is based on how old you are when you buy the policy. It goes up only with inflation over time, not because you're getting older. This is generally the best deal long-term.

**Attained-age rated:** Your premium goes up every year as you age, in addition to any inflation increases. This is the most common method. Policies look cheap at 65 but can become expensive in your 70s and 80s.

**Community rated (no-age rated):** Everyone in the same area pays the same premium regardless of age. This often looks expensive at 65 but becomes the best deal later.

I made this mistake myself when I first started in this field. I helped someone pick the cheapest-looking Plan G without asking the insurer how they priced it. She had an attained-age policy, and by 78, her premium had climbed more than $80 a month beyond what community-rated plans were charging in her county. We switched her during an open-enrollment window, but not every state guarantees that option. Ask the company directly: "Is this policy issue-age rated, attained-age rated, or community rated?" If the sales rep can't answer that clearly, walk away.

## The Enrollment Timing You Cannot Afford to Miss

Your Medigap Open Enrollment Period (OEP) is the six months starting the first day of the month you're both 65 and enrolled in Medicare Part B. During this window, no insurer can deny you coverage or charge you more because of a pre-existing condition. None. It's the one time in most people's lives when Medigap is guaranteed-issue, regardless of health history.

After that window closes, you're at the mercy of medical underwriting in most states. I've seen people get turned down for Plan G because of controlled diabetes, a history of COPD, or even sleep apnea. The insurer reviews your medical records and can say no or charge you significantly more. A few states (Connecticut, Massachusetts, New York, and Maine as of this year) have year-round guaranteed-issue rights for Medigap, but those are exceptions.

There are also Special Enrollment Periods that can apply, such as when a Medicare Advantage plan leaves your area or you move. But these are narrower than people think. Don't count on them as a backup plan.

One thing most people don't realize: if you're turning 65 but still have employer coverage and aren't enrolling in Part B yet, your six-month Medigap OEP hasn't started. It begins when you actually enroll in Part B, not on your 65th birthday. That's actually good news for people who work past 65.

## Comparing Plans Side by Side

Here's a quick look at how the most common plans stack up on the benefits that matter most for most people:

| Benefit | Plan G | Plan N | Plan K | Plan A |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Part A hospital coinsurance | 100% | 100% | 50% | 100% |
| Part A deductible ($1,676/benefit period) | Yes | Yes | 50% | No |
| Part B coinsurance/copay | 100% | 100% (with copays) | 50% | No |
| Part B deductible ($257/year) | No | No | No | No |
| Part B excess charges | Yes | No | No | No |
| Skilled nursing facility coinsurance | Yes | Yes | 50% | No |
| Foreign travel emergency | 80% | 80% | No | No |
| Annual out-of-pocket limit | None | None | $7,220 | None |

Source: CMS standardized benefit charts, current as of July 2026.

A practical example of how this plays out: Maria, a 66-year-old in Phoenix with rheumatoid arthritis, sees her rheumatologist six times a year and gets quarterly lab work. She enrolled in Plan G during her OEP and pays $148/month. In a typical year, her out-of-pocket costs are just the $257 Part B deductible. Under Plan N, she'd save maybe $45/month on premium but would pay $20 per specialist visit, totaling $120 in office visit copays alone. Net difference: roughly $420/year in her favor on Plan G. For someone with fewer doctor visits, Plan N often comes out ahead.

Contrast that with David, a healthy 65-year-old retired teacher in Ohio who sees his primary care doctor twice a year and rarely needs specialist care. He chose Plan N at $109/month. Over his first two years, he paid $240 in total copays and saved more than $1,100 compared to Plan G premiums in his area. The math worked for him.

## Picking the Right Company

Once you've settled on a plan letter, you're shopping on price, financial strength, and rate stability. The coverage is the same.

I'd check AM Best ratings (look for A or better) and look at the insurer's history of rate increases in your state. Your state insurance department's website often publishes rate filing history, and that's more revealing than any salesperson's pitch. Companies that have hiked rates 8-10% annually are a pattern, not a fluke.

Medicare.gov's plan comparison tool (at medicare.gov) lets you compare Medigap plans available in your zip code, including premium estimates. It's not perfect, and I'd always verify directly with the insurer or a licensed broker, but it's a solid starting point. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (cms.gov) also publishes the official Choosing a Medigap Policy guide, which is free and detailed.

A few things I tell everyone: call and get quotes from at least three companies for the same plan letter. Ask about household discounts (many insurers give 5-12% off if two people in the same household both buy policies). And don't underestimate the value of a local independent agent who represents multiple carriers. They cost you nothing; they're paid by the insurer. A broker who only sells one company's products is telling you something important about their advice.
